Cauchy's theorem, the integral formula, singular points

Teacher update: residue theorem is out of the syllabus (Shobha ma'am); contour integration is in. Only the last section (residues) is optional.

The most reliable marks in complex calculus: a closed-contour integral of a fraction. It came in every one of the three MATH 241 papers (9 to 15 marks), and one of those years also asked to state and use Cauchy-Goursat. The questions are all solved by one decision: which singular points lie inside the curve?

Types. (A) State Cauchy-Goursat and use it to get 0 2022-23. (B) Cauchy integral formula, simple and derivative form every year. (C) Several singular points inside: partial fractions. (D) Locate and classify singular points 2023-24. Mandatory: 7 cards. The residue question of 2023-24 is in its own "came in papers, not taught" section at the end.

Type A · Cauchy-Goursat: when the integral is 0

Cauchy-Goursat theorem (her wording, write this)
1. If \(f(z)\) is analytic in a region \(R\) and on its boundary \(C\), then \(\displaystyle\oint_Cf(z)\,dz=0\).
2. If \(f\) is analytic in the region between two simple closed curves \(C\) and \(C_1\) (with \(C_1\) inside \(C\)), then \(\displaystyle\oint_Cf\,dz=\oint_{C_1}f\,dz\). (Deformation: you may shrink the curve.)
The basic one (Schaum 4.21)
\(\displaystyle\oint_C\frac{dz}{z-a}=\begin{cases}0&a\text{ outside }C\\2\pi i&a\text{ inside }C\end{cases}\). Inside: deform \(C\) to the small circle \(z=a+\varepsilon e^{i\theta}\); then \(\oint=\int_0^{2\pi}i\,d\theta=2\pi i\).

Recipe · show an integral is 0

  1. State the theorem.
  2. Find the points where \(f\) is not analytic: zeros of the denominator, here the zeros of \(\cosh z\) for \(\operatorname{sech}z\).
  3. Check each is outside \(C\): compare its distance from the centre with the radius.
  4. So \(f\) is analytic in and on \(C\), and the integral is \(0\). The direction of \(C\) does not matter.

What to write

The statement of Cauchy-Goursat, the singular points with their distances, "all outside \(|z|=1\), so \(f\) is analytic in and on \(C\)", and \(\boxed{0}\).

Traps

  • Saying "analytic everywhere" for \(\operatorname{sech}z=1/\cosh z\). It is not: \(\cosh z=0\) at \(z=(n+\tfrac12)\pi i\). It is only the nearest one, \(|{\pm\tfrac\pi2 i}|=\tfrac\pi2>1\), that makes the answer 0.
  • Points on the curve itself: the integral is then undefined, so check carefully (\(|z|=2\) with a pole at \(z=2\) was a printing slip in one question bank).

Type B · Cauchy integral formula

Cauchy integral formula (CIF)
If \(f\) is analytic inside and on a simple closed curve \(C\) and \(a\) is inside \(C\), then \(\displaystyle\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz=2\pi i\,f(a)\).
Derivative form
\(\displaystyle\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz=\frac{2\pi i}{n!}\,f^{(n)}(a)\).

Recipe · CIF evaluation

  1. Find the zeros of the denominator.
  2. For each, decide inside or outside: distance from the centre against the radius.
  3. With one point \(a\) inside: write the integrand as \(\dfrac{g(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\), where \(g\) is everything else (analytic inside \(C\)). Answer \(\dfrac{2\pi i}{n!}g^{(n)}(a)\).
  4. With several points inside: use partial fractions (Type C), or put a small circle round each and add.
  5. Simplify. If there is a factor \(\dfrac1{2\pi i}\) in front, cancel it.

What to write

The CIF statement, the zeros of the denominator and which are inside, the choice of \(g\) and \(a\), the substitution, the final simplified value.

Traps

  • \(9-z^2=-(z-3)(z+3)\): do not lose the sign, or just evaluate \(9-z^2\) at the point as in the example below.
  • A point on the circle, or the wrong inside/outside decision.
  • Forgetting \(n!\) in the derivative form.
  • \((z^2+1)^2\) has double poles at \(\pm i\): you need the derivative form, one term for each pole.

Type C · Two singular points inside: partial fractions

When the denominator has two simple factors and both points are inside \(C\), split the fraction, then apply CIF to each piece. A piece whose point is outside \(C\) gives 0 by Cauchy-Goursat. This is her class method.

Recipe · partial fractions

  1. Factor the denominator and split: \(\dfrac1{(z-1)(z-2)}=\dfrac1{z-2}-\dfrac1{z-1}\). Check by cross-multiplying.
  2. Take the numerator \(g(z)\) with each piece: \(\oint\dfrac{g(z)}{z-2}dz-\oint\dfrac{g(z)}{z-1}dz\).
  3. CIF on each piece whose point is inside: \(2\pi i\,g(2)-2\pi i\,g(1)\).
  4. Drop pieces with the point outside.

Trap

If only one of the two points is inside, you do not need partial fractions at all: let \(g\) be the whole rest of the integrand and use CIF directly. For \(\oint_{|z-1|=1}\frac{3z^2+z}{z^2-1}\) only \(z=1\) is inside, so \(g=\frac{3z^2+z}{z+1}\) and the answer is \(2\pi i\cdot\frac{4}{2}=4\pi i\).

Type D · Singular points

Singular point
A point where \(f\) is not analytic.
Isolated
A circle \(|z-z_0|=\delta\) can be drawn that encloses no other singular point.
Pole of order \(n\)
\(\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)^nf(z)\) exists, finite and \(\ne0\). \(n=1\): simple pole. Her definition omits "finite"; say it.
Removable
The limit of \(f\) at \(z_0\) exists, for example \(\dfrac{\sin z}z\) at \(0\).
At \(\infty\)
Look at \(f(1/w)\) at \(w=0\). Example: \(z^3\) has a pole of order 3 at \(\infty\).

Recipe · locate and classify

  1. Find the zeros of the denominator.
  2. Check the numerator is not 0 there (otherwise the factor cancels: removable or lower order).
  3. The order of the pole is the multiplicity of the factor. Prove it by computing \(\lim(z-z_0)^nf\ne0\).
  4. Write "analytic everywhere except at \(\ldots\)". Add the behaviour at \(\infty\) if asked.

What to write

The denominator's zeros with their multiplicity, the limit \(\lim(z-z_0)^nf(z)\) as a nonzero number for each, and the final sentence naming the region of analyticity.

Came in papers, not taught: the residue question

Teacher update · Shobha ma'am

"Residue theorem isn't included in the syllabus. Contour integration included." So skip this section: the card below is optional. Everything else on this page (Cauchy-Goursat, the Cauchy integral formula and its derivative form, which are contour integration) stays mandatory.

She never taught residues, but 2023-24 Q5(c) (15 marks) asked part (i) with a \(\coth z\) integrand and wrote "by residue theorem". Part (ii) of the same question is the \(\sin\pi z^2\) integral above, which her CIF method solves. Below is the minimum you need for part (i).

Residue theorem
\(\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}f\), summed over the singular points inside \(C\).
Residue at a simple pole \(a\)
\(\operatorname{Res}_{z=a}f=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)\). If \(f=\dfrac{p}{q}\) with \(q(a)=0\), \(q'(a)\ne0\): \(\operatorname{Res}=\dfrac{p(a)}{q'(a)}\).

Recipe · \(\oint\) of a function with simple poles

  1. List every singular point inside \(C\), including the zeros of \(\sinh z\) (\(z=n\pi i\)) hidden inside \(\coth z=\cosh z/\sinh z\).
  2. At each, \(\operatorname{Res}=\dfrac{p(a)}{q'(a)}\) or \(\lim(z-a)f\).
  3. Answer \(2\pi i\times\) (sum of residues).

Traps

  • Missing the pole of \(\coth z\) at \(z=0\) (it hides in \(\sinh z=0\)); only counting the obvious pole at \(z=i\).
  • Including \(\pm\pi i\): \(|\pi i|=\pi>2\), outside \(|z|=2\).
  • Version of the question: the scan has \(\dfrac{\coth z}{z-i}\) (answer below). The Math241QBank copy prints \(\dfrac{\coth z}{(z-1)(z-i)}\) and its answer \(\pi[2-\coth1-\cot1+i(\coth1-\cot1)]\approx0.1410+2.1078i\) is correct for that misprint only. Use the scan's version.
  • Do not trust computer-algebra \(\operatorname{residue}\) for coth/cot: it gives \(2\pi\cot1\) here, which is wrong. This answer was checked by integrating numerically around the circle.

One-minute summary

  • Cauchy-Goursat: \(f\) analytic in and on \(C\) \(\Rightarrow\oint_Cf\,dz=0\). Check every singular point is outside. \(\oint\operatorname{sech}z\,dz=0\) on \(|z|=1\) since \(\cosh z=0\) first at \(|z|=\tfrac\pi2\).
  • \(\oint_C\dfrac{dz}{z-a}=2\pi i\) if \(a\) is inside, \(0\) if outside.
  • CIF: \(\oint\dfrac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=2\pi i\,f(a)\); \(\oint\dfrac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz=\dfrac{2\pi i}{n!}f^{(n)}(a)\).
  • Two points inside: partial fractions, \(\dfrac1{(z-1)(z-2)}=\dfrac1{z-2}-\dfrac1{z-1}\); then \(2\pi i\,[g(2)-g(1)]\).
  • Pole of order \(n\) at \(z_0\): \(\lim(z-z_0)^nf\) finite and \(\ne0\). Removable: \(\sin z/z\) at 0. At \(\infty\): study \(f(1/w)\).
  • Answers to know: \(\oint_{|z|=2}\frac{z\,dz}{(9-z^2)(z+i)}=\frac\pi5\); \(\oint_{|z|=3/2}\frac{4-3z}{z(z-1)(z-2)}=2\pi i\); \(\oint_{|z|=3}\frac{\sin\pi z^2+\cos\pi z^2}{(z-1)(z-2)}=4\pi i\); \(\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{e^{zt}}{(z^2+1)^2}=\frac12(\sin t-t\cos t)\).
  • Not taught, in papers: \(\operatorname{Res}=\lim(z-a)f=p(a)/q'(a)\); \(\oint_{|z|=2}\frac{\coth z}{z-i}dz=2\pi(\cot1-1)\).
