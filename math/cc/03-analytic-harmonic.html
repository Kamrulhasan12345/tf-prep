Analytic and harmonic functions, harmonic conjugate, \(f(z)\)

"Show \(u\) is harmonic, find \(v\), write \(f\) in terms of \(z\)" came in all three MATH 241 papers (15, 13 and 17 marks): the most reliable question of the whole complex-calculus section. Schaum 3.7 (\(u=e^{-x}(x\sin y-y\cos y)\)) is marked "Exam" in her notes and appeared word for word in 2023-24.

Types: (1) prove harmonic, find the conjugate and \(f(z)\) every year · (2) \(f(z)\) when \(v\) (not \(u\)) is given 2024-25 · (3) proofs: analytic \(\Rightarrow\) harmonic, polar Laplace, \(\nabla^2|f|^2=4|f'|^2\). Mandatory: 4 cards (3 paper questions, 1 class proof), the rest are optional extras.

1. Analytic, harmonic, conjugate

Analytic (regular, holomorphic) in \(R\)
\(f'(z)\) exists at every point of \(R\). If \(R=\mathbb C\): entire.
Harmonic
\(\psi\) has continuous second partials and \(\psi_{xx}+\psi_{yy}=0\) (\(\nabla^2\psi=0\), Laplace's equation).
Harmonic conjugate
\(u,v\) are conjugate harmonic functions if \(u+iv\) is analytic, i.e. they satisfy \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\). The conjugate \(v\) of a given \(u\) is unique up to an added constant.
C-R as used in class
If \(f=u+iv\) is analytic in \(R\), then \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\) (necessary). If the partials are also continuous, the equations are sufficient.
Velocity potential and stream function
For \(f=\phi+i\psi\) analytic, \(\phi\) is the velocity potential and \(\psi\) the stream function: same equations with \(\phi\) in the role of \(u\), \(\psi\) of \(v\).

What to write

Statement "Analytic \(\Rightarrow\) C-R \(\Rightarrow\) \(u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\)" is not needed to show a given function is harmonic: compute \(u_{xx}+u_{yy}\) and show it is 0. Name the domain whenever the function has \(\log\) or \(1/(x^2+y^2)\): "harmonic for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\)".

2. Harmonic conjugate and \(f(z)\)

Recipe · \(u\) given: find \(v\) and \(f(z)\) (her method)

  1. Harmonic check: \(u_x,u_y,u_{xx},u_{yy}\); show \(u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\) (state the domain).
  2. C-R: \(v_y=u_x\) and \(v_x=-u_y\).
  3. Integrate \(v_y=u_x\) with respect to \(y\) (\(x\) is a constant), and add \(F(x)\): \(v=\int u_x\,dy+F(x)\).
  4. Differentiate this \(v\) with respect to \(x\) and equate it to \(-u_y\). This gives \(F'(x)\); integrate: \(F=\)const (usually 0).
  5. \(f\) in terms of \(z\): put \(y=0\): \(f(x)=u(x,0)+iv(x,0)\); then replace \(x\) by \(z\). (Add the constant \(ic\) or \(c\).)

Recipe · \(v\) given: find \(u\) and \(f(z)\)

  1. Show \(v_{xx}+v_{yy}=0\).
  2. \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\). Integrate \(u_x=v_y\) with respect to \(x\) and add \(G(y)\).
  3. Differentiate with respect to \(y\), equate to \(-v_x\), get \(G'(y)\).
  4. Put \(y=0\), replace \(x\) by \(z\).
Milne-Thomson (the name for this). In class she only wrote the "put \(y=0\)" step. The shortcut that goes with it: \(f'(z)=u_x(z,0)-i\,u_y(z,0)\) when \(u\) is given, and \(f'(z)=v_y(z,0)+i\,v_x(z,0)\) when \(v\) is given; integrate to get \(f(z)\), then fix the constant. It skips finding the second function entirely, but you still need the harmonic check. Use whichever you are surer of; the answer is the same.

What to write

(i) \(u_x,u_y,u_{xx},u_{yy}\) and the sum \(=0\) with the domain; (ii) "By C-R \(v_y=u_x\), \(v_x=-u_y\)"; (iii) the integration with \(F(x)\); (iv) the comparison giving \(F'\); (v) \(v=\dots+c\); (vi) \(f=u+iv\), then "put \(y=0\), \(x\to z\)" and the final \(f(z)\). A one-line check at the end (differentiate \(v\) again) is cheap.

Traps

  • Sign of \(F'\): \(v_x\) must equal \(-u_y\), not \(+u_y\).
  • Forgetting the constant of integration (state \(+c\) for \(v\), and \(+ic\) or \(+c\) at the end).
  • Put-\(y=0\) with \(1/(x^2+y^2)\) terms: it still works (\(\tfrac yx\ldots\to\) terms in \(\tfrac1x\to\tfrac1z\)), but check by recombining, since \(u(x,0)\) may be singular at \(x=0\).
  • For \(\tfrac12\log(x^2+y^2)\): \(f=\log z\) is analytic on a cut plane (cut along \((-\infty,0]\)), not on \(\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}\).
  • Integration by parts: \(\int y\cos y\,dy=y\sin y+\cos y\) (watch the signs in 3.7).

3. Polar form and proofs

Recipe · \(f=u+iv\) in polar form, one of \(u,v\) given

  1. Use the polar C-R equations (proved on the previous page): \(u_r=\dfrac1rv_\theta\), \(v_r=-\dfrac1ru_\theta\).
  2. If \(v\) is given: \(u_r=\dfrac{v_\theta}r\), integrate in \(r\), add \(\phi(\theta)\). Then use \(u_\theta=-rv_r\) to find \(\phi'(\theta)\).
  3. If \(u\) is given: \(v_\theta=ru_r\), integrate in \(\theta\), add \(\psi(r)\); check with \(v_r=-\dfrac1ru_\theta\).

What to write for the proofs

"Let \(f=u+iv\) be analytic. Then \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\). Differentiate (second partials exist and \(v_{xy}=v_{yx}\)) ... add ... \(\nabla^2u=0\)." State \(v_{xy}=v_{yx}\) as the reason the mixed terms cancel.

Optional extras

One-minute summary

  • Harmonic: \(\psi_{xx}+\psi_{yy}=0\) (give the domain). Analytic: \(f'\) exists in a neighbourhood. Conjugate \(v\): \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\).
  • \(u\) given: \(v=\int u_x\,dy+F(x)\); \(v_x=-u_y\) fixes \(F\). \(v\) given: \(u=\int v_y\,dx+G(y)\); \(u_y=-v_x\) fixes \(G\).
  • \(f(z)\): put \(y=0\), then \(x\to z\). Milne-Thomson: \(f'(z)=u_x(z,0)-iu_y(z,0)=v_y(z,0)+iv_x(z,0)\).
  • \(u=e^{-x}(x\sin y-y\cos y)\): \(v=e^{-x}(x\cos y+y\sin y)\), \(f=ize^{-z}\).
  • \(u=\tfrac12\log(x^2+y^2)\): \(v=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\), \(f=\log z\) (cut plane).
  • \(v=\dfrac x{x^2+y^2}+\cosh x\cos y\): \(u=\dfrac y{x^2+y^2}-\sinh x\sin y\), \(f=i\left(\cosh z+\dfrac1z\right)+c\).
  • Analytic \(\Rightarrow\) \(u,v\) harmonic (differentiate C-R, use \(v_{xy}=v_{yx}\)). Polar Laplace: \(u_{rr}+\dfrac1ru_r+\dfrac1{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}=0\). \(\nabla^2|f|^2=4|f'|^2\).
