Limits, continuity, differentiability and the Cauchy-Riemann equations

The "is it differentiable here?" question came in all three MATH 241 papers (\(|z|^2\), \(\bar z^2/z\), \(x^3+i(1-y)^3\), \(e^{\bar z}\)), and deriving C-R (rectangular and polar) came in the last two. She said "Proof imp for exam" about the polar derivation, and "problems imp, theorems not".

Types: (1) limits, ε-δ and the two-path test · (2) continuity · (3) differentiable at a point every year · (4) derive C-R, then polar 2023-24, 2024-25 must proof. Mandatory: 5 cards (all from papers), the rest are optional extras.

1. Limits

Limit (ε-δ)
\(\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=l\) if for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there is a \(\delta\gt0\) such that \(|f(z)-l|\lt\varepsilon\) whenever \(0\lt|z-z_0|\lt\delta\). The value must be the same along every path into \(z_0\).

Recipe · three jobs with limits

  1. Evaluate: substitute. If \(\tfrac00\), factor (e.g. \(z^6+1=(z^2+1)(z^4-z^2+1)\)) or use L'Hôpital.
  2. Prove with ε-δ: assume \(\delta\le1\), bound \(|f-l|\lt K\delta\) using \(|z-z_0|\lt\delta\) and the triangle inequality, then choose \(\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/K)\).
  3. Show it does not exist: let \(z\to z_0\) along the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) and along the \(y\)-axis (\(x=0\)). Different values: no limit. If the axes agree, try \(y=x\).

What to write

ε-δ: "Let \(\varepsilon\gt0\). Choose \(\delta=\dots\). If \(0\lt|z-z_0|\lt\delta\) then \(|f(z)-l|\lt\dots\lt\varepsilon\)." Path test: the two limits along two named paths and the sentence "they differ, so the limit does not exist".

Traps

  • Writing the relation backwards: the proof is \(\delta=\varepsilon/2\), not "\(\varepsilon=\delta/2\)" (misprint in one set of notes).
  • Two equal paths do not prove the limit exists; they only fail to disprove it. For \((z/\bar z)^2\) both axes give 1, but along \(y=x\) it is \(-1\).

2. Continuity

Continuous at \(z_0\)
All three hold: (1) \(\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)\) exists; (2) \(f(z_0)\) is defined; (3) \(\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=f(z_0)\). If the limit exists but differs from \(f(z_0)\) (or \(f(z_0)\) is undefined) the discontinuity is removable.

Recipe · continuity of a piecewise function at \(z_0\)

  1. Compute \(f(z_0)\) from the second rule.
  2. Compute \(\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)\) from the first rule. To prove it is \(f(z_0)\) use a bound such as \(|f(z)|\le|z|\to0\); two or three paths only disprove.
  3. Compare. Equal: continuous. Different: removable discontinuity; redefine \(f(z_0)\) to repair it.

Trap

Checking a few paths and calling the function "continuous". You need a bound that works for every approach, e.g. \(|\bar z^2/z|=|z|\to0\).

3. Differentiable at a point: by the definition

Derivative
\(f'(z_0)=\lim_{\Delta z\to0}\dfrac{f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0)}{\Delta z}\), if it exists (same value along every path). Differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous, not conversely (\(|z|^2\)).
Analytic in a region
\(f'(z)\) exists at every point of an open neighbourhood. So a function differentiable at one point or along a curve is not analytic anywhere.

Recipe · definition test

  1. Form \(\dfrac{f(z+\Delta z)-f(z)}{\Delta z}\) and simplify (use \(\overline{z+\Delta z}=\bar z+\overline{\Delta z}\)).
  2. Let \(\Delta z\to0\) along \(\Delta y=0\) (real direction; \(\overline{\Delta z}/\Delta z=1\)) and along \(\Delta x=0\) (\(\overline{\Delta z}/\Delta z=-1\)).
  3. Equal values for every \(z\): differentiable there. Unequal: not differentiable. Solve "values equal" to find the points where it can be.

What to write

The difference quotient, its two limits along the two axes, the equation that makes them equal, and a final sentence naming the points of differentiability and saying "analytic nowhere" (no neighbourhood).

4. Differentiable at a point: by Cauchy-Riemann

Cauchy-Riemann equations
For \(f=u+iv\) analytic in \(R\), a necessary condition is \[u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.\] If \(u,v\) and their first partials are also continuous, the equations are sufficient (at each point where they hold). Then \(f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y\).

Recipe · "where is \(f\) differentiable? find \(f'\) there"

  1. Write \(f=u+iv\) (use \(z=x+iy\), \(\bar z=x-iy\)).
  2. Compute \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\); check they are continuous.
  3. Impose \(u_x=v_y\) and \(u_y=-v_x\): solve for the set of \((x,y)\).
  4. That set (a point, a line, nothing, or the whole plane) is where \(f\) is differentiable. Then \(f'=u_x+iv_x\) there.
  5. Analytic only if the set contains a whole disc around each of its points.

What to write

\(u=\dots,\ v=\dots\); the four partials; the two C-R equations written out; the solution of them; "\(f'\) exists only at \(\dots\) and \(f'(\dots)=\dots\)".

Traps

  • C-R at a point does not give differentiability if the partials are not continuous. For \(\bar z^2/z\) with \(f(0)=0\) the C-R equations hold at 0, yet \(f'(0)\) does not exist (card below). Use the definition at the origin.
  • \(\bar z=x-iy\): the sign of \(v\) is easily lost for \(e^{\bar z}\).
  • One equation with two unknowns gives a curve (e.g. \(y=2x\)), not a point: then differentiable on the curve only.

5. Deriving the C-R equations proof: must

Ma'am did the polar derivation in class (Lec 9) and marked it "Proof imp for exam". The 2024-25 paper asked for the rectangular form and hence the polar form; she did not derive the rectangular one in class, but it takes four lines. Learn both, in this order: rectangular first, then polar by the chain rule.

Recipe · what the proof contains

  1. Rectangular: \(f'(z)\) exists, so the limit is the same along \(\Delta y=0\) and along \(\Delta x=0\). Compute both and compare real and imaginary parts.
  2. Polar: write \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\); get \(r_x,r_y,\theta_x,\theta_y\) from \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\).
  3. Chain rule for \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\) in \(u_r,u_\theta,v_r,v_\theta\).
  4. Put into \(u_x=v_y\) and \(u_y=-v_x\); name \(A=u_r-\tfrac1rv_\theta\) and \(B=\tfrac1ru_\theta+v_r\). The two equations become \(A\cos\theta-B\sin\theta=0\) and \(A\sin\theta+B\cos\theta=0\).
  5. Combine to get \(A=0\) and \(B=0\).

What to write

Define \(f=u+iv\) analytic, so \(u_x=v_y\), \(u_y=-v_x\). Then the chain-rule table (\(r_x=\cos\theta\), \(\theta_x=-\sin\theta/r\), \(r_y=\sin\theta\), \(\theta_y=\cos\theta/r\)), the two substituted equations, the combination, and the box \(u_r=\tfrac1rv_\theta,\ v_r=-\tfrac1ru_\theta\).

Traps

  • \(\theta_x=-y/r^2=-\sin\theta/r\) and \(\theta_y=x/r^2=\cos\theta/r\): do not swap them.
  • Do not stop after "equating real and imaginary parts" in the polar proof: you must show the combination \(A=0\), \(B=0\). The cos/sin elimination is the step the marker looks for.

Optional extras

One-minute summary

  • Limit: same value along every path. Does not exist if \(y=0\) and \(x=0\) (or \(y=x\)) disagree: \(\bar z/z\to1\) vs \(-1\).
  • ε-δ: \(\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/K)\) after bounding \(|f-l|\lt K\delta\).
  • Continuous at \(z_0\): limit exists, \(f(z_0)\) defined, equal. \(\bar z^2/z\): \(|f|=|z|\to0\).
  • \(\dfrac{\Delta w}{\Delta z}\): along \(\Delta y=0\) gives \(\overline{\Delta z}/\Delta z=1\); along \(\Delta x=0\) gives \(-1\). \(|z|^2\): differentiable only at 0, \(f'(0)=0\), analytic nowhere.
  • C-R: \(u_x=v_y,\ u_y=-v_x\); \(f'=u_x+iv_x\). Sufficient at a point if partials are continuous. Analytic needs a neighbourhood.
  • Rectangular proof: \(\Delta y=0\Rightarrow f'=u_x+iv_x\); \(\Delta x=0\Rightarrow f'=v_y-iu_y\).
  • Polar C-R: \(u_r=\dfrac1rv_\theta,\ \ v_r=-\dfrac1ru_\theta\); \(f'=e^{-i\theta}(u_r+iv_r)=-\dfrac iz(u_\theta+iv_\theta)\).
  • Chain rule: \(u_x=u_r\cos\theta-\dfrac{u_\theta\sin\theta}r\), \(u_y=u_r\sin\theta+\dfrac{u_\theta\cos\theta}r\).
