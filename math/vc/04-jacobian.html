A. The three definitions

Jacobian (matrix and determinant)
For a map \((u,v)\mapsto(x,y)\) with \(x=x(u,v)\), \(y=y(u,v)\), the Jacobian matrix is the table of all first partial derivatives. Its determinant is the Jacobian: \[ \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\begin{vmatrix} x_u & x_v\\ y_u & y_v\end{vmatrix}=x_u y_v-x_v y_u . \] In general, for \(\vec f=(f_1,\dots,f_n)\) of \((x_1,\dots,x_n)\) the matrix is \(J=[\partial f_i/\partial x_j]\); when it is square, \(\det J=\dfrac{\partial(f_1,\dots,f_n)}{\partial(x_1,\dots,x_n)}\) is the Jacobian. It is used for changing coordinates.
Hessian
For a scalar function \(f(x_1,\dots,x_n)\), the matrix of all second partial derivatives \(H=[\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j]\). It is symmetric. It is used to classify maxima and minima. In two variables \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\): \(D\gt0,\ f_{xx}\gt0\) minimum; \(D\gt0,\ f_{xx}\lt0\) maximum; \(D\lt0\) saddle point.
Laplacian
\(\nabla^2 f=\nabla\cdot\nabla f=\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}\). It is the trace (sum of the diagonal) of the Hessian.

Three tiny examples to see them once.

  • Polar: \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\). \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r\).
  • Hessian of \(f=x^3+y^3-3xy\): \(f_{xx}=6x,\ f_{yy}=6y,\ f_{xy}=-3\), so \(H=\begin{pmatrix}6x&-3\\-3&6y\end{pmatrix}\). Critical points: \(3x^2=3y,\ 3y^2=3x\) gives \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). At \((0,0)\): \(D=-9\lt0\), saddle. At \((1,1)\): \(D=36-9=27\gt0\), \(f_{xx}=6\gt0\), minimum.
  • Laplacian of \(f=x^2y+z^3\): \(f_{xx}=2y,\ f_{yy}=0,\ f_{zz}=6z\), so \(\nabla^2 f=2y+6z\).

What to write

One line each: the definition in symbols, what it is used for (coordinate change; maxima and minima; \(\nabla\cdot\nabla f\), the trace of the Hessian), and one small example. For a 5-mark question that is the whole answer.

B. Change of variables in a double integral

B1. The idea, in plain words

To integrate over a region \(R\) with awkward curved sides, describe the region in new coordinates \(u,v\) in which it becomes a plain rectangle. Then the integral is easy. The only price: the little area piece \(dx\,dy\) must be converted, because a unit square in the \(uv\)-plane is not a unit square in the \(xy\)-plane. The conversion factor is the Jacobian.

Change of variables formula
\[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\iint_{R'} f\big(x(u,v),y(u,v)\big)\,\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\,du\,dv, \] where \(R'\) is the region \(R\) becomes in the \(uv\)-plane. Always the absolute value of the Jacobian.

B2. The shortcut: you almost never solve for x and y

Usually it is easy to write \(u,v\) in terms of \(x,y\) (read straight from the boundary curves) and hard to solve for \(x,y\). So compute the Jacobian the easy way round and flip it:

\[ \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\frac{1}{\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}}\qquad\text{so}\qquad dx\,dy=\frac{du\,dv}{\left|\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\right|}. \]

Why it is true: the two Jacobian matrices are inverses of each other (chain rule), and \(\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A\).

The result of \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)\) will be a function of \(x,y\). You must rewrite it in terms of \(u,v\) before integrating. (In the \(e^{xy}\) example it comes out as \(2y/x\), which is exactly \(2v\).)

B3. How to choose u and v from the boundary

Recipe · choose u, v and draw the new region

  1. List the four boundary curves of \(R\). They come in two pairs. Each pair is a "family": two curves that differ only by a constant.
  2. For each pair, write both curves as \((\text{expression in }x,y)=\text{constant}\). The expression is a new variable. Examples: \(x+y=1\) and \(x+y=3\) give \(u=x+y\), \(u\) from 1 to 3. \(y=\tfrac12x\) and \(y=x\) both read \(y/x=\text{const}\), so \(v=y/x\) from \(\tfrac12\) to 1.
  3. The region in the \(uv\)-plane is the rectangle \(u_1\le u\le u_2,\ v_1\le v\le v_2\). Draw it. Check the corner values match the corners of \(R\).
  4. Compute \(\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=u_xv_y-u_yv_x\). Rewrite in \(u,v\). Then \(dx\,dy=\dfrac{du\,dv}{|\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)|}\).
  5. Rewrite the integrand in \(u,v\). Often it is already \(u\), \(v\) or a function of them (that is why the question was set).
  6. Integrate. A rectangle with a separable integrand splits into two single integrals.

Traps

  • Using \(|\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)|\) times \(du\,dv\) instead of dividing by it. (Check on the first example: the factor must make the area of the \(uv\)-rectangle match the area of \(R\).)
  • Forgetting the absolute value, or leaving \(x,y\) in the answer instead of \(u,v\).
  • Mixing up which pair of lines goes with which variable. Each variable is constant along its two curves.
  • Wrong order of corners when reading the limits of \(R'\): evaluate \(u,v\) at each corner of \(R\) and compare.

What to write

(1) the substitution \(u=\dots,\ v=\dots\) with limits, (2) the Jacobian \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)\) and \(dx\,dy=\dots\,du\,dv\), (3) the integral in \(u,v\) with limits, (4) the evaluation, (5) a boxed answer. A small sketch of both regions earns the "region" mark.

One-minute summary

  • \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=x_uy_v-x_vy_u\); Hessian \(=[f_{x_ix_j}]\), \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\); Laplacian \(=f_{xx}+f_{yy}+f_{zz}\).
  • \(\iint_R f\,dx\,dy=\iint_{R'} f\,\left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv\), and \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=1\Big/\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\).
  • Pick \(u,v\) so each is constant on a pair of boundary curves; \(R'\) is then a rectangle.
  • \(x\pm y=c\): \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\), \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)=-2\). \(xy=c,\ y=mx\): \(u=xy,\ v=y/x\), \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)=2y/x=2v\).
  • Polar \(J=r\); \(x=au,y=bv\) \(J=ab\).
  • \(\iint e^{xy}dA=\dfrac{e(e-1)\ln2}{2}\approx1.619\); \(\iint\frac{x-y}{x+y}dA=\frac14\ln3\).
