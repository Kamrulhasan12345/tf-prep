Euler–Cauchy PDEs (variable coefficients)

An equation like \((x^2D_x^2-xyD_xD_y-2y^2D_y^2+xD_x-2yD_y)z=x^2y^3\) has \(x\) and \(y\) in front of the derivatives. The substitution \(x=e^u,\ y=e^v\) turns it into a constant-coefficient equation in \(u,v\), which you already know how to solve. It came in all three MATH 241 papers (2022-23 Q8(a), 2023-24 Q7(c), 2024-25 Q7(c)); two of them are slide exercises word for word.

Types. (1) homogeneous, RHS 0; (2) RHS a power \(x^\alpha y^\beta\); (3) RHS with \(\ln x,\ln y\) or \(\sin(\ln x^2)\); (4) a term like \(yD_y^2\) that needs multiplying by \(y\) first; (5) irreducible operator. every year weak spot Mandatory: 7 cards. Everything is solved slowly, line by line.

1. How to recognise it, and the idea

Look at the powers. Every term must have the form (power of x)(power of y)(derivative of the same order in x, of the same order in y): \(x^2D_x^2,\ xyD_xD_y,\ y^2D_y^2,\ xD_x,\ yD_y\). The power of the variable equals the order of the derivative. That match is what makes the substitution work.

Substitution
\(x=e^u,\ y=e^v\), that is \(u=\ln x,\ v=\ln y\). Write \(D_u=\partial/\partial u,\ D_v=\partial/\partial v\).
Why \(xD_x=D_u\)
\(z_x=z_u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}=\dfrac1x z_u\), so \(x z_x=z_u\).
Why \(x^2D_x^2=D_u(D_u-1)\)
\(z_{xx}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(\dfrac1x z_u\Big)=-\dfrac1{x^2}z_u+\dfrac1{x^2}z_{uu}\), so \(x^2z_{xx}=z_{uu}-z_u=D_u(D_u-1)z\).

\(yD_y\)\(D_v\)
\(x^2D_x^2\)\(D_u(D_u-1)=D_u^2-D_u\)

Right-hand sides: \(x^\alpha y^\beta=e^{\alpha u+\beta v}\), \(\ln x=u\), \(\ln y=v\), \(\log(y/x)=v-u\), \(\sin(\ln x^2)=\sin 2u\), \(x+2y=e^u+2e^v\).

2. Solving in \(u,v\) and coming back

The result is \(F(D_u,D_v)z=f\) with constant coefficients, so use the chapter-4 rules with \(u\) in the role of \(x\) and \(v\) in the role of \(y\). Complementary function from each factor:

\(D_u-mD_v\)\(\phi(v+mu)\)\(\phi(x^my)\) since \(v+mu=\ln(x^my)\)
\(D_u-mD_v-k\)\(e^{ku}\phi(v+mu)\)\(x^k\phi(x^my)\)
\(D_u\)\(\phi(v)\)\(\phi(y)\)
\(D_v\)\(\phi(u)\)\(\phi(x)\)

Why \(\phi(\ln(x^my))\) can be written \(\phi(x^my)\): \(\phi\) is an arbitrary function, so \(\phi(\ln w)\) is just another arbitrary function of \(w\). Call it \(\phi\) again. Two forms can look different but be the same answer, e.g. \(\phi(2u-v)\) is \(\phi(\ln(x^2/y))\), which is \(\psi(y/x^2)\).

Particular integral \(F(D_u,D_v)^{-1}f\): exponential \(e^{au+bv}\to e^{au+bv}/F(a,b)\); polynomial in \(u,v\): expand in series; \(e^{au+bv}V\): shift \(D_u\to D_u+a,\ D_v\to D_v+b\); \(\sin/\cos\): put \(D^2\to-a^2\). If \(F(a,b)=0\), the failure rule (below). Then replace \(e^{u}\) by \(x\), \(e^{v}\) by \(y\), \(u\) by \(\ln x\), \(v\) by \(\ln y\).

Recipe · Euler–Cauchy

  1. Check the pattern (power of \(x\) = order of \(D_x\)). If a term like \(yD_y^2\) or \(xD_x^2\) has the wrong power, multiply the whole equation by \(y\) (or \(x\)) first, RHS too.
  2. Write: put \(x=e^u,\ y=e^v\), so \(xD_x=D_u,\ x^2D_x^2=D_u(D_u-1),\ yD_y=D_v,\ y^2D_y^2=D_v(D_v-1),\ xyD_xD_y=D_uD_v\).
  3. Replace every term, expand, and collect like terms. Often the single-\(D\) terms cancel completely; do not skip the check.
  4. Factor the operator into \((aD_u+bD_v+c)\) factors. Check the factorisation by multiplying back.
  5. Translate the RHS into \(e^{\dots}\), \(u\), \(v\).
  6. CF from the table above. PI by the rules.
  7. Back-substitute \(e^u=x,\ e^v=y,\ u=\ln x,\ v=\ln y\). The final answer is in \(x,y\) only: \(z=\text{CF}+\text{PI}\).

What to write

"Put \(x=e^u,\ y=e^v\); then \(xD_x=D_u,\ yD_y=D_v,\ x^2D_x^2=D_u(D_u-1),\ y^2D_y^2=D_v(D_v-1),\ xyD_xD_y=D_uD_v\)." Then the transformed equation \(F(D_u,D_v)z=\dots\) in factored form, "CF", "PI" as separate headings, and the final line "\(z=\)CF\(+\)PI" in \(x,y\).

Traps

  • Forgetting the first-order terms. \(x^2D_x^2\) is \(D_u^2-D_u\), not \(D_u^2\). The \(-D_u\) it leaves is usually cancelled by the \(+xD_x\) term of the question. If you drop it you get a wrong operator that still looks neat.
  • \(yD_y^2\) (no \(y^2\)). Not Euler type as it stands. Multiply the equation by \(y\); then \(y^2D_y^2-yD_y\) is \(D_v(D_v-1)-D_v=D_v(D_v-2)\) (slide ex. 4 and 8).
  • Treating a reducible non-homogeneous operator as homogeneous. \(D_u(D_u+2D_v-2)\) has a constant \(-2\): use \(e^{2u}\phi(2u-v)\), not "auxiliary equation \(m^2=0\)" (a note-taker put a "?" there).
  • Repeated factor gives \(\ln x\), not \(x\). In \(u,v\) the repeated factor gives \(u\,\phi_2\), and \(u=\ln x\). The \(x\,\phi_2\) of chapter 4 becomes \(\ln x\,\phi_2\) here. (But a factor \((D_u-2D_v-1)\) next to \((D_u-2D_v)\) is not a repeat; it gives \(x\phi_2\), see 2024-25 7c.)
  • Translating the RHS: \(x^2y^3\) is \(e^{2u+3v}\), so \(a=2,\ b=3\). Do not put \(x=2,\ y=3\).
  • Failure case: if \(F(a,b)=0\) you cannot divide. \(\dfrac1{F(D)}e^{av}=\dfrac{v}{F'(a)}e^{av}\) when \(F(a)=0,\ F'(a)\ne0\).
  • Back-substitution: \(e^{2u}=x^2\), \(e^{3u-2v}=x^3/y^2\), \(\sin2u=\sin(\ln x^2)\). Leave no \(u,v\) in the answer.

One-minute summary

  • \(x=e^u,\ y=e^v:\quad xD_x=D_u,\ yD_y=D_v,\ x^2D_x^2=D_u(D_u-1),\ y^2D_y^2=D_v(D_v-1),\ xyD_xD_y=D_uD_v\).
  • Collect terms; the single-\(D\) terms usually cancel. Factor into \((aD_u+bD_v+c)\).
  • \((D_u-mD_v)\to\phi(x^my)\); \((D_u-mD_v-k)\to x^k\phi(x^my)\); \(D_u\to\phi(y)\); \(D_v\to\phi(x)\); \((D_v-k)\to y^k\phi(x)\); repeated factor adds \(\ln x\).
  • RHS \(x^\alpha y^\beta=e^{\alpha u+\beta v}\), PI \(=\dfrac{x^\alpha y^\beta}{F(\alpha,\beta)}\); \(\ln x=u,\ \ln y=v\); \(\sin(\ln x^2)=\sin2u\).
  • Failure \(F(a,b)=0\): \(\dfrac{v}{F'(a)}e^{av}\) (or shift and expand). Polynomial RHS: expand \(F^{-1}\) in a series.
  • \(yD_y^2\) alone: multiply by \(y\) first. Always finish in \(x,y\).
