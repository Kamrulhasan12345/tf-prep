Particular integrals and the full higher-order solutions

Q7 of every paper (2022-23, 2023-24, 2024-25) is three higher-order equations. Each needs the CF (previous page) and a particular integral, and the marks are in the PI: which rule, the operator algebra line by line, and what to do when the denominator is zero. Farid sir taught this straight from his slides, so every slide example and exercise is here.

Rules: 1 \(e^{ax+by}\) · 2 \(\phi(ax+by)\) · 3 \(\sin/\cos(ax+by)\) · 4 polynomial · 5 shift rule \(e^{ax+by}V\) · 6 \(V\sin/\cos\) · 7 \(e^{ax+by}\sin/\cos\) · 8 general method · 9 failure cases. every year weak spot Mandatory: 24 cards (all slide exercises, Practice Sheet 2, and every non-Euler–Cauchy Q7 part of the three MATH 241 papers).

0. What "PI" means and how to read \(\dfrac1{F(D_x,D_y)}\)

We solve \(F(D_x,D_y)\,z=f(x,y)\) by \(z=z_c+z_p\) where the particular integral is written \[z_p=\frac1{F(D_x,D_y)}\,f(x,y),\] meaning "the function \(g\) with \(F(D_x,D_y)g=f\)". Rules for reading and moving operators:

Inverse of a derivative
\(\dfrac1{D_x}f=\displaystyle\int f\,dx\) with \(y\) held constant; \(\dfrac1{D_y}f=\displaystyle\int f\,dy\) with \(x\) held constant. No constants of integration are needed (any one \(g\) will do).
Factors commute and can be taken in any order
\(\dfrac1{(L_1)(L_2)}f=\dfrac1{L_1}\Big(\dfrac1{L_2}f\Big)=\dfrac1{L_2}\Big(\dfrac1{L_1}f\Big)\). This is what lets you handle a vanishing factor last (section 9).
Linear
\(\dfrac1F(f_1+f_2)=\dfrac1Ff_1+\dfrac1Ff_2\). Split the right side into pieces and use the right rule for each piece; the answers add.
Geometric series
\(\dfrac1{1-u}=1+u+u^2+u^3+\cdots\), \(\dfrac1{1+u}=1-u+u^2-\cdots\), \(\dfrac1{(1-u)^2}=1+2u+3u^2+\cdots\). On a polynomial the series stops, because each \(D\) lowers the degree.

The nine rules below are all consequences of three facts: \(D_xe^{ax+by}=ae^{ax+by}\), \(D_x[e^{ax}V]=e^{ax}(D_x+a)V\), and (for general \(f\)) the general method. Understand these three and nothing has to be memorised blind.

1. Rule 1: \(f=e^{ax+by}\) every year

Since \(D_xe^{ax+by}=ae^{ax+by}\) and \(D_ye^{ax+by}=be^{ax+by}\), every \(D_x^iD_y^j\) turns into \(a^ib^j\): \[F(D_x,D_y)\,e^{ax+by}=F(a,b)\,e^{ax+by}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{\frac1{F(D_x,D_y)}e^{ax+by}=\frac{e^{ax+by}}{F(a,b)}\ \ (F(a,b)\ne0)}.\] Substitution rule: replace \(D_x\to a\), \(D_y\to b\) everywhere in \(F\), including constants and each factor.

Recipe · exponential right side

  1. Read off \(a\) and \(b\) from the exponent \(ax+by\) (careful with signs: \(e^{2x-y}\) has \(a=2,\ b=-1\)).
  2. Compute \(F(a,b)\) factor by factor if \(F\) is factored.
  3. If \(F(a,b)\ne0\): \(z_p=e^{ax+by}/F(a,b)\). If \(F(a,b)=0\): failure case, section 9.

What to write

"\(z_p=\dfrac1{F(D_x,D_y)}e^{2x-y}=\dfrac{e^{2x-y}}{F(2,-1)}\)" then the arithmetic of \(F(2,-1)\) on its own line.

Traps

  • Check \(F(a,b)\ne0\) before dividing. Exponents like \(x+y,\ 2x+y,\ 3x-y\) are often CF arguments.
  • Sign slips on \(b\): \(e^{3x-2y}\) has \(b=-2\), so \(D_xD_y\to3\cdot(-2)=-6\).
  • For a factored \(F\), evaluate each factor, then multiply.

Worked examples

2. Rule 2: \(f=\phi(ax+by)\), \(F\) homogeneous of degree \(n\)

For \(w=ax+by\): \(D_x\phi(w)=a\phi'(w)\), \(D_y\phi(w)=b\phi'(w)\), so a homogeneous \(F\) of degree \(n\) gives \(F(D_x,D_y)\phi(w)=F(a,b)\,\phi^{(n)}(w)\). Reversing it: \[\boxed{\frac1{F(D_x,D_y)}\phi(ax+by)=\frac1{F(a,b)}\underbrace{\iint\!\!\cdots\!\!\int}_{n\text{ times}}\phi(w)\,dw^n,\quad w=ax+by,\ F(a,b)\ne0.}\] Use this for \(\sqrt{\ }\), \(\log\), \(\tan\), powers of \((ax+by)\). (If \(F\) is not homogeneous, use the general method, section 8.)

Recipe

  1. Name \(w=ax+by\); read \(a,b\).
  2. Compute \(F(a,b)\) (stop if 0: section 9).
  3. Integrate \(\phi(w)\) \(n\) times in \(w\), divide by \(F(a,b)\), put \(w\) back.

Traps

  • The number of integrations equals the order \(n\) (2 for a second-order operator).
  • A polynomial in \(x,y\) of the form \((x+y)^k\) can be done either this way or by the series method; the two answers differ by a CF term and are both correct.

Worked examples

3. Rule 3: \(f=\sin(ax+by)\) or \(\cos(ax+by)\) every year

Differentiate twice: \(D_x^2\sin(ax+by)=-a^2\sin(ax+by)\), \(D_xD_y\sin(ax+by)=-ab\sin(ax+by)\), \(D_y^2\sin(ax+by)=-b^2\sin(ax+by)\). The same holds for \(\cos\). So any even-degree combination can be replaced by a number: \[\boxed{D_x^2\to-a^2,\qquad D_xD_y\to-ab,\qquad D_y^2\to-b^2.}\] After the replacement:

  • if only a number remains, \(\frac1{\text{number}}\) just divides the trig function;
  • if odd powers remain (e.g. \(D_y-4\) or \(2D_x+9\)), multiply top and bottom by the conjugate to make an even power: \(\dfrac1{D_y-4}=\dfrac{D_y+4}{D_y^2-16}\), then replace \(D_y^2\to-b^2\) again, and finally apply the numerator \((D_y+4)\) as a derivative;
  • higher powers: split off \(D_x\): \(D_x^3=D_x\cdot D_x^2\to-a^2D_x\), \(D_x^2D_y\to-a^2D_y\).

Recipe · trig right side

  1. Read \(a,b\). Write out the replacement rules with the numbers filled in: \(D_x^2\to-a^2,\ D_xD_y\to-ab,\ D_y^2\to-b^2\).
  2. If \(F\) is factored, multiply it out first (or replace inside the product, but then you must expand).
  3. Replace every \(D_x^2,\ D_xD_y,\ D_y^2\) (also inside \(D_x^3\), etc.). Constants stay.
  4. If the result is a number \(N\ne0\): \(z_p=\frac1N f\). If it is \(N+\alpha D_x+\beta D_y\): conjugate, replace again, apply the derivatives.
  5. If the result is 0 (denominator vanishes): failure case, section 9.

What to write

The replacement rules with numbers ("\(D_x^2\to-4,\ D_xD_y\to-2,\ D_y^2\to-1\)"), the operator after replacement, the conjugate step if used, then the derivatives applied to \(\sin\) or \(\cos\).

Traps

  • \(D_xD_y\to-ab\) with the signs of \(a\) and \(b\): for \(\sin(2x-3y)\), \(ab=-6\) so \(D_xD_y\to+6\).
  • Only squares and the cross product are replaced; a lone \(D_x\) or \(D_y\) stays and is applied at the end.
  • After conjugating, replace again: \((D_y-4)(D_y+4)=D_y^2-16\), and \(D_y^2\to-b^2\).
  • \(D_x\sin(ax+by)=a\cos(ax+by)\), \(D_y\cos(ax+by)=-b\sin(ax+by)\): watch signs when applying the numerator.

Worked examples

4. Rule 4: polynomial right side \(x^my^n\) (series) every year

Idea: pull out of \(F\) one simple factor so that what is left looks like \(1-u\) or \(1+u\), where \(u\) is made of \(D\)'s, and expand \(\frac1{1-u}=1+u+u^2+\cdots\). Because every \(D\) reduces the degree of a polynomial, the series stops.

Recipe · polynomial right side

  1. Homogeneous \(F\): pull out \(a_0D_x^n\) (the \(D_x^n\) term). Then \(F=a_0D_x^n\big[1-G\big]\), where \(G\) consists of terms with \(D_y/D_x\) in them. Example: \(D_x^2-6D_xD_y+9D_y^2=D_x^2\big(1-\tfrac{3D_y}{D_x}\big)^2\).
  2. Non-homogeneous \(F\) with a constant term \(c\ne0\): pull out the constant: \(F=c\,[1+G]\), \(G\) made of all the other terms.
  3. Irreducible \(F\) with no constant (e.g. \(D_x^2-D_y\)): pull out the lowest-degree term (\(-D_y\)): \(F=-D_y\big[1-\tfrac{D_x^2}{D_y}\big]\).
  4. Expand \((1-u)^{-1}=1+u+u^2+\cdots\) (or \((1-u)^{-2}=1+2u+3u^2+\cdots\)). Apply to the polynomial term by term: \(\frac1{D_x}\) integrates in \(x\), \(\frac1{D_y}\) in \(y\). Stop when the derivatives give 0.
  5. Finally apply the pulled-out \(\frac1{D_x^n}\) (integrate \(n\) times) or \(\frac1c\).

What to write

The pulled-out form, the expansion with its coefficients, then each \(D_y^k V\) computed on its own line (the derivatives of \(V\) are where the marks are), then the integrations.

Traps

  • In \((1-u)^{-2}\) the coefficients are \(1,2,3,\dots\), not \(1,1,1\). With \(u=\frac{2D_y}{D_x}\): \(1+4\frac{D_y}{D_x}+12\frac{D_y^2}{D_x^2}+\cdots\).
  • Do not stop the series early: for \(x^2y^2\) you need terms up to \(D_y^2\) (since \(D_y^3(x^2y^2)=0\)); for a non-homogeneous \(F\) with \(xy\) you need \(G^2\) (seniors dropped it in 22-23 7b and lost marks).
  • \(\frac1{D_x}\) integrates with respect to \(x\) with \(y\) constant, so \(\frac1{D_x}y=xy\), and \(\frac1{D_x}x=\frac{x^2}2\).
  • Pull out the term that is the lowest power (constant, or \(D_x^n\) when homogeneous). If you pull out a higher one, the series does not terminate.

Worked examples

5. Rule 5: the shift rule, \(f=e^{ax+by}V\) every year

Because \(D_x\big[e^{ax}V\big]=e^{ax}(D_x+a)V\) (product rule), and the same for \(D_y\) with \(b\), moving \(e^{ax+by}\) out of any operator shifts the operator: \[\boxed{F(D_x,D_y)\big[e^{ax+by}V\big]=e^{ax+by}F(D_x+a,\,D_y+b)V\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac1{F(D_x,D_y)}\big[e^{ax+by}V\big]=e^{ax+by}\,\frac1{F(D_x+a,\,D_y+b)}V.}\] Then \(V\) is a polynomial (Rule 4) or a trig function (Rules 3, 6). Shift both \(D_x\) and \(D_y\); you may skip the \(D_y\) shift only when \(V\) has no \(y\) (the derivatives of \(V\) in \(y\) are then 0 anyway). The shifted operator has a zero constant term exactly when \(F(a,b)=0\); then you simply expand about its lowest term, and the \(x\) of a failure case appears automatically.

Recipe · shift rule

  1. Identify \(a,b\) and \(V\).
  2. Write \(F(D_x+a,\,D_y+b)\) and expand every bracket. Collect: constant + first-degree terms + second-degree terms.
  3. Pull out the constant (or lowest term): \(c\,[1+G]\).
  4. Expand \(\frac1{1+G}\) and apply to \(V\) as in Rule 4.
  5. Multiply by \(e^{ax+by}\) in front.

Traps

  • \(e^{ax+by}\) leaves the operator and stays outside as a factor; do not forget to multiply by it at the end.
  • Expand every constant correctly: \((D_x+2)^2=D_x^2+4D_x+4\); each bracket contributes constants.
  • Both notes dropped the \(D_y\) shift in one example and got a wrong answer (L7 #3).

Worked examples

6. Rule 6: \(V\sin(ax+by)\) or \(V\cos(ax+by)\), \(V\) a polynomial

Write \(\cos(ax+by)=\operatorname{Re}\,e^{i(ax+by)}\) and \(\sin(ax+by)=\operatorname{Im}\,e^{i(ax+by)}\), then use the shift rule with \(a\to ia,\ b\to ib\): \[\frac1{F}\,V\,\cos(ax+by)=\operatorname{Re}\Big[e^{i(ax+by)}\frac1{F(D_x+ia,\,D_y+ib)}V\Big],\] and Im for \(\sin\). Then expand in \(D\) like Rule 4 (with complex coefficients) and take the real or imaginary part at the end. Shift both \(D_x\) and \(D_y\) unless \(V\) contains no \(y\).

Recipe

  1. \(V\cos(ax+by)=\operatorname{Re}\big[V\,e^{i(ax+by)}\big]\). Write \(F(D_x+ia,D_y+ib)\) and expand: constant (complex) + first degree + second degree.
  2. Pull out the constant and expand the series on \(V\).
  3. Multiply by \(e^{i(ax+by)}=\cos+i\sin\) and keep the real part (for \(\cos\)) or imaginary part (for \(\sin\)).

Traps

  • \(i^2=-1\): the constant term gets contributions like \(-a^2\), \(-ab\), \(-b^2\) from the shifted squares.
  • Keep track of which of Re/Im you need. For \(\cos\): Re. For \(\sin\): Im.

Worked examples

7. Rule 7: \(e^{ax+by}\sin(cx+dy)\) or \(e^{ax+by}\cos(cx+dy)\)

Shift by \((a,b)\) first, so the exponential leaves, and then use Rule 3 on the trig function: \[\frac1{F}\,e^{ax+by}\sin(cx+dy)=e^{ax+by}\,\frac1{F(D_x+a,\,D_y+b)}\sin(cx+dy).\] In the shifted operator replace \(D_x^2\to-c^2,\ D_xD_y\to-cd,\ D_y^2\to-d^2\) as in Rule 3. (Alternative: use \(e^{(a+ic)x+(b+id)y}\) and take Im/Re.) Note that after the shift and the replacement the operator can reduce to a linear one such as \(7D_x-4D_y\): see the worked examples for how to invert it.

Worked examples

8. Rule 8: the general method (any right side) needed for \(\sqrt{\ }\), log, tan

For one linear factor, write the equation \((D_x-mD_y)g=f\), i.e. \(g_x-mg_y=f(x,y)\). Along the line \(y+mx=c\) (a characteristic) \(y=c-mx\), and along it \(\frac{dg}{dx}=f(x,c-mx)\). Integrating in \(x\) with \(c\) held fixed, and then putting \(c=y+mx\) back: \[\boxed{\frac1{D_x-mD_y}f(x,y)=\int f(x,\,c-mx)\,dx\ \Big|_{c=y+mx}}\qquad \boxed{\frac1{D_x-mD_y-k}f=e^{kx}\int e^{-kx}f(x,c-mx)\,dx\ \Big|_{c=y+mx}.}\] For several factors, apply the rule one factor at a time, innermost first, each time starting from the result of the previous one. Write the substitution \(y\to c-mx\) explicitly (examiners give marks for it).

Recipe · general method

  1. Factor \(F\) into \((D_x-m_iD_y)\) factors (from the auxiliary roots \(m_i\)). A factor \(\alpha D_x-\beta D_y\) becomes \(\alpha(D_x-\frac\beta\alpha D_y)\); take out the constant.
  2. For the first factor with root \(m\): in \(f\), replace \(y\) by \(c-mx\), integrate in \(x\), then replace \(c\) by \(y+mx\).
  3. Use the result as the new right side and repeat with the next factor.
  4. Collect the constants \(\frac1\alpha\) you took out.

Traps

  • The sign of \(m\): \((D_x+2D_y)\) is \((D_x-mD_y)\) with \(m=-2\), so \(y\to c+2x\) and \(c=y-2x\).
  • Integrate with respect to \(x\) only, treating \(c\) as constant, then put \(c=y+mx\) back. Do not substitute \(c\) before integrating.
  • Do all of one factor before starting the next.

Worked examples

9. Failure cases: the denominator is zero 22-23 7a, 7b; 24-25 7a

Rules 1 and 3 divide by \(F(a,b)\) (or by the number left after replacement). If that is 0, the right side \(e^{ax+by}\) (or \(\sin\), \(\cos\) of \(ax+by\)) is already a solution of the homogeneous equation, i.e. it is part of the CF, and a particular integral cannot look like it. The fix is an extra factor of \(x\).

9.1 The key lemma

If \(g\) is a function of \(y+mx\) only, then \(g(y+mx)\) is constant along \(y+mx=c\), and the general method gives \[\frac1{D_x-mD_y}\,g(y+mx)=\int g(c)\,dx=x\,g(y+mx),\qquad \frac1{(D_x-mD_y)^r}\,g(y+mx)=\frac{x^r}{r!}\,g(y+mx).\] A factor \((bD_x-aD_y)=b(D_x-\frac abD_y)\) belongs to \(m=\frac ab\) and \(ax+by=b(y+\frac abx)\), so \[\frac1{(bD_x-aD_y)^r}\,\phi(ax+by)=\frac{x^r}{b^r\,r!}\,\phi(ax+by).\] In particular \(e^{ax+by}\) is a function of \(y+mx\) exactly when \(a=mb\), i.e. exactly when the factor \((D_x-mD_y)\) gives 0 at \((a,b)\).

Recipe · exponential failure \(F(a,b)=0\) (factor method, the safe one)

  1. Factor \(F\) completely. Find which factor(s) vanish at \((D_x,D_y)=(a,b)\).
  2. Apply the non-vanishing factors first, by the substitution rule: they contribute \(\frac1{G(a,b)}\).
  3. Apply the vanishing factor last: \(\frac1{D_x-mD_y}e^{ax+by}=x\,e^{ax+by}\). If the same factor vanishes \(r\) times, use \(\frac{x^r}{r!}\).
  4. Put together: \(z_p=\dfrac{x^r}{r!}\cdot\dfrac{e^{ax+by}}{G(a,b)}\).

Shortcut (the one on Farid sir's slide #5): if \(F(a,b)=0\) but \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial D_x}(a,b)\ne0\), then \(\dfrac1Fe^{ax+by}=x\,\dfrac{e^{ax+by}}{\partial F/\partial D_x\,(a,b)}\). If that is also 0, \(\dfrac1Fe^{ax+by}=x^2\dfrac{e^{ax+by}}{\partial^2F/\partial D_x^2\,(a,b)}\), and so on. Why: \(x\,e^{ax}=\partial_a e^{ax}\), so differentiating \(F(D)e^{ax+by}=F(a,b)e^{ax+by}\) with respect to \(a\) gives \(F(D)\big[xe^{ax+by}\big]=\big(F_a+xF\big)e^{ax+by}\), which equals \(F_a\,e^{ax+by}\) when \(F(a,b)=0\). Differentiate again for \(x^2\): \(F(D)[x^2e]=(F_{aa}+2xF_a+x^2F)e\).

9.2 Trig failure

Write \(\sin(ax+by)=\operatorname{Im}e^{i(ax+by)}\) (or \(\cos=\operatorname{Re}\)) and apply the exponential rule with \(a\to ia,\ b\to ib\): the rule fails when \(F(ia,ib)=0\). Then \(z_p=\operatorname{Im}\Big[x\,\dfrac{e^{i(ax+by)}}{F_{D_x}(ia,ib)}\Big]\) (or \(x^2/F_{D_xD_x}\), etc.). In terms of the replacement rule of section 3: the denominator vanishes exactly when the number left after replacing \(D_x^2\to-a^2,\ D_xD_y\to-ab,\ D_y^2\to-b^2\) is 0.

9.3 Other right sides

  • \(\phi(ax+by)\) with \(F\) homogeneous and \(F(a,b)=0\): factor and use the boxed \(\frac{x^r}{b^r r!}\phi\) formula. Example 2010-11 6a(i) below.
  • Polynomials never fail (the lowest term is pulled out), and in the shift rule the expansion handles the failure by itself.
  • Anything else (\(\sqrt{x+y}\), \(\log\), mixed forms): use the general method of section 8; it never fails.

Traps

  • Apply the vanishing factor last. If you apply the non-vanishing factors to \(xe^{ax+by}\) with the substitution rule you get a wrong answer, because \(x\) is no longer an exponential.
  • Use the derivative shortcut with respect to \(D_x\); if \(\partial F/\partial D_x=0\) at \((a,b)\), differentiate again (get \(x^2\)) or use the factor method.
  • A failure does not mean "no solution". It means multiply by \(x\).
  • For trig failure with \(\sin\) take Im, with \(\cos\) take Re of the complex answer.

One-minute summary

  • \(\frac1Fe^{ax+by}=\frac{e^{ax+by}}{F(a,b)}\). If \(F(a,b)=0\): factor, non-vanishing factors first by substitution, the vanishing factor last gives \(x\) (\(\frac{x^r}{r!}\) if repeated); or \(\frac{x\,e^{ax+by}}{F_{D_x}(a,b)}\), then \(\frac{x^2e}{F_{D_xD_x}}\).
  • \(\sin/\cos(ax+by)\): \(D_x^2\to-a^2,\ D_xD_y\to-ab,\ D_y^2\to-b^2\); odd remainder: multiply by the conjugate and replace again.
  • \(\phi(ax+by)\), homogeneous \(F\): \(\frac1{F(a,b)}\iint\phi\); failure: \(\frac1{(bD_x-aD_y)^r}\phi(ax+by)=\frac{x^r}{b^rr!}\phi\).
  • Polynomial: pull out \(D_x^n\) (homog.), the constant (non-homog.), or the lowest term (irreducible); expand \((1-u)^{-1}\) or \((1-u)^{-2}=1+2u+3u^2+\cdots\); \(\frac1{D_x}=\int dx\).
  • Shift: \(\frac1F e^{ax+by}V=e^{ax+by}\frac1{F(D_x+a,D_y+b)}V\); for \(V\cos(ax+by)\) shift by \(ia,ib\) and take Re (Im for \(\sin\)). Shift both \(D_x\) and \(D_y\).
  • General method: \(\frac1{D_x-mD_y}f=\int f(x,c-mx)dx\), \(c=y+mx\); with \(-k\): \(e^{kx}\int e^{-kx}f\,dx\). One factor at a time.
  • Always: \(z=z_c+z_p\); split the right side into pieces; omit nothing from the CF (\(n\) functions for an order-\(n\) reducible operator).
