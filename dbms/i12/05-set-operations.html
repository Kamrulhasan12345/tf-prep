Union, Difference, Intersection, and Division

The set operators combine two relations with the same attributes. Union and difference are basic; intersection is derived from difference (slide 24). Division, which the slides do not cover but the exam questions use for every "for all" query, is built from the same pieces.

1. The second half of the RA slides (slides 21–22)

Slide 21 opens "Advanced Relational Algebra" with three topics: set operations, "fancier" RA (the theta and equi-joins of Chapter 6), and extensions and limitations (Chapter 9). Slide 22 repeats the operator list from slide 4, now highlighting union and difference, and adds the semi-join to the derived joins (also in Chapter 6).

2. Union and difference (slide 23)

Union-compatible
Two relations are union-compatible when they have the same set of attributes (same names, and the same domain for each). Under the named perspective the attributes must match by name; SQL only requires the same number of columns with compatible types, matched by position. ∪, ∩ and − are defined only for union-compatible inputs.
Union R ∪ S
The tuples that are in R, in S, or in both. Each tuple appears once. SQL: UNION.
Difference R − S
The tuples that are in R but not in S. SQL: MINUS in Oracle (EXCEPT in the SQL standard and in PostgreSQL).

Slide 23's examples: ActiveEmployees ∪ RetiredEmployees is everyone who is active or retired; AllEmployees − RetiredEmployees is everyone who is not retired. Both make sense only because the relations have the same schema. πname(Employee) ∪ Department would be rejected: different attributes. Project both sides onto the same attributes first, and rename (ρ) if the names differ.

Useful facts: ∪ and ∩ are commutative and associative, so R ∪ S = S ∪ R and (R ∪ S) ∪ T = R ∪ (S ∪ T). Difference is neither: R − S and S − R are different relations, and (R − S) − T ≠ R − (S − T) in general. Sizes: max(|R|, |S|) ≤ |R ∪ S| ≤ |R| + |S|, and 0 ≤ |R − S| ≤ |R|.

3. Intersection is derived (slide 24)

Slide 24: R1 ∩ R2 = ?

The tuples in both R1 and R2 are the tuples of R1 that are not in the part of R1 outside R2:

R1 ∩ R2 = R1 − (R1 − R2)

Why: R1 − R2 is the crescent of R1 that does not overlap R2 (slide 24's lower picture). Removing that crescent from R1 leaves exactly the overlap. Because ∩ can be written with −, it is not a basic operator. (It can also be written R1 ⋈ R2 when the two have the same attributes, Chapter 4.) Slide 24's example, UnionizedEmployees ∩ RetiredEmployees, is evaluated below exactly in this way.

4. Division ÷ (beyond the slides)

The question bank often asks queries with "every" or "all": suppliers who supply every part, employees who borrowed all books by Carl Hamacher. The operator made for these is division. The slides do not list it, but it is in the standard algebra (Silberschatz §2.6; the GMUW textbook expresses the same queries with − and ×).

Division R ÷ S
Let R have attributes A ∪ B and S have attributes B. R ÷ S is the set of A-values a such that, for every tuple b of S, the tuple (a, b) is in R. Result attributes: A.

Think of R(sid, pid) as "who supplies what" and S(pid) as "the list of parts". Then R ÷ S is "suppliers that appear with every part on the list". Division is derived; in basic operators:

R ÷ S = πA(R) − πA((πA(R) × S) − R)

  1. πA(R) × S: every candidate a paired with every required b, the pairs that should be in R.
  2. Minus R: the required pairs that are missing.
  3. πA of that: the candidates missing at least one required b.
  4. Remove those from all candidates: what remains has every b.

Two things to watch. First, project R onto exactly A ∪ B before dividing: if Catalog(sid, pid, cost) were divided directly, the cost column would become part of A and spoil the result. Second, if S is empty, every candidate qualifies (there is nothing it can miss), so πA(R) ÷ ∅ = πA(R).

In SQL there is no division keyword. The usual Oracle pattern is "there is no required part that this supplier does not supply":

SELECT s.sid FROM Suppliers s
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
  SELECT pid FROM Parts
  MINUS
  SELECT pid FROM Catalog c WHERE c.sid = s.sid);

Exam questions

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 20 marks

Consider the following schema: Suppliers(sid: integer, sname: string, address: string), Parts(pid: integer, pname: string, color: string), Catalog(sid: integer, pid: integer, cost: real). Write relational algebra expressions for the following:

(a) Find the sids of suppliers who supply some red or green part.
(b) Find the sids of suppliers who supply some red parts and are at the address '221 Packer Street'.
(c) Find the sids of suppliers who supply some red parts and some green parts.
(d) Find the sids of suppliers who supply every part.

Show worked answer

Catalog and Parts share pid; Catalog and Suppliers share sid; Suppliers and Parts share nothing, so natural joins are safe here.

(a) One part can be red or green, so a single selection with ∨ works:

πsid(σcolor='red' ∨ color='green'(Parts) ⋈ Catalog)

(Equivalently πsid(σcolor='red'(Parts) ⋈ Catalog) ∪ πsid(σcolor='green'(Parts) ⋈ Catalog).)

(b) Both conditions are about the same supplier, so join the three relations and select on both:

πsid(σaddress='221 Packer Street'(Suppliers) ⋈ Catalog ⋈ σcolor='red'(Parts))

(c) Here ∧ inside one selection is wrong: σcolor='red' ∧ color='green' asks for a single part that is both colours, which is always empty. The supplier needs one red part and one (other) green part, so compute the two sets of suppliers and intersect them:

πsid(σcolor='red'(Parts) ⋈ Catalog) ∩ πsid(σcolor='green'(Parts) ⋈ Catalog)

(d) "Every part" is division. Project Catalog onto (sid, pid) first so that cost does not take part in the division:

πsid,pid(Catalog) ÷ πpid(Parts)

Without ÷: πsid(Catalog) − πsid((πsid(Catalog) × πpid(Parts)) − πsid,pid(Catalog)).

Check on sample data (the evaluator below; parts 10 and 12 are red, 11 and 14 green): (a) {1, 2, 3, 5}; (b) {1} (supplier 4 is also at 221 Packer Street but supplies only a blue part); (c) {1, 5} (supplier 2 has only red, 3 only green); (d) {1}. The SQL box gives the same rows.

Two more "every" questions are worked in Chapter 9 (suppliers who supply every part, together with a counting query; employees who borrowed all books by an author).

Summary: ∪, −, ∩ need union-compatible inputs (same attributes). ∪ (UNION) and − (Oracle MINUS) are basic; ∩ (INTERSECT) is derived: R ∩ S = R − (R − S). "Some red and some green" needs ∩ of two sets, never ∧ in one selection. Division R(A,B) ÷ S(B) keeps the A-values paired with every tuple of S; it answers "for all / every" queries and equals πA(R) − πA((πA(R) × S) − R).
