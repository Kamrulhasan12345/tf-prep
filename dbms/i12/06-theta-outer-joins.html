Theta Joins, Equi-Joins, Outer Joins and Semi-Joins

The "fancier" joins: a theta join is a product followed by any condition, an equi-join is the special case with equalities. Outer joins keep the dangling tuples that every other join drops, and a semi-join only asks whether a partner exists. The chapter ends with the bank's two "compute the output" questions, worked step by step.

1. Theta join (slides 25–26)

Theta join R1 ⋈θ R2
The pairs of tuples (one from each relation) that satisfy condition θ: R1 ⋈θ R2 = σθ(R1 × R2). θ can be any condition, using =, <, ≤, >, ≥, <>, ∧, ∨. The result keeps all attributes of both relations, like ×.
SELECT *
FROM Students, People
WHERE θ;

Students ⋈θ People

Slide 26's note, "natural join is a theta join + a projection", follows from the definitions: R ⋈ S = πA∪B(R ⋈R.C=S.C S), where C are the shared attributes and the projection drops the second copy of each.

A non-equality example: pair each student with every person whose name comes alphabetically before the student's name. Theta joins with < or ≠ are common in "find pairs" and "find the maximum" queries (Chapter 8).

2. Equi-join (slide 27)

Equi-join R1 ⋈A=B R2
A theta join whose condition is a conjunction of equalities: σA=B(R1 × R2). Unlike the natural join, the attributes may have different names, and both copies are kept.
SELECT *
FROM Students S, People P
WHERE sname = pname;

S ⋈sname=pname P

The slide's other example, Employee ⋈SSN=SSN Dependents, should be read as Employee ⋈Employee.SSN = Dependents.SSN Dependents: pair each employee with his or her own dependents. The slide calls the equi-join "the most common join in practice": almost every join in real queries follows a foreign key, and a foreign-key join is an equality.

3. Outer joins (beyond the slides)

Every join so far drops dangling tuples, the tuples with no partner. Sometimes you want them: "list every supplier with the parts it supplies, including suppliers that supply nothing". The outer joins (textbook §5.2.7, and Lec 09 of this course for the SQL side) keep them and fill the missing side with NULL.

Left outer join R ⟕ S
R ⋈ S plus every tuple of R that has no partner in S, padded with NULL in S's attributes. SQL: R LEFT OUTER JOIN S ON ….
Right outer join R ⟖ S
R ⋈ S plus the dangling tuples of S, padded with NULL in R's attributes.
Full outer join R ⟗ S
R ⋈ S plus the dangling tuples of both sides.

(Some fonts draw ⟕ ⟖ ⟗ almost like ⋈: the left outer join has the extra short lines on the left, the right outer join on the right, the full outer join on both sides. The trees in this lecture also label them "left outer", "right outer", "full outer".) Each comes in a natural version (match on shared names) and a theta version (⟕θ). Size: |R ⟕ S| ≥ |R| always, because each R tuple appears at least once. (The bank question asking for the minimum and maximum size of A ⟕ B under bag semantics is answered in Sukarna sir's Lec 08 chapter.)

4. Semi-join

Semi-join R ⋉ S
The tuples of R that have at least one partner in S (on the shared attributes, or on a condition θ). Only R's attributes are kept: R ⋉ S = πattrs(R)(R ⋈ S). Each R tuple appears at most once, however many partners it has. SQL: WHERE EXISTS (…) or IN (…).

Slide 22 lists the semi-join among the derived joins. It is what IN subqueries compute (Lec 11), and distributed databases use it to ship fewer tuples between sites.

Summary: R ⋈θ S = σθ(R × S) keeps all attributes; with equalities it is an equi-join (the most common join). The natural join is an equi-join on the shared attributes plus a projection. Outer joins ⟕ ⟖ ⟗ keep dangling tuples padded with NULL. The semi-join R ⋉ S keeps the R tuples that have a partner, with R's attributes only.
