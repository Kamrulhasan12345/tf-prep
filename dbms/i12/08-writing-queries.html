Writing Queries in Relational Algebra

The slides' worked examples on the textbook's PC/Laptop/Printer database (one of which needs a correction), operator precedence, and the linear "assignment" notation. Then four exam questions in the same style, each answered and run on sample data.

1. A method for writing RA queries

  1. Find the relations that hold the attributes the question mentions (in the conditions and in the output).
  2. Connect them: natural join when the linking attributes have the same name and no other names clash; theta/equi-join otherwise.
  3. Select with the question's conditions, as low in the tree as possible.
  4. Project onto what is asked.
  5. Watch for the special patterns: "and also / both" across different tuples → ∩ of two queries; "but not / never / no" → −; "two or more / pairs / at least two different" → self-product with renaming; "the highest / largest" → remove everything that is smaller than something (− with a self-join), or γ (Chapter 9); "every / all" → ÷ (Chapter 5).

2. The PC/Laptop/Printer examples (slides 45–48)

The schema is the textbook's (Exercise 2.4.1): Product(maker, model, type), PC(model, speed, ram, hd, price), Laptop(model, speed, ram, hd, screen, price), Printer(model, color, type, price). The sample data below is the book's (Figures 2.20–2.21), with Printer.color written 'T'/'F' as on slide 46. The slides write each answer as Answer(…) := expression, the book's linear notation (section 4).

Slide 45: What PC models have a speed of at least 3.00? Which manufacturers make laptops with a hard disk of at least 100GB?

Answer(model) := πmodel(σspeed≥3.00(PC))

On the data: 1005, 1006, 1013.

Answer(maker) := πmaker(σhd≥100(Product ⋈ Laptop))

Product and Laptop share only model, so the natural join attaches each laptop's maker. Laptops with hd ≥ 100: 2001 (E), 2005 (A), 2007 (B), 2008 (F), 2010 (G), so the makers are A, B, E, F, G. Pushing the selection down, πmaker(Product ⋈ σhd≥100(Laptop)), is equivalent and cheaper.

Slide 46: Find the model numbers of all color laser printers. Find those manufacturers that sell laptops but not PCs.

πmodel(σcolor='T' ∧ type='laser'(Printer))

Both conditions are about the same printer, so ∧ in one selection is right: 3003, 3007.

πmaker(σtype='laptop'(Product)) − πmaker(σtype='pc'(Product))

"but not" is a difference of two sets of makers. Laptop makers {A, B, E, F, G} minus PC makers {A, B, C, D, E} = {F, G}. A single selection σtype='laptop' ∧ type≠'pc' would be wrong: it tests one product tuple at a time, and every laptop tuple has type ≠ 'pc', so it would return all laptop makers.

Slide 47: Find those hard-disk sizes that occur in two or more PCs. Find pairs of PC models with both the same speed and RAM, each pair listed once.

πpc1.hd(σpc1.model≠pc2.model ∧ pc1.hd=pc2.hd(ρpc1(PC) × ρpc2(PC)))

"Two or more" means two different PCs with the same hd, so compare two renamed copies and require different models. Result: 250, 80, 160 (250 occurs in six PCs; 300, 320 and 200 occur once).

πpc1.model, pc2.model(σpc1.model<pc2.model ∧ pc1.speed=pc2.speed ∧ pc1.ram=pc2.ram(ρpc1(PC) × ρpc2(PC)))

Using < instead of ≠ lists each pair once: (1004, 1012) passes, (1012, 1004) does not, and a PC is never paired with itself. Result: (1004, 1012), both 2.80 GHz with 1024 MB.

Slide 48: Find the manufacturer(s) of the PC or laptop with the highest available speed.

The idea: collect all computers with their speeds; remove every computer that is slower than some other computer; what is left has the maximum speed; look up its maker. The slide writes:

R1(model, speed) := πmodel,speed(PC) ∪ πmodel,speed(Laptop)
T(model, speed)  := R1 − σR1.speed < R2.speed(R1 × ρR2(R1))
Answer(maker)    := πmaker(T ⋈ Product)

Correction. The second line is not valid as written: R1 has two attributes, but R1 × ρR2(R1) has four, so the difference is between relations that are not union-compatible. The selected pairs must be projected back onto the slower computer's attributes first:

T(model, speed) := R1 − πR1.model, R1.speed(σR1.speed < R2.speed(R1 × ρR2(R1)))

(Load the "slide 48 as printed" example in the evaluator above to see the error.) On the data, the fastest computers are PCs 1005 and 1006 at 3.20 GHz, both made by B. If several makers tie at the top speed, all of them are returned.

3. Precedence of the operators (slide 49)

To save parentheses, RA operators bind with this precedence, highest first:

  1. σ, π, ρ (unary, highest)
  2. ×, ⋈ (and the other joins)
  3. ∩
  4. ∪, − (lowest)

So R ∪ S ⋈ T means R ∪ (S ⋈ T), and R − S ∩ T means R − (S ∩ T). Operators of equal precedence group from the left: R − S ∪ T = (R − S) ∪ T. The evaluator follows these rules (it puts ÷ with × and ⋈). When in doubt, write parentheses; an examiner never deducts for them.

4. Linear notation: assignment

A long expression is easier to write and read as a sequence of named steps (textbook §2.4.13). R(A1, …, An) := E evaluates E and calls the result R, with attributes A1, …, An (the attribute list is optional). Later steps can use R as if it were a stored relation. Slide 48 uses it. In the evaluator, write each assignment on its own line: T := … or T(a, b) := …; the last line is the answer.

DB QB · 19-20 7a · 20 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2019–20 · 15 marks

Given the following schema of a university: classroom(building, room_number, capacity), department(dept_name, building, budget), course(course_id, title, dept_name, credits), instructor(ID, name, dept_name, salary), section(course_id, sec_id, semester, year, building, room_number, time_slot_id), teaches(ID, course_id, sec_id, semester, year), student(ID, name, dept_name, tot_cred), takes(ID, course_id, sec_id, semester, year, grade), advisor(s_ID, i_ID), timeslot(time_slot_id, day, start_time, end_time), prereq(course_id, prereq_id). Write the following queries in relational algebra:

i. Find the ID and name of each instructor in the Physics department.
ii. Find the ID and name of each instructor in a department located in the building 'Watson'.
iii. Find all the courses taught in the Fall 2022 semester but not in Spring 2022 semester.
iv. Find the ID and name of those instructors who earn more than the instructors in the Physics department.
v. Find the ID and name of each student who has not taken any course section in the year 2022.

Show worked answer

i. πID, name(σdept_name='Physics'(instructor))

ii. The building is in department; instructor and department share only dept_name:

πID, name(instructor ⋈ σbuilding='Watson'(department))

iii. "but not" is a difference. section records which course was offered in which semester:

πcourse_id(σsemester='Fall' ∧ year=2022(section)) − πcourse_id(σsemester='Spring' ∧ year=2022(section))

iv. "More than the instructors in Physics" means more than every Physics instructor. Find the instructors who earn less than or equal to some Physics instructor, and remove them from all instructors:

P   := σdept_name='Physics'(instructor)
NotMore := πI.ID, I.name(σI.salary ≤ P.salary(ρI(instructor) × ρP(P)))
Answer  := πID, name(instructor) − NotMore

(If the examiner meant "more than at least one Physics instructor", the answer is simply πI.ID, I.name(σI.salary > P.salary(ρI(instructor) × ρP(P))). State your reading.) Physics instructors never qualify under the first reading, because each earns ≤ his own salary.

v. All students minus those who took a section in 2022. student and takes share only ID:

πID, name(student) − πID, name(student ⋈ σyear=2022(takes))

On the university sample data used in this lecture: (i) Einstein, Gold; (ii) Einstein, Gold, Crick (Physics and Biology are in Watson); (iii) CS-315, PHY-101; (iv) Brandt (98000 > 95000, the top Physics salary); (v) 19991 Brandt and 44553 Peltier. Run them below.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2015–16 · 15 marks

Consider the schema Route(FN, DC, AC, DI, SD, SA, PM), Flight(FN, DT, NP, AD, AA), Plane(PM, CA, TP) (FN = flight number, DC = departure city, AC = arrival city, DI = distance, SD = scheduled departure, SA = scheduled arrival, PM = plane model, DT = date, NP = number of passengers, AD = actual departure, AA = actual arrival, CA = capacity, TP = type), with the instance below. Translate the following queries into relational algebra:

(a) Which flights have at least 50 passengers on board and arrived more than 30 minutes late?
(b) What are the departure and arrival cities of all flights on October 10th that were completely full?
(c) What are the plane types that travel between BOSTON (BOS) and FRANCISCO (SFO)?
(d) Which flights departed late but arrived without any delay?

Show worked answer

Flight and Route share only FN; Route and Plane share only PM, so natural joins connect them. A "flight" in Flight is identified by (FN, DT), so the answers project both.

(a) πFN, DT(σNP≥50 ∧ AA−SA>30(Flight ⋈ Route))

Here AA − SA means the difference in minutes. The times are stored as hhmm numbers, and plain subtraction of hhmm values is wrong across an hour boundary (1005 − 0950 = 55, but the real gap is 15 minutes), so the evaluator converts: minutes(t) = ⌊t/100⌋·60 + (t mod 100). Result: (110, 2000-10-10), 35 minutes late with 403 passengers, and (124, 2000-10-09), 40 minutes late with 150.

(b) "Completely full" means the passengers equal the plane's capacity, which needs all three relations:

πDC, AC(σDT='2000-10-10' ∧ NP=CA(Route ⋈ Flight ⋈ Plane))

On this instance the result is empty: no flight on October 10 was full (the fullest, flight 124, carried 414 of 440). The expression is still the answer; it would return cities for any full flight.

(c) "Between" covers both directions:

πTP(σ(DC='BOS' ∧ AC='SFO') ∨ (DC='SFO' ∧ AC='BOS')(Route ⋈ Plane))

Only route 124 (SFO → BOS, an L1011) qualifies, so the result is jet.

(d) Late departure: AD > SD; no arrival delay: AA ≤ SA.

πFN, DT(σAD>SD ∧ AA≤SA(Flight ⋈ Route))

Result: (1205, 2000-10-09), (110, 2000-10-11), (398, 2000-10-10), (124, 2000-10-10). (Comparisons of hhmm times on the same day are correct without conversion.)

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2011–12 · 10 marks

Using the schema Emp(eno, ename, title, city), Proj(pno, pname, budget, city), Works(eno, pno, resp, dur), Pay(title, salary), express the following queries using relational algebra:

(a) Find names of employees whose salary is more than $1000.
(b) List all projects where at least one person with 'Programmer' title is working on it. (A project name may appear more than once.)

Show worked answer

(a) Salary depends on the title (Pay); Emp and Pay share only title:

πename(σsalary>1000(Emp ⋈ Pay))

(b) The trap: Emp and Proj both have city. Emp ⋈ Works ⋈ Proj would also require Emp.city = Proj.city and silently lose programmers who live elsewhere. Project the clashing attribute away first:

πpno, pname(πpno, pname(Proj) ⋈ Works ⋈ πeno(σtitle='Programmer'(Emp)))

The note "a project name may appear more than once" says pname is not a key: two different projects can share a name. That is why the answer keeps pno with pname; πpname alone would merge two such projects into one row. In the sample data below, Asha (Programmer, Dhaka) works on project P1 in Khulna: the correct expression lists P1, the naive three-way natural join (also loaded below) misses it.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2010–11 · 8 marks

Using the schema Product(pid, name, price, category, year, maker_cid), Purchase(buyer_ssn, seller_ssn, store, pid), Company(cid, name, stock_price, country), Person(ssn, name, phone_number, city), express the following queries using relational algebra:

(a) Find names of people who bought Indian products and live in Gulshan.
(b) Find people who bought stuff from Alam or bought products from a company whose stock price is more than BDT 2500.

Show worked answer

Person, Product and Company all have an attribute called name, and the links use different names (ssn/buyer_ssn, maker_cid/cid), so natural joins would be wrong here. Use equi-joins.

(a)

πPerson.name(σcity='Gulshan'(Person) ⋈ssn=buyer_ssn Purchase ⋈Purchase.pid=Product.pid Product ⋈maker_cid=cid σcountry='India'(Company))

(b) "or" between two different ways of qualifying: compute the two sets of buyers and take the union, then attach the person's details.

FromAlam  := πbuyer_ssn(Purchase ⋈seller_ssn=ssn σname='Alam'(Person))
RichMaker := πbuyer_ssn(Purchase ⋈Purchase.pid=Product.pid Product ⋈maker_cid=cid σstock_price>2500(Company))
Answer    := πssn, name(Person ⋈ssn=buyer_ssn (FromAlam ∪ RichMaker))

Here "bought stuff from Alam" is read as "the seller is the person named Alam". If Alam is a store, replace the first line by πbuyer_ssn(σstore='Alam'(Purchase)).

Summary: Find the relations, connect them with joins (natural only when the shared names are exactly the linking attributes), select, project. "but not" → −; "both … and … (different tuples)" → ∩; "two or more / pairs" → ρ and a self-product with ≠ or <; "highest" → all minus those smaller than something; "every" → ÷. Precedence: σ π ρ, then × ⋈, then ∩, then ∪ −. Slide 48's T needs πR1.model,R1.speed before the difference.
