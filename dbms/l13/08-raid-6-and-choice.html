RAID 6, and Choosing a RAID Level

RAID 6 adds a second check block per stripe so the array survives two failures. Then the slides' comparison of RAID 1, 5 and 6, the factors for choosing a level, and why latent block failures make RAID 6 more important every year.

1. RAID 6: P+Q redundancy (slide 20)

RAID level 6
The P+Q redundancy scheme: similar to RAID 5, but each stripe stores two error-correcting blocks, P and Q, instead of a single parity block, to guard against multiple disk failures. Better reliability than RAID 5 at a higher cost, and becoming more important as storage sizes increase.
P block
The ordinary XOR parity of the stripe, as in RAID 5.
Q block
A second check block computed with a different formula, an error-correcting Reed–Solomon code. Its arithmetic (over a finite field) is beyond this course; what matters is that P and Q are two independent equations about the stripe.

One equation can be solved for one unknown; two independent equations can be solved for two unknowns. So when any two blocks of a stripe are lost, RAID 6 can compute both from the rest. That is exactly the case where RAID 5 loses data.

Placement (slide 20)

With 6 disks, the two error-correcting blocks Pk and Qk for data blocks 4k, 4k+1, 4k+2, 4k+3 are stored on disk k mod 6 and disk (k + 1) mod 6. The data blocks fill the other four disks in order. Both check blocks rotate, as RAID 5's parity does.

Cost. Two disks' worth of space go to P and Q: N disks give N − 2 disks of usable space. For 8 TB of data on 2 TB disks, RAID 6 needs 4 + 2 = 6 disks. Small writes are dearer than in RAID 5: an update must fix both P and Q, so it reads old B, old P, old Q and writes all three, 3 reads + 3 writes = 6 transfers.

Try it: the simulator below fails D1 and D3. In stripe 0 that costs Q0 and B1 (B1 comes back from P alone, then Q0 is recomputed); in stripe 1 it costs P1 and B5 (only Q can help, so the Reed–Solomon equations are used); in stripe 2 it costs B9 and Q2 (B9 comes back from P2 = B8 ⊕ B9, then Q2 is recomputed).

3. Choice of RAID level (slides 21–22)

The slides name four factors:

  1. Monetary cost: how many disks must be bought for the space needed.
  2. Performance during normal operation: the number of I/O operations per second (for small random requests) and the bandwidth (bytes per second for large transfers).
  3. Performance during failure: how slow the array is while a disk is dead. RAID 1 reads the mirror at full speed; RAID 5 must compute every lost block from all the other disks.
  4. Performance during rebuild of the failed disk, including the time taken to rebuild it. A long rebuild slows everything else and is also a long window with reduced protection.

And they draw these conclusions:

  • RAID 0 is used only when data safety is not important, e.g. when the data can be recovered quickly from other sources.
  • Level 1 provides much better write performance than level 5. Level 5 requires at least 2 block reads and 2 block writes to write a single block, whereas level 1 only requires 2 block writes.
  • Level 1 has higher storage cost than level 5 (half the disks versus one disk's worth).
  • Level 5 is preferred for applications where writes are sequential and large (many blocks, so full stripes are written without reads) and which need large amounts of data storage.
  • RAID 1 is preferred for applications with many random or small updates, because it avoids the small-write penalty.
  • Level 6 gives better data protection than RAID 5, since it can tolerate two disk (or disk block) failures.

Latent block failures

Latent block failure
A block that has gone bad on a disk without anyone noticing, because nobody has read it recently. It is discovered only when someone tries to read it.

Picture a RAID 5 array where one disk dies. To rebuild it, the system must read every block of every surviving disk. If one survivor has a latent bad block, its stripe now has two missing pieces, and RAID 5 can fix only one: that data is lost. RAID 1 has the same problem if the bad block is on the surviving mirror. RAID 6 can still recover, because it handles two missing pieces per stripe. Disks keep getting bigger, so a rebuild reads more blocks and takes longer, and the chance of meeting a latent failure during it keeps rising. That is why the slide says RAID 6 is increasing in importance: a latent failure on one disk coupled with the failure of another disk can lose data with RAID 1 and RAID 5.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 5 marks

Discuss the applications where you will use RAID level 1 and RAID level 5.

Show worked answer

RAID 1 (mirroring) writes a block with just 2 writes and recovers by copying, but costs half the disks. Use it where updates are many, small and random, and must be safe:

  • the database log (the slide's example): a constant stream of small appends that must reach disk quickly at commit;
  • OLTP data such as banking or reservation systems, with many single-record updates;
  • system disks and other small, critical data where the extra disks cost little.

RAID 5 (distributed parity) costs only one disk's worth of space but needs 2 reads + 2 writes per small update. Use it where data is large and mostly read, or written in large sequential batches (full stripes need no reads):

  • data warehouses and decision-support databases, loaded in bulk and then queried;
  • archives, file and media servers, backups on disk;
  • any large read-mostly data where storage cost matters.

Summary: RAID 6 stores two check blocks per stripe (P = XOR parity, Q = Reed–Solomon code), on disks k mod n and (k+1) mod n, so it survives any two failures at the cost of n − 2 usable disks and 6 transfers per small update. Choose a level by monetary cost, normal performance (IOPS and bandwidth), performance during failure and during rebuild. RAID 0 only for data that can be recovered elsewhere; RAID 1 for many small random updates (2 writes per block); RAID 5 for large, sequentially written data; RAID 6 when latent block failures during long rebuilds make a second failure likely.
