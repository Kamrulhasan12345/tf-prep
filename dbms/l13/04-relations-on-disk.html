Placing Relations on Disk and Counting Seeks

Slide 11's Question w1-2 puts the ideas of Chapter 3 to work: why the DBMS reads blocks, where three relations land on the tracks, and how many seeks three queries cost. Also the exam question on head movement between relations.

1. Why placement matters

Chapter 3 showed that a seek costs milliseconds while reading the next track costs almost nothing extra. A full scan such as SELECT * FROM sale reads every block of the relation. If the relation sits on consecutive tracks, the head seeks once to its first track and then moves track by track while reading: that is a sequential scan. The expensive moments are the jumps from where the head is now to the start of the next relation. Counting those jumps is what slide 11 asks for.

Head movement
The sequence of tracks the head visits, and the total number of tracks it crosses.
Seek (in these questions)
A long arm movement to a track that is not the next one. Moving from a track to the adjacent track while scanning is not counted as a separate seek (slide 9: in a sequential pattern, a seek is required only for the first block).

The textbook makes the same point in §13.3.2: store data that is accessed together on one cylinder or on adjacent cylinders, and after the first seek the disk can run at its full transfer rate.

Question w1-2 (worked answer)

Disk storage size: tracks 1–20,000 hold 20.48 GB; tracks 20,001–40,000 hold 10.24 GB. The relations customer, product and sale (10.24 GB each) are stored consecutively on the disk platter starting from track number 1 and platter number 1. The queries Q1 SELECT * FROM SALE, Q2 SELECT * FROM CUSTOMER, Q3 SELECT * FROM PRODUCT are executed consecutively. (a) Explain why block is used in DBMS instead of segment to READ/WRITE to disk. (b) Find the starting track number of relations customer, product and sale. (c) Explain the disk head movement and the number of seeks to process the three queries. The initial head position was in track 1.

(a) Why blocks. A block is a fixed-size run of consecutive sectors (4–16 KB) that is always read or written as a unit. The DBMS uses it, and neither single sectors nor large variable-size segments, because:

  • Every disk request pays a seek and a rotational latency of several milliseconds, and moving 4 KB takes only a few hundredths of a millisecond. Reading a whole block per request spreads that fixed cost over many records. A single 512-byte sector would need eight times as many requests for the same data.
  • Blocks have one fixed size, so a block number is enough to compute a block's address, free space is managed simply, and one block fits exactly one frame of the main-memory buffer. Variable-size segments would fragment the disk and the buffer and make allocation and replacement complicated.
  • A large segment would often transfer bytes the query does not need and waste buffer space; the block size is the balance point named on slide 9 (smaller blocks mean more transfers, larger blocks waste space).

(b) Starting tracks. First find how much one track holds in each range:

TracksSizePer track
1 – 20,00020.48 GB20.48 GB ÷ 20,000 = 1.024 MB
20,001 – 40,00010.24 GB10.24 GB ÷ 20,000 = 0.512 MB

The relations are stored in the order listed, starting at track 1:

  • customer starts at track 1. It needs 10.24 GB ÷ 1.024 MB = 10,000 tracks: tracks 1–10,000.
  • product starts at track 10,001 and also needs 10,000 tracks: 10,001–20,000.
  • sale starts at track 20,001. The tracks there hold only 0.512 MB, so it needs 10.24 GB ÷ 0.512 MB = 20,000 tracks: 20,001–40,000.

(The given sizes are those of one surface of the disk in Question w1-1: 20,000 × 2000 × 512 bytes = 20.48 GB. If a relation were instead laid out cylinder by cylinder across all 10 surfaces, the starting cylinders would be 1, 1001 and 2001. The slide's wording, “starting from track number 1 and platter number 1”, describes the track-by-track layout used here.)

(c) Head movement and seeks (step through the animation):

  1. Q1, sale: the head is at track 1 and sale starts at 20,001: seek 1, crossing 20,000 tracks. Then sale is read sequentially from 20,001 to 40,000 (19,999 track-to-track steps, no further seeks).
  2. Q2, customer: the head is at track 40,000 and customer starts at track 1: seek 2, back across 39,999 tracks. Customer is read sequentially from 1 to 10,000.
  3. Q3, product: the head is at track 10,000 and product starts at the very next track, 10,001. Only a one-track step is needed, so no real seek. Product is read from 10,001 to 20,000.

Number of seeks = 2 (3 if the one-track step to product is also counted). The head crosses 20,000 + 19,999 + 39,999 + 9,999 + 1 + 9,999 = 99,997 tracks in total, of which 60,000 are seek movement (20,000 + 39,999 + 1) and the rest is reading.

Lesson: the order of the queries matters. Running them in storage order (customer, product, sale) would need no seek at all (the head starts on track 1) and only 39,999 track steps. This is the idea behind disk-arm scheduling (Chapter 9).

DB QB · 18 CT3 1 · 5 marks

The track positions of relations r1, r2, r3 and r4 are 40000, 1, 20000 and 10000 respectively. The queries executed sequentially are: SELECT * FROM r1, SELECT * FROM r2, SELECT * FROM r3, SELECT * FROM r4. The current position of the read/write head is at track no. 50000. Write the head movement to process the given queries and the total number of seeks. Assume every movement requires a seek.

Show worked answer

The queries run one after another, so the head visits the relations in query order (first come, first served):

QueryHead movesTracks crossedSeek
SELECT * FROM r150,000 → 40,00010,0001
SELECT * FROM r240,000 → 139,9992
SELECT * FROM r31 → 20,00019,9993
SELECT * FROM r420,000 → 10,00010,0004

Head movement: 50,000 → 40,000 → 1 → 20,000 → 10,000, crossing 79,998 tracks, with 4 seeks (one per movement, as the question says). The tracks read while scanning each relation are not given, so they are not counted.

For comparison, the order r1, r3, r4, r2 (an elevator sweep downward) would cross only 50,000 − 1 = 49,999 tracks, but the question fixes the order.

Summary: The DBMS reads fixed-size blocks so each expensive seek and latency is shared by many records and blocks map onto buffer frames. To find where relations lie, divide each relation's size by the capacity per track of the region it falls in. A full scan of a contiguously stored relation costs one seek plus track-to-track steps; the seeks come from jumping between relations, so the order of queries changes the number of seeks and the total head movement.
