Inside a Magnetic Hard Disk

The parts of a hard disk (platters, spindle, tracks, sectors, cylinders, heads and the arm assembly), how one sector is read, and how to compute a disk's capacity. Includes Question w1-1.

1. The parts of a disk (slides 5–6)

A magnetic hard disk (HDD) stores bits as tiny magnetised regions on spinning metal or glass discs. Figure 1 redraws the slide's schematic diagram with every part labelled.

Platter
One rigid disc, coated with magnetic material on both sides. Each side is a surface that stores data. A drive has 1 to 5 platters usually (slide 6).
Spindle
The shaft through the centre of all platters. A motor spins it at a constant speed, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), typically 5400 to 15,000 rpm. All platters turn together; the slide says the platter “spins continually”.
Track
One circular ring on a surface. The surface is logically divided into tracks, like the rings of a tree. A typical surface has 50,000 to 100,000 tracks (slide 5).
Sector
One piece of a track. A sector is the smallest unit of data that can be read or written: the disk never reads half a sector. A sector typically holds 512 bytes (newer disks use 4096).
Read-write head
A tiny electromagnet that floats a few nanometres above a surface. It reads bits by sensing the magnetisation passing under it, and writes by changing it. There is one head per surface.
Arm and arm assembly
Each head sits at the end of an arm. All the arms are mounted on one common arm assembly (also called the head-disk assembly or actuator), so all heads move in and out together. The slide's “one head per platter, mounted on a common arm” means exactly this.
Cylinder
Cylinder i consists of the i-th track of all the platters (slide 6). Because the heads move together, when one head is over track i, every head is over track i of its own surface. So all the tracks of one cylinder can be read without moving the arm.

Tracks and sectors from above

Looking straight down on one surface, tracks are concentric rings and each ring is cut into sectors. Outer tracks are longer than inner ones, so modern disks put more sectors on them. The slide's numbers: 500 to 1000 sectors per track on inner tracks, and 1000 to 2000 on outer tracks. Between sectors there are small gaps that the controller uses to find where each sector starts.

A disk block (Chapter 3) is the unit the database reads and writes. It is a run of consecutive sectors on one track, for example 8 sectors of 512 bytes make a 4 KB block.

2. Reading or writing a sector (slide 6)

The slide gives the procedure in three lines:

  1. The disk arm swings to position the head on the right track. This is the seek.
  2. The platter spins continually, so the controller waits until the wanted sector comes round. This wait is the rotational latency.
  3. The data is read or written as the sector passes under the head. This is the transfer.

3. Disk capacity (slide 7)

Capacity follows directly from the geometry. One track holds sectors per track × bytes per sector. One surface holds the sum over its tracks. One platter has two surfaces, and the disk has several platters:

capacity = platters × 2 surfaces × Σ (tracks × sectors per track) × bytes per sector

When the number of sectors per track varies, add up each range of tracks separately.

Question w1-1 (worked answer)

In a disk storage system, the outermost track number is 1 and the innermost track number is 40,000 for each platter. Tracks 1–20,000 have 2000 sectors per track; tracks 20,001–40,000 have 1000 sectors per track. The sector size is 512 bytes. There are 2 sides in each platter and 5 platters in the disk. (a) Find the storage size of one platter in GB. (b) Find the storage size of the disk in GB.

(a) One platter. First count the sectors on one side (one surface):

TracksNumber of tracksSectors per trackSectors
1 – 20,000 (outer)20,000200040,000,000
20,001 – 40,000 (inner)20,000100020,000,000
One side60,000,000

One side = 60,000,000 sectors × 512 bytes = 30,720,000,000 bytes = 30.72 GB. A platter has 2 sides:

one platter = 2 × 30.72 GB = 61.44 GB

(61,440,000,000 bytes. Here 1 GB = 109 bytes, the convention slide 11 also uses: 20,000 × 2000 × 512 bytes is written there as 20.48 GB. With 1 GB = 230 bytes the platter is 57.22 GB.)

(b) The whole disk. 5 platters × 61.44 GB = 307.2 GB (307,200,000,000 bytes, or 286.10 GB with 1 GB = 230).

Notice that the outer half of the tracks holds two thirds of the data (40 of the 60 million sectors on each side). That is why data placed on outer tracks can be read faster: more sectors pass under the head per rotation.

Practice

Key terms

Quick check

Summary: A disk has platters on a spinning spindle. Each surface is divided into circular tracks, each track into sectors (the smallest unit read or written, typically 512 bytes). One head per surface sits on an arm, and all arms move together on the arm assembly, so the i-th tracks of all surfaces form cylinder i. Reading a sector means seek (arm moves), rotational latency (wait for the sector) and transfer (read as it passes). Capacity = platters × 2 sides × total sectors per side × sector size.
