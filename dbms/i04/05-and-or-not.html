Combining Conditions: AND, OR, NOT and Precedence

How to join several conditions in one WHERE clause, the classic mistake of using AND to ask "makes both kinds of product" (slide 38), and why A OR B AND C means A OR (B AND C) (slides 39–40), shown row by row on the slide's table.

1. AND, OR and NOT

C1 AND C2
TRUE when both conditions are TRUE for the row.
C1 OR C2
TRUE when at least one of the conditions is TRUE for the row.
NOT C
TRUE when C is FALSE. (NOT BETWEEN, NOT LIKE and NOT IN are shorthand for NOT applied to those conditions.)

Every condition is evaluated on one row at a time. This sentence explains all the examples below. (When NULLs are involved, a third truth value UNKNOWN appears; Chapter 8 extends these rules.)

2. AND (slide 36)

Show all household products having prices more than $100.

SELECT *
FROM   Product
WHERE  category = 'Household'
AND    price >= 100;              -- MultiTouch

A small mismatch: the request says "more than $100" (>) but the query uses >=. On this data both give MultiTouch; a household product priced exactly 100 would separate them. When translating English to SQL, "more than" is > and "at least" is >=.

3. OR (slide 37)

Show all manufacturers that make photography or household items.

SELECT DISTINCT manufacturer
FROM   Product
WHERE  category = 'Photography'
OR     category = 'Household';    -- Canon, Hitachi

Each row is tested on its own: SingleTouch passes the first test, MultiTouch the second, the gadgets neither. DISTINCT then lists each manufacturer once (it matters when a manufacturer has several qualifying products).

4. The "both" mistake (slide 38)

Show all manufacturers that make both photography and household items. The slide marks this query WRONG:

SELECT DISTINCT maker
FROM   Product
WHERE  category = 'Photography'
AND    category = 'Household';    -- always empty

Why it is wrong (worked answer)

WHERE looks at one row at a time, and one row has one category. No single product can have category Photography and category Household at the same time, so the condition is FALSE for every row and the answer is always empty, whatever the data. "Makes both" is a statement about two different rows of the same manufacturer, which a single-row condition cannot express.

(The query also says maker, but this Product table's column is manufacturer: a second slip on the slide.)

A correct query finds the manufacturers of photography products, the manufacturers of household products, and keeps those in both lists. SQL's set operator INTERSECT does that (set operations and other methods, such as subqueries, come in Lectures 8–11):

SELECT manufacturer FROM Product WHERE category = 'Photography'
INTERSECT
SELECT manufacturer FROM Product WHERE category = 'Household';

On the slide data this is also empty, but for the right reason: Canon makes only photography items and Hitachi only household items. Add a Canon household product and it returns Canon, while the AND query stays empty.

5. Precedence: AND before OR (slides 39–40)

Operator precedence
The order in which operators are applied when there are no parentheses. In SQL, comparisons and LIKE are evaluated first, then NOT, then AND, and OR last. So AND binds tighter than OR, just as × binds tighter than + in arithmetic.
-- slide 39
SELECT *
FROM   Product
WHERE  pname LIKE '%Giz%'
OR     category LIKE '%hold'
AND    price > 100;
-- means  A OR (B AND C)
-- → Gizmo, MultiTouch
-- slide 40
SELECT *
FROM   Product
WHERE  (pname LIKE '%Giz%'
OR      category LIKE '%hold')
AND    price > 100;
-- means  (A OR B) AND C
-- → MultiTouch

Name the three conditions A = pname LIKE '%Giz%', B = category LIKE '%hold', C = price > 100. The animation evaluates them on every row and then combines them both ways.

Gizmo is the row that separates the two queries: it satisfies A alone. In slide 39, A on its own is enough (A OR …), so Gizmo is returned even though it costs only 19.99. In slide 40 the parentheses force the price test on every row, and Gizmo fails it. Notice also that Powergizmo fails A: its name contains giz with a lower-case g, and LIKE is case-sensitive.

Rule: whenever a WHERE clause mixes AND and OR, write the parentheses, even when the default precedence already gives what you mean. It costs nothing and removes all doubt for the reader.

Summary: AND needs both conditions, OR at least one, NOT reverses a condition; each is evaluated on one row at a time, so category = 'X' AND category = 'Y' can never be true. "Makes both X and Y" needs two rows, e.g. INTERSECT. Without parentheses, NOT binds before AND, and AND before OR: A OR B AND C = A OR (B AND C). Write parentheses whenever AND and OR are mixed.
