Testing Conflict-Serializability with a Precedence Graph

Instead of hunting for swaps, draw one arc for each conflicting pair and look for a cycle. This chapter gives the test, works the slides' two examples, and provides a tool that runs the test on any schedule you type, step by step. Most exam questions in this topic are this procedure.

1. Precedence between transactions (slide 22)

T1 takes precedence over T2, written T1 <S T2
In schedule S there are actions A1 of T1 and A2 of T2 such that (1) A1 is ahead of A2 in S, (2) both involve the same database element, and (3) at least one of them is a write.

Conditions (2) and (3) say exactly that A1 and A2 conflict, so they can never be swapped past each other.

Slide 22 question: why must a conflict-equivalent serial schedule of S have T1 before T2?

A1 and A2 conflict, so no legal swap can ever exchange their order: every schedule reachable from S by non-conflicting swaps still has A1 ahead of A2. In a serial schedule, all of T1's actions are on one side of all of T2's. If T2 came first, A2 would be ahead of A1, which is impossible. So any conflict-equivalent serial schedule puts T1 before T2.

2. The precedence graph and the test (slides 23–24)

Precedence graph of S
A directed graph with one node per transaction of S (node i stands for Ti) and an arc from node i to node j whenever Ti <S Tj. (Also called a serialization graph.)
Cycle
A path of arcs that starts and ends at the same node, e.g. 1 → 2 → 1.
Topological order
An ordering of the nodes of an acyclic graph such that for every arc a → b, a comes before b. Found by repeatedly removing a node with no incoming arcs from the remaining nodes.

Test for conflict-serializability.

  1. Construct the precedence graph of S: for every pair of actions of different transactions on the same element with at least one write, draw an arc from the earlier action's transaction to the later one's.
  2. If the graph has a cycle, S is not conflict-serializable. If it is acyclic, S is conflict-serializable, and every topological order of the graph is a conflict-equivalent serial order.

A practical way to do step 1 without missing anything: go element by element. For element X, list X's actions in schedule order, and for each write, draw arcs from every earlier action on X (of another transaction) to it, and from it to every later action on X (of another transaction). Two reads never produce an arc.

Slide 23: an acyclic example

S: r₂(A); r₁(B); w₂(A); r₃(A); w₁(B); w₃(A); r₂(B); w₂(B) (textbook Example 18.7). Step through the test:

On A, T₂'s actions all come before T₃'s, and w₂(A) is ahead of r₃(A) and w₃(A) (also r₂(A) ahead of w₃(A)): any one of these justifies the arc 2 → 3. On B, r₁(B) is ahead of w₂(B), and w₁(B) is ahead of r₂(B) and w₂(B): arc 1 → 2. Those are the only arcs. The graph 1 → 2 → 3 is acyclic, and its only topological order gives the serial schedule

S′: r₁(B); w₁(B); r₂(A); w₂(A); r₂(B); w₂(B); r₃(A); w₃(A)

A note on slide 23: it gives the reason for T₁ <S T₂ as “r₁(B); w₁(B)”. Both of those are T₁'s own actions, so they cannot justify an arc between two transactions. The intended pair is r₁(B) … w₂(B) (or w₁(B) … r₂(B)), as in the textbook.

Slide 25: a cyclic example

S₁: r₂(A); r₁(B); w₂(A); r₂(B); r₃(A); w₁(B); w₃(A); w₂(B) differs from S only in that r₂(B) moved forward three places (textbook Example 18.9).

On A nothing changed: 2 → 3. On B, r₁(B) is still ahead of w₂(B) (1 → 2), but now r₂(B) is ahead of w₁(B), giving 2 → 1. The cycle 1 → 2 → 1 says T₁ must be both before and after T₂ in any conflict-equivalent serial schedule. So S₁ is not conflict-serializable.

3. Run the test on any schedule

Type a schedule (forms such as r1(A) w2(B), r1(A); w2(B);, R1(A), W2(B) are all accepted). The tool lists the conflicting pairs element by element, draws the arcs as they are found, then either highlights a cycle or performs the topological sort and the swaps that reach the serial schedule. Every exam schedule in the questions below can be checked here.

DB QB · 19-20 4a · 15 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2019–20 · 15 marks

Explain conflict serializability with an example. Two transactions T2: READ (A), WRITE (A), COMMIT and T1: READ (A), READ (B) COMMIT are given. Write down the concurrent schedule and the corresponding precedence graph. Using the precedence graph, prove that your schedule is serializable. Find the equivalent serial schedule.

Show worked answer

Conflict serializability. Two actions conflict if they are from different transactions, touch the same element, and at least one is a write (or if they belong to the same transaction). A schedule is conflict-serializable if swapping adjacent non-conflicting actions can turn it into a serial schedule. Example: r₁(A); w₁(A); r₂(A); w₂(A); r₁(B); w₁(B); r₂(B); w₂(B) becomes (T₁, T₂) by four swaps (Chapter 3).

A concurrent schedule of T₁: r₁(A); r₁(B); c₁ and T₂: r₂(A); w₂(A); c₂:

S: r₁(A); r₂(A); r₁(B); w₂(A); c₁; c₂

Precedence graph. A: r₁(A) is before w₂(A): arc T₁ → T₂. r₁(A), r₂(A) are both reads: no arc. B: only T₁. So the graph is T₁ → T₂.

Proof. The graph has no cycle, so S is conflict-serializable, and conflict-serializable schedules are serializable. Concretely, swap r₂(A) with r₁(B) (different elements) to get r₁(A); r₁(B); r₂(A); w₂(A), which is serial. Equivalent serial schedule: (T₁, T₂), i.e. r₁(A); r₁(B); c₁; r₂(A); w₂(A); c₂. Intuitively T₁ read the old A, which is exactly what it sees when it runs entirely before T₂.

DB QB · Jan 20 1b · 15 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2018–19 · 15 marks

Two transactions are given as follows: (i) Transaction T1 transfers the sum of 20% of balances of accounts A and B to account C. (ii) Transaction T2 transfers Tk. 2000 from account D to account C. Prepare a concurrent schedule for the above two transactions and prove that the schedule is conflict serializable.

Show worked answer

The transactions. T₁ takes 20% of A and 20% of B and adds the sum to C:

T₁: READ(A,a); x := 0.2*a; a := a − x; WRITE(A,a);
    READ(B,b); y := 0.2*b; b := b − y; WRITE(B,b);
    READ(C,c); c := c + x + y; WRITE(C,c)          → r₁(A) w₁(A) r₁(B) w₁(B) r₁(C) w₁(C)
T₂: READ(D,d); d := d − 2000; WRITE(D,d);
    READ(C,c); c := c + 2000; WRITE(C,c)            → r₂(D) w₂(D) r₂(C) w₂(C)

A concurrent schedule:

S: r₁(A); w₁(A); r₂(D); w₂(D); r₁(B); w₁(B); r₁(C); w₁(C); r₂(C); w₂(C)

Proof via the precedence graph. The only element both use is C. r₁(C), w₁(C) come before r₂(C), w₂(C), and the pairs w₁(C)/r₂(C), w₁(C)/w₂(C), r₁(C)/w₂(C) all conflict with T₁ first: arc T₁ → T₂. A, B (T₁ only) and D (T₂ only) give no arcs. The graph T₁ → T₂ is acyclic, so S is conflict-serializable, equivalent to (T₁, T₂).

Proof via swaps: move r₂(D) and w₂(D) down past r₁(B), w₁(B), r₁(C), w₁(C); each pair involves D and a different element, so no swap is a conflict. The result r₁(A) w₁(A) r₁(B) w₁(B) r₁(C) w₁(C) r₂(D) w₂(D) r₂(C) w₂(C) is serial.

(Any interleaving that keeps all of T₁'s C-actions on one side of T₂'s C-actions is a correct answer. Interleaving them, e.g. r₁(C); r₂(C); w₁(C); w₂(C), would create arcs both ways and be a lost update.)

DB QB · 17-18 2a · 21 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2017–18 · 21 marks

The highly anticipated Marvel movie "Avenger: Endgame" has been released recently and theatres are flooded with movie-lovers. Three superhero enthusiasts Leonard, Howard, and Raj are dying to watch the movie in any first-day show, but they failed to manage tickets. Out of desperation, they decided to hack the database managing seat reservation system of a nearby theatre concurrently. At first, Leonard hacked into the system. He read all the reservations for the matinee show. After making changes, he saved the reservations. Then Howard read the reservations for the same show and did the same as Leonard. Next, Leonard read the reservations for night show on the same date and after making changes saved those. Subsequently, Raj changed the reservations for a morning show and saved it in the database. Finally, Howard repeated the same process for the same night show as Leonard did. [Note that, there are different relations for morning, matinee and night show.]
(i) Construct the schedule of actions using standard notations
(ii) What is a conflict serializable schedule? Show whether the schedule of previous question is conflict serializable or not without using Precedence graph.
(iii) Show whether the schedule is conflict serializable or not using Precedence graph. If it is not conflict serializable, then briefly describe the reason. If it is conflict serializable, then determine the serializability order using topological sort.

Show worked answer

(i) T₁ = Leonard, T₂ = Howard, T₃ = Raj; elements M (matinee), N (night), O (morning). Raj “changed” the morning reservations, which means reading them and writing them back.

S: r₁(M); w₁(M); r₂(M); w₂(M); r₁(N); w₁(N); r₃(O); w₃(O); r₂(N); w₂(N)

(ii) A schedule is conflict-serializable if it can be turned into a serial schedule by swapping adjacent actions of different transactions that do not conflict (do not touch the same element with at least one write). Swaps:

  1. Move r₁(N), w₁(N) up past w₂(M), r₂(M): N vs M, no conflict. Now r₁(M) w₁(M) r₁(N) w₁(N) r₂(M) w₂(M) r₃(O) w₃(O) r₂(N) w₂(N).
  2. Move r₃(O), w₃(O) up past w₂(M), r₂(M): O vs M, no conflict. Now r₁(M) w₁(M) r₁(N) w₁(N) r₃(O) w₃(O) r₂(M) w₂(M) r₂(N) w₂(N).

This is the serial schedule (T₁, T₃, T₂), so S is conflict-serializable. (T₂'s N-actions never had to pass T₁'s N-actions, which would be forbidden.)

(iii) M: w₁(M) before r₂(M), w₂(M) (and r₁(M) before w₂(M)): T₁ → T₂. N: w₁(N) before r₂(N), w₂(N): T₁ → T₂. O: only T₃.

Acyclic, so conflict-serializable. Topological sort: initially T₁ and T₃ have no incoming arcs. Removing them in either order, then T₂, gives the orders (T₁, T₂, T₃), (T₁, T₃, T₂) and (T₃, T₁, T₂). All three are conflict-equivalent to S.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2010–11 · 10 marks

Explain with examples, the testing of serializability by using precedence graph. Show the cases of both conflict and non-conflict schedules.

Show worked answer

Test. Nodes = transactions. For each pair of actions A₁ of Ti and A₂ of Tj (i ≠ j) with A₁ earlier, same element, at least one a write, add arc Ti → Tj. If the graph has a cycle the schedule is not conflict-serializable; if not, it is, and any topological order is an equivalent serial order. Reason: conflicting pairs can never change order under legal swaps, so every arc is a constraint “Ti before Tj” on the serial order; a cycle makes the constraints contradictory, and without a cycle a topological order satisfies all of them (proof in Chapter 5).

Conflict-serializable case: S = r₂(A); r₁(B); w₂(A); r₃(A); w₁(B); w₃(A); r₂(B); w₂(B). Arcs 2 → 3 (on A) and 1 → 2 (on B). Acyclic; serial order (T₁, T₂, T₃).

Non-conflict-serializable case: S₁ = r₂(A); r₁(B); w₂(A); r₂(B); r₃(A); w₁(B); w₃(A); w₂(B). Arcs 2 → 3, 1 → 2 (r₁(B) before w₂(B)) and 2 → 1 (r₂(B) before w₁(B)). Cycle 1 → 2 → 1; not conflict-serializable.

Both graphs are drawn step by step in the two animations of Section 2.

Summary: draw a node per transaction and an arc Ti → Tj for each conflicting pair (same element, at least one write) with Ti's action first. A cycle means not conflict-serializable. No cycle means conflict-serializable, and every topological order of the graph is an equivalent serial order. Work element by element so no pair is missed, and remember that two reads never make an arc.
