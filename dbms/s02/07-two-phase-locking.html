Two-Phase Locking

One extra rule turns locking into a guarantee: every transaction takes all its locks before it releases any. This chapter states the rule, proves that it makes every legal schedule conflict-serializable, and shows the price it does not remove: deadlock. It includes a 2PL simulator that tracks growing and shrinking phases.

1. The 2PL condition (slide 36)

Two-phase locking (2PL) condition
In every transaction, all lock actions precede all unlock actions. Formally: Ti has no li(X) after any ui(Y).
Growing phase
The first phase of a 2PL transaction, in which it obtains locks (and may read and write) but releases none.
Shrinking phase
The second phase, starting at its first unlock, in which it releases locks and obtains none.
Lock point
The moment between the phases: after the last lock is obtained and before the first unlock. At that moment the transaction holds every lock it will ever hold.
2PL transaction / 2PL schedule
A transaction obeying the condition is two-phase-locked. A schedule “satisfies 2PL” when all its transactions are 2PL.

2PL is a condition on each transaction's own order of actions, like consistency. It says nothing about when a transaction must unlock each element, only that no lock request may come after an unlock.

The slides call the result “surprising”: a legal schedule of consistent 2PL transactions is always conflict-serializable. The rest of this chapter shows why, and what 2PL does not fix.

2. A 2PL schedule (slide 37)

Slide 37 repeats the schedule of Fig. 18.13 (Chapter 6). Step through it with the phases shown:

Slide 37 question: why does this schedule satisfy the 2PL condition?

T₁'s lock actions are l₁(A) and l₁(B); its unlocks are u₁(A) and u₁(B). Both locks come before u₁(A), its first unlock, so T₁ is 2PL (lock point just after l₁(B)). T₂'s lock actions are l₂(A) and l₂(B) (requested, denied, then granted); its first unlock u₂(A) comes after l₂(B). So T₂ is 2PL too. Compare Fig. 18.12 in Chapter 6, where T₁ executes u₁(A) and then l₁(B): that transaction is not 2PL, and the schedule was not serializable.

3. Why 2PL schedules are conflict-serializable (slides 38–40)

Claim. Every legal schedule S of consistent 2PL transactions is conflict-serializable. Conflict-equivalence concerns reads and writes only, so we swap reads and writes and put lock and unlock actions back around them at the end.

Proof by induction on n, the number of transactions.

Basis, n = 1. A schedule of one transaction is serial.

Induction step. Assume every such schedule of n − 1 transactions is conflict-serializable. Let Sn have n transactions, and let ui(X) be the first unlock action in the whole schedule; it belongs to some Ti.

Slide 39 question 1: why is there no unlock action before ui(X)?

By the choice of ui(X): it is the first unlock in Sn, of any transaction. So before it, no transaction has released anything.

Slide 39 question 2: why can no other transaction precede Ti?

Suppose some action aj of another transaction Tj conflicts with an action ai of Ti on element Y and comes ahead of it, say wj(Y) … wi(Y). By consistency, Tj held a lock on Y at wj(Y) and Ti held one at wi(Y). By legality they cannot have held them at the same time, so the schedule must contain, in this order:

… wj(Y) … uj(Y) … li(Y) … wi(Y) …

uj(Y) is an unlock, and ui(X) is the first unlock of all, so ui(X) comes before (or is no later than) uj(Y), and therefore before li(Y). Then Ti locks Y after unlocking X, so Ti is not 2PL, a contradiction. The same argument works for any conflicting pair (read–write or write–read). So no action of another transaction that conflicts with an action of Ti comes before it. In the slide's words: at ui(X), Ti already holds all its locks, so nobody else can have touched Ti's elements in a conflicting way earlier.

Hence all of Ti's reads and writes can be swapped to the front without passing a conflicting action, giving

(actions of Ti) (actions of the other n − 1 transactions)

The tail is still a legal schedule of consistent 2PL transactions (removing Ti cannot break any of these properties for the others), so by the induction hypothesis it is conflict-serializable. Put Ti first and the tail's serial order after it. ∎

Consequence. One conflict-equivalent serial order is the order of the transactions' first unlocks (equivalently, of their lock points). In Fig. 18.13, T₁ unlocks first, so the order is (T₁, T₂), matching A = B = 250.

4. The price: deadlock (slide 41)

2PL does not stop transactions from waiting for each other forever. Change T₂ to work on B first (both are still 2PL):

T₁: l₁(A); r₁(A); A := A+100; w₁(A); l₁(B); u₁(A); r₁(B); B := B+100; w₁(B); u₁(B);
T₂: l₂(B); r₂(B); B := B*2;   w₂(B); l₂(A); u₂(B); r₂(A); A := A*2;   w₂(A); u₂(A);
Deadlock
A situation in which each transaction of a set is waiting for a lock held by another transaction of the set, so none of them can ever proceed.
Waits-for graph
A node per transaction and an arc T → U when T waits for a lock that U holds. A deadlock is a cycle in this graph.

T₁ holds A and wants B; T₂ holds B and wants A. Each waits for the other forever. Note that it would be wrong simply to let both proceed: T₁ has already added 100 to A and T₂ has already doubled B, so whatever happens next, A and B have had their operations in opposite orders and A = B cannot hold. The system must abort one of them; methods to detect or prevent deadlocks (timeouts, waits-for graphs, timestamp-based wait-die and wound-wait) are the subject of Lec 04 (textbook §19.2).

5. 2PL simulator

Type any requests with l and u actions, or type only reads and writes and let the scheduler insert the locks. “Unlock: all at the end” gives 2PL transactions; “right after last use” often breaks 2PL, and the simulator then flags the offending lock. Watch the phase of each transaction, the delays, and the serial order reported for the reads and writes that ran.

DB QB · 24-25 1c · 9 marks (Compulsory)  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-1 | 2024–25 · 9 marks

Define Strict 2PL (two-phase locking) protocol. Show that a 2PL schedule is conflict-serializable.

Show worked answer

Strict 2PL. A transaction follows strict two-phase locking if it is two-phase locked and it releases no exclusive (write) locks until it has committed or aborted (and, in the textbook's version, §19.1.3, until the commit or abort log record has been flushed to disk). So no other transaction can read or overwrite a value it wrote until that value is committed; this avoids cascading rollback (proof in Lec 03, Chapter 8). Many systems hold all locks until commit (“rigorous” 2PL).

2PL ⇒ conflict-serializable. Induction on the number n of transactions in a legal schedule S of consistent 2PL transactions.

  • n = 1: S is serial.
  • n > 1: let ui(X) be the first unlock in S, from Ti. Suppose an action of Tj (j ≠ i) on element Y conflicts with a later action of Ti on Y. By legality and consistency, S contains … aj(Y) … uj(Y) … li(Y) … ai(Y). Since ui(X) is the first unlock, it precedes uj(Y), hence precedes li(Y), so Ti locks after unlocking: not 2PL, contradiction. So every read/write of Ti can be swapped to the front past non-conflicting actions: S ≡ (Ti's actions)(rest).
  • The rest is a legal schedule of n − 1 consistent 2PL transactions, conflict-serializable by hypothesis. So S is conflict-serializable, with Ti first. ∎

Alternative form (used by the question bank, also correct): if 2PL schedule S had a cycle T₁ → T₂ → ⋯ → Tn → T₁ in its precedence graph, each arc Tk → Tk+1 means Tk released a lock that Tk+1 later acquired, so lockpoint(Tk) < lockpoint(Tk+1). Around the cycle this gives lockpoint(T₁) < lockpoint(T₁), impossible.

DB QB · 23-24 7a · 10 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-1 | 2023–24 · 10 marks

Show that if a schedule follows two-phase locking (2PL) protocol, then it is conflict-serializable.

Show worked answer

Let S be a legal schedule of consistent 2PL transactions. Induction on n.

Basis n = 1: S is serial, so conflict-serializable.

Hypothesis: every such schedule with n − 1 transactions is conflict-serializable.

Step. Let ui(X) be the first unlock action in S; it belongs to Ti. Claim: no action of another transaction Tj that conflicts with an action of Ti appears before it. Suppose wj(Y) precedes wi(Y) (the read–write cases are identical). Consistency means Tj held a lock on Y at wj(Y) and Ti at wi(Y); legality means Tj released Y before Ti locked it:

… wj(Y) … uj(Y) … li(Y) … wi(Y) …

ui(X) is the first unlock, so it is at or before uj(Y), hence before li(Y). Ti would lock Y after unlocking X, contradicting 2PL. Therefore each read and write of Ti can be moved to the front by swaps with non-conflicting actions of other transactions, giving (actions of Ti)(actions of the others). The others form a legal schedule of n − 1 consistent 2PL transactions, which by hypothesis can be swapped into a serial schedule. Placing lock/unlock actions back around each transaction's reads and writes gives a legal serial schedule conflict-equivalent to S, with the transactions in the order of their first unlocks. ∎

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2012–13 · 6 marks

If all transactions are well-formed and two-phase, then any legal history will be serializable. Justify this statement.

Show worked answer

“Well-formed” is another name for consistent: a transaction reads or writes an element only while holding a lock on it, and releases every lock it takes. “Legal history” means a legal schedule: no two transactions hold a lock on the same element at once. “Two-phase” means all lock actions precede all unlock actions in each transaction.

Justification: exactly the theorem of Section 3. Take the first unlock ui(X) in the history. Any conflicting action of another transaction Tj placed before an action of Ti on element Y would force uj(Y) and then li(Y) between them (well-formedness and legality), and li(Y) would come after Ti's first unlock (two-phase violated). So Ti's actions move to the front by non-conflicting swaps, and induction on the remaining transactions finishes the proof. The history is conflict-serializable, and conflict-serializable implies serializable. The equivalent serial order is the order of the transactions' lock points.

Each hypothesis is needed. Without well-formedness a transaction could access data it has not locked. Without legality two transactions could hold the same lock. Without two-phase, Fig. 18.12 (Chapter 6) is legal and well-formed but not serializable.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2011–12 · 8 marks

Define serial, serializable, conflict-serializable, and two-phase locking in terms of database transactions.

Show worked answer
  • Serial schedule: all actions of one transaction, then all actions of the next, and so on, with no interleaving. Example: r₁(A) w₁(A) r₂(A) w₂(A) is the serial schedule (T₁, T₂).
  • Serializable schedule: a schedule S for which some serial schedule S′ has the same effect on the database for every initial state. Example: r₁(A) w₁(A) r₂(A) w₂(A) r₁(B) w₁(B) r₂(B) w₂(B) has the effect of (T₁, T₂).
  • Conflict-serializable schedule: a schedule that can be turned into a serial schedule by swapping adjacent actions that do not conflict (actions conflict when they are from the same transaction, or touch the same element with at least one write). Test: its precedence graph is acyclic. Every conflict-serializable schedule is serializable, not conversely.
  • Two-phase locking: a locking discipline in which every transaction performs all its lock actions before any unlock action: a growing phase acquiring locks, then a shrinking phase releasing them. Every legal schedule of consistent 2PL transactions is conflict-serializable. Example: l₁(A) r₁(A) w₁(A) l₁(B) u₁(A) r₁(B) w₁(B) u₁(B) is 2PL; l₁(A) r₁(A) u₁(A) l₁(B) … is not.
BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2010–11 · 5 marks

Explain two-phase locking protocol.

Show worked answer

Under two-phase locking each transaction must request a lock on an element before reading or writing it, and in each transaction all lock requests come before all unlocks. A transaction therefore has a growing phase (acquiring locks, no releases) and a shrinking phase (releasing locks, no new locks). The point between them, when it holds all its locks, is its lock point. The locking scheduler grants a lock only if no other transaction holds a conflicting lock on the element, and otherwise makes the requester wait.

Guarantee: every legal schedule of 2PL transactions is conflict-serializable, with the transactions ordered by their lock points (proof in Section 3). Example: T₁: l₁(A) r₁(A) w₁(A) l₁(B) u₁(A) r₁(B) w₁(B) u₁(B) and T₂ similar: the scheduler delays T₂'s l₂(B) until T₁ unlocks B, so the result equals (T₁, T₂) (Fig. 18.13).

Limitations: it can deadlock (T₁ holds A and waits for B while T₂ holds B and waits for A), and basic 2PL allows cascading rollback if a transaction releases a write lock before committing; strict 2PL (hold write locks until commit) fixes the latter.

DB QB · 17-18 3c · 5 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2017–18, 2016–17, 2011–12 · 5 marks

What is deadlock in database transaction? Explain with example.

Show worked answer

A deadlock is a state in which each transaction of a set waits for a lock (or other resource) held by another transaction of the same set, so none of them can ever continue. With locking schedulers it arises because a transaction keeps its locks while waiting for more.

Example (slide 41). T₁ and T₂ are both two-phase locked:

l₁(A); r₁(A);
                    l₂(B); r₂(B);
w₁(A);
                    w₂(B);
l₁(B)  denied       l₂(A)  denied

T₁ holds A and waits for B; T₂ holds B and waits for A. The waits-for graph has the cycle T₁ → T₂ → T₁. Neither can proceed, and they cannot both be allowed to continue, since T₁ has already changed A and T₂ has changed B in opposite orders. The system must break the deadlock by aborting (rolling back) one transaction, then let the other finish. Deadlocks are detected with a waits-for graph or timeouts, or prevented by ordering lock requests or by timestamp schemes (wait-die, wound-wait).

Summary: two-phase locking requires every transaction to take all its locks before releasing any. Then every legal schedule of consistent 2PL transactions is conflict-serializable: the transaction with the first unlock can be swapped to the front, and induction does the rest; the serial order is the order of lock points. 2PL does not prevent deadlock, where transactions hold locks while waiting for each other's; one of them must be aborted.
