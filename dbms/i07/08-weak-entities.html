Weak Entity Sets

Room 201 means nothing until we say which building. This chapter explains weak entity sets, their identifying owner and partial key, the notation (double rectangle, double diamond, dashed underline), the programming-team and building-room examples, and how a weak entity becomes an Oracle table.

1. What a weak entity set is (slide 78)

Weak entity set
An entity set that cannot be identified by its own attributes alone. Its entities are identified only together with an entity of another set.
Identifying owner
The entity set (or sets) that supplies the missing part of the identity. Building owns Room.
Identifying relationship
The relationship from the weak entity set to its owner. It is many-to-one toward the owner, and every weak entity must take part in it (total participation): a room belongs to exactly one building.
Partial key (discriminator)
The weak entity's own attributes that distinguish it among the entities of the same owner: room_no distinguishes the rooms of one building.
Strong entity set
An ordinary entity set with a key of its own.

The slide's intuition: a room number may identify a room only inside a particular building. Room 201 in one building is different from Room 201 in another building. The full identity is

identity of a Room = key of its Building + room_no

2. Strong or weak? (slide 79)

Weak (identity depends on an owner)Not necessarily weak
A Room may require a Building.
An Answer may require a Question (answer 1, 2, 3 of question 17).
A Team may require a University.		 A Tire can be treated as strong if the system tracks tires independently, even before they are attached to a car (each tire has a serial number).

Weakness is a modeling decision based on how the domain identifies things. If the tire shop numbers tires "front-left, front-right, …" of a car, Tire is weak with owner Car and partial key position. If every tire carries a factory serial number and is stocked before it is fitted, Tire is strong with key serial_no, and "fitted to" is an ordinary relationship.

3. Notation (slide 80)

  • Weak entity set: double rectangle.
  • Identifying relationship: double diamond, with the arrow toward the owner (many-to-one).
  • Partial key: dashed underline.

4. Example: programming contest teams (slides 81–83)

Universities have programming contest teams. A team has a number, team name, and coach. The same team number may be used by different universities.

Team is weak if number is unique only inside one University. University is the identifying owner, and number is the partial key.

Why Team is weak here (slide 83). "Programming contest team 1" is not specific enough: BUET has a team 1, and so does every other university. "BUET programming contest team 1" is specific. The owner entity gives the missing context:

Team identity = University identity + Team partial key = (University.name, number)

The slide also puts totalTeam on University. That value can be computed by counting the university's teams, so it is a derived attribute (Chapter 10 draws such attributes with a dashed oval). If it is stored, it must be kept consistent with the actual teams.

6. From weak entity to table

Chapter 11 treats all conversion rules; the weak-entity rule is short enough to state now because exam questions on weak entities always ask for it:

  1. The weak entity set becomes a table with its own attributes.
  2. Add the key of the owner as columns; they form a foreign key to the owner's table.
  3. The primary key is owner key + partial key.
  4. The identifying relationship needs no table: the foreign key already records which owner each weak entity belongs to.
  5. Usually ON DELETE CASCADE: a room cannot exist without its building, so deleting the building deletes its rooms.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2012–13 · 10 marks

What is a weak entity set? What is the requirement of a weak entity set?

Show worked answer

A weak entity set is one that cannot be identified by its own attributes; it is identified through an owner entity set (definition and example as above: Room in Building).

Requirements (for E to be a legal weak entity set, Garcia-Molina et al. §4.4.2):

  1. An identifying owner exists. E is connected to one or more entity sets F by identifying relationships R.
  2. R is many-to-one from E to F (arrow into F) with total participation of E: every weak entity has exactly one owner through R. (If R were many-to-many, "the owner's key" would not be one value.)
  3. The key of E is its partial key (zero or more of its own attributes, dashed underline) plus the key attributes of every owner F. F may itself be weak; then its key is taken recursively from its own owner.
  4. Existence dependency: a weak entity cannot exist without its owner, so deleting an owner deletes its weak entities (ON DELETE CASCADE in tables).
  5. In the diagram, E is a double rectangle and R a double diamond.
BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 10 marks

Discuss with example the difference between strong and weak relationships in the context of relational database modelling. How does security concern arise by the choice of primary key?

Show worked answer

In relational modelling (and design tools such as Oracle SQL Developer Data Modeler or MySQL Workbench) a strong relationship is an identifying one: the parent's primary key becomes part of the child's primary key, which is exactly how a weak entity set is attached to its owner. A weak relationship is non-identifying: the parent's key appears in the child only as an ordinary foreign key, and the child has its own primary key. (The names are confusing: a strong, identifying relationship is the one that creates a weak entity.)

Example. Department–Employee via worksIn is non-identifying (weak): Employee(emp_id, name, dept_id); an employee exists and is identified independently of the department, and dept_id may even be NULL or change. Employee–Dependent via has is identifying (strong): Dependent(emp_id, dep_name, relation); the dependent cannot be identified or exist without the employee.

Security and the choice of primary key. A primary key is copied into every table that references it (as a foreign key), and into every child key along identifying relationships. If the primary key is a sensitive natural value, such as a national ID, SSN, phone number or email, that value spreads to every related table, index, log, URL and report, and anyone allowed to read any of those tables sees it. Problems:

  • Exposure: users who need only Dependent or Payroll data also learn the employee's national ID.
  • Hard to change or erase: if a person's ID or email must change or be removed (privacy law), every referencing row must be updated.
  • Guessable keys: sequential or meaningful keys in application URLs (…/student/2105001) let an attacker enumerate other records if access checks are weak.

Mitigation: use a meaningless surrogate key (an Oracle identity column or sequence number) as the primary key, keep the sensitive natural key as a UNIQUE NOT NULL attribute in one well-protected table, and grant access to it separately (views, column privileges). Identifying relationships are then built on the surrogate key.

Summary: a weak entity set cannot be identified by its own attributes; its key is the owner's key plus its partial key. It is drawn as a double rectangle joined by a double diamond (many-to-one, total) to its owner, with the partial key dashed-underlined. Weakness is a modeling decision. In tables, the weak entity gets the owner's key as a foreign key that is also part of its primary key, usually with ON DELETE CASCADE, and the identifying relationship gets no table.
