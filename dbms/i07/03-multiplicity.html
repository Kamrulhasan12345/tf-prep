Multiplicity, Roles and the Notation Summary

How many entities may be related? This chapter explains one-to-one, many-to-one and many-to-many relationships, how the slides draw them with arrowheads, how to read them one side at a time, recursive relationships with role names, and the full notation, ending with a drill that draws random diagrams for you to read.

1. What multiplicity is (slide 26)

Multiplicity (of a binary relationship)
A constraint on how many entities of one set may be related to one entity of the other set. The common forms are one-to-one, many-to-one (equivalently one-to-many read the other way) and many-to-many. Chen-style books call it the cardinality ratio and write 1:1, N:1, M:N.

In the slides' notation (the textbook's, Garcia-Molina et al. §4.1.6):

  • an arrowhead marks the "one" side: the arrow points into the entity set of which there is at most one;
  • a plain connector marks the "many" side.

The question to ask, from the slide: for one entity on the other side, how many entities can appear here? If the answer is "at most one", draw an arrow into this side. Multiplicity is a design decision taken from the requirements, not something we discover from sample data. And the arrow shows a constraint; it does not show data flow. Nothing "moves" from Course to Department.

2. Multiplicity as matching (slide 27)

Picture each entity set as a bag of entities and each relationship pair as a line between two of them. The four kinds differ only in how many lines may meet at one entity.

Note that "at most one" allows zero: in the many-to-one row, element 3 might have no partner at all. Whether every entity must take part is a different constraint, called participation; the slides record it as a design note, and Chapter 10 shows the textbook's double-line notation for it.

3. Reading multiplicity (slide 28)

  1. Read one side at a time.
  2. An arrow toward an entity set means at most one entity from that set.
  3. A plain connector means many possible entities from that set.
  4. Arrows on both sides: one-to-one. No arrows: many-to-many.

The slide's question is the procedure: if one item on the other side is fixed, how many items can appear here? The animation reads the three examples of slides 29–31 this way.

Slides 29–31 in words

One-to-one (slide 29): Manager ←manages→ Branch. One manager manages at most one branch, and each branch has at most one manager.

One-to-many (slide 30): Faculty ←offers— Course. The arrow points to Faculty: fix a course, and there is at most one faculty offering it. Fix a faculty, and the line into Course is plain: many courses. "One faculty may offer many courses, but each course is offered by at most one faculty."

Many-to-many (slide 31): Book —writtenBy— Author. Fix a book: many authors. Fix an author: many books. No arrows.

4. Recursive relationships and roles (slide 32)

Recursive relationship
A relationship in which the same entity set appears more than once. Slide 32's manages connects Employee to Employee: one employee manages another.
Role
The function an entity set plays in a relationship, written as a label on the line. When an entity set appears twice, the role names (manager, subordinate) say which line is which; without them "Employee manages Employee" is ambiguous.

Read it like any binary relationship, using the roles as the two "sides": fix a subordinate, and there is at most one manager (arrow on the manager line); fix a manager, and there may be many subordinates (plain line). The relationship set is a set of pairs (manager, subordinate), both of which are employees. A recursive relationship is not restricted to Employee: a course has prerequisite courses (Chapter 11), a person marries a person, a comment replies to a comment.

Other notations in exam papers. Many past questions draw diagrams in Chen's notation (Silberschatz, Elmasri–Navathe), where multiplicity is written as labels 1, N, M on the lines instead of arrows. The label sits next to the entity set it counts: in Teacher —1— teach —m— Course each course has one teacher and a teacher has m courses. The two notations translate directly: "1" next to an entity set = arrow into it; "N"/"M" = plain line. Some papers use crow's-foot marks (a fork for "many", a bar for "one"). Chapter 10 covers these, plus the double line for total participation.

6. A complete ERD (slide 34)

Reading every relationship of slide 34:

  • enrolls (Student, Course): no arrows, many-to-many. It carries grade, a relationship attribute.
  • offeredBy (Course, Department): arrow into Department. Each course is offered by at most one department; a department offers many courses.
  • teaches (Course, Instructor): no arrows in the slide, so many-to-many: a course may have several instructors and an instructor teaches several courses. (Slide 91 warns that making it one-to-many would be a mistake if a course can have several instructors.)
  • Keys: id for Student and code for Course are underlined. The slide omits the keys of Department and Instructor; a full design would add them (Chapter 9, "missing keys").

Summary: multiplicity says how many entities may be related. In the slides an arrow points into the "one" side and a plain line means "many"; arrows both ways is one-to-one, none is many-to-many. Read one side at a time: fix one entity on the other side and ask how many can appear here. A recursive relationship uses one entity set twice, with role names on the lines. Chen's 1/N/M labels and the slides' arrows say the same thing.
