Why Data Lives in Several Tables

Before learning how to query several tables at once, we need to see why a database splits its data into several tables in the first place. This chapter follows slides 2–6: one big table causes repetition and update mistakes, splitting it fixes that, and a primary key / foreign key pair lets us join the pieces back together.

1. One table is not enough (slides 2–3)

Slide 2 starts with a single table, Product, that lists four products with their name, price, category and manufacturer. So far there is no problem: each row describes one product.

Now the slide asks us to add company information, like the country and the stock price. The obvious move is to add two more columns to the same table (slide 3). The result looks harmless, but look at the GizmoWorks rows: both say USA and 25. The country and stock price of a company are facts about the company, yet they are now stored once for every product the company makes. If GizmoWorks made 500 products, "USA, 25" would be written 500 times.

Redundancy
Storing the same fact more than once. It wastes space, and worse, the copies can disagree.
Update anomaly
A change to one fact has to be made in several rows. If any copy is missed, the table contradicts itself.
Insertion and deletion anomalies
You cannot record a company that has no products yet (there is no product row to put it in), and deleting a company's last product also deletes everything known about the company.

The animation shows the update anomaly. GizmoWorks' stock price rises from 25 to 30. In the wide table that fact lives in two rows, and an update that touches only one of them leaves the database saying two different things.

2. The solution: split the table (slides 4–5)

The fix is to store each kind of fact in its own table. Product facts go in Product, company facts go in Company. Slide 4 first just cuts the wide table into two vertical pieces, and the circled row shows that the cut alone is not enough: the Company piece still has GizmoWorks twice. Slide 5 removes the duplicate, so each company appears exactly once in Company.

After the split, GizmoWorks' stock price is stored in one place. Changing it is a single-row update, and there is nothing to get out of step. A company with no products can still be inserted into Company, and deleting a product does not delete its maker.

Primary key and foreign key

Slide 5 labels the two columns that tie the tables together:

Primary key
A column (or set of columns) whose value is different in every row, so it identifies a row. In Company, the company name is the primary key: there is exactly one row per company.
Foreign key
A column in one table whose values must appear in the primary key of another table. Product.manufacturer is a foreign key that refers to Company. It says "this product is made by that company row", and the DBMS refuses a manufacturer that is not in Company.

In Oracle the two tables are declared like this (the slide names the company column Manufacturer; from slide 13 on it is called cname, and we use that name from now on):

CREATE TABLE Company (
  cname      VARCHAR2(20) PRIMARY KEY,
  stockPrice NUMBER,
  country    VARCHAR2(20)
);
CREATE TABLE Product (
  pname        VARCHAR2(20) PRIMARY KEY,
  price        NUMBER(8,2),
  category     VARCHAR2(20),
  manufacturer VARCHAR2(20) REFERENCES Company(cname)   -- foreign key
);

Notice the direction: many products can point to the same company, but each product points to one company. The foreign key sits on the "many" side. Prices are stored as plain numbers; the slides print them with a $ sign only for display.

3. How do we get the information back? (slide 6)

Splitting costs us something. A question such as "in which country is the Gizmo made?" now needs two tables: find Gizmo's manufacturer in Product, then look that name up in Company. Slide 6 asks exactly this: how to get the information back?

The answer is a join: a query that lists several tables in its FROM clause and matches their rows on the foreign key. The next chapter builds it from scratch. As a preview, this query glues every product to its company row and gives back the wide table of slide 3, without storing anything twice:

Question (worked answer): why not keep the wide table and be careful?

Because "be careful" is not something a database can rely on. Every program that updates the table would have to remember to change every copy, and one bug or one interrupted update leaves the data contradicting itself. With the split design, the DBMS itself guarantees that each fact has one home (the primary key stops a second GizmoWorks row) and that products only refer to real companies (the foreign key). The price is that queries must join tables, which is cheap and is exactly what SQL is built for. The design theory chapters (functional dependencies and normalization) make this splitting systematic.

Summary: putting company facts into the product table repeats them once per product, which wastes space and invites update, insertion and deletion anomalies. Splitting into Product and Company stores each fact once. Company's primary key (cname) identifies each company; Product's foreign key (manufacturer) refers to it. The split loses nothing, because a join on manufacturer = cname rebuilds the combined view whenever we need it.
