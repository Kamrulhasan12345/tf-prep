Writing Join Queries

Now we apply the join to the Product and Company tables and answer real questions with it. This chapter animates the Product–Company join, shows how to add ordinary conditions next to the join condition, warns about AND/OR precedence, and answers slide 18's question "what is the problem?" (duplicates, fixed by DISTINCT).

1. Joining Product and Company (slide 13)

The schema, with keys underlined on the slides, is

Product (pname, price, category, manufacturer)
Company (cname, stockPrice, country)

Product.manufacturer is a foreign key referring to Company.cname. Slide 13 joins the two tables on exactly that pair of columns:

SELECT *
FROM   Product, Company
WHERE  manufacturer = cname;

The column names are all different now (manufacturer vs cname), so no prefixes are needed. The cross product has 4 · 3 = 12 rows; the condition keeps the 4 in which a product meets its own manufacturer. The lines on the slide connect each product to its company row. Step through it:

Because manufacturer is a foreign key, every product finds exactly one company, so the join has as many rows as Product. That is the typical shape of a foreign-key join: the "many" side's rows, each extended with its "one" side's row. Here the result is the wide table of slide 3, rebuilt on demand.

2. Adding conditions (slides 14–15)

Find all products under $200 manufactured in Japan; return their names and prices.

"Manufactured in Japan" is a fact about the company, "under $200" is a fact about the product, so we need both tables. The WHERE clause then has three conditions joined by AND:

SELECT pname, price
FROM   Product, Company
WHERE  manufacturer = cname      -- the join condition (slide 14's bubble)
AND    country = 'Japan'         -- a condition on Company's columns
AND    price < 200;              -- a condition on Product's columns

Only the join condition relates the two tables. The other two are ordinary filters. On the slide data: of the 4 joined rows, the Japanese ones are SingleTouch (Canon) and MultiTouch (Hitachi), and only SingleTouch costs less than 200. Result: SingleTouch, 149.99 (slide 15).

Question (worked answer): what happens if you forget the join condition?

Without manufacturer = cname, the query works on the full cross product. Every product under 200 (Gizmo, Powergizmo, SingleTouch) is paired with every Japanese company (Canon, Hitachi), giving 3 · 2 = 6 rows, including nonsense such as "Gizmo, made in Japan". Forgetting the join condition is the single most common join bug: the query runs without an error and returns too many rows. A useful habit: with n tables in FROM you normally need at least n − 1 join conditions.

3. More joins, and AND/OR precedence (slide 16)

Find all Chinese companies that manufacture electronic products or toys.

SELECT cname
FROM   Product, Company
WHERE  manufacturer = cname
AND    country = 'China'
AND    (category = 'electronic' OR category = 'toy');

The parentheses are essential. SQL evaluates AND before OR (AND has higher precedence), just as multiplication comes before addition. Without the parentheses the condition means

(manufacturer = cname AND country = 'China' AND category = 'electronic')
OR category = 'toy'

and the second branch has no join condition at all. Every toy is then paired with every company in the cross product. On the slide's own four products there are no Chinese companies, so the slide's query returns nothing; the playground below uses the extended data (with two invented Chinese companies, Xiaomi and Lenovo) so you can see both versions:

The correct query returns Xiaomi twice, once for its electronic RedmiPad and once for its toy MiRobot. That is the subtlety of the next section.

4. A subtlety about joins: duplicates (slides 17–19)

Find all countries that manufacture some product in the 'Gadgets' category.

SELECT country
FROM   Product, Company
WHERE  manufacturer = cname
AND    category = 'Gadgets';

Slide 18 shows the result as "??, ??" and asks what is the problem?

Question (worked answer): slide 18, "What is the problem?"

Two products are in the Gadgets category: Gizmo and Powergizmo. Both are made by GizmoWorks, so both join with the same Company row, and each joined row contributes its country. The result is USA, USA: the same country twice (slide 19). The question asks for countries, and a country is either in the answer or not, so the duplicate is wrong.

Duplicates appear because SQL results are bags (Chapter 2, slide 11): one output row per qualifying combination of input rows. Whenever the SELECT list does not include a key of every joined table, two different combinations can produce the same output. Solution (slide 19): add DISTINCT.

SELECT DISTINCT country
FROM   Product, Company
WHERE  manufacturer = cname
AND    category = 'Gadgets';      -- result: USA (once)

Do not sprinkle DISTINCT everywhere "just in case". It costs a sort or hash of the result, and sometimes the duplicates are meaningful (for example, "list the price of every product sold", where two equal prices are two facts). Ask: can two different input combinations produce the same output row, and should they be counted once?

Summary: a join query lists the tables in FROM and puts the join condition (foreign key = primary key) in WHERE, followed by any ordinary filters on either table. Forgetting the join condition silently returns cross-product rows. AND binds tighter than OR, so put parentheses around OR-conditions. A join can produce the same output row from different input rows; when the question asks for a set of values (countries, companies), use SELECT DISTINCT.
