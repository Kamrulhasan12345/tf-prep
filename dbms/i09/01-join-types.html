Inner and Outer Joins

An ordinary join throws away every row that has no partner. Sometimes those rows are exactly what we want to see: departments without employees, makers without laptops. This chapter lists the six kinds of join on slide 2, defines dangling tuples, and animates the inner, left, right and full outer joins of slides 3–6 so you can see which rows survive and where the NULLs come from. It also covers Oracle's old (+) notation.

1. Six ways to combine two tables (slide 2)

Slide 2 lists the join types in order of how they treat rows. In Oracle each has its own ANSI syntax in the FROM clause:

RIGHT OUTER JOINFROM PC RIGHT OUTER JOIN Laptop ON …inner join + unmatched rows of the right table
FULL OUTER JOINFROM PC FULL OUTER JOIN Laptop ON …inner join + unmatched rows of both tables

The words INNER and OUTER are optional: JOIN means INNER JOIN, and LEFT JOIN means LEFT OUTER JOIN. Chapters 2 and 3 of Lecture 08 covered the first three; this lecture is about the outer joins.

Careful with NATURAL JOIN here: PC(model, maker) and Laptop(model, maker) share both column names, so PC NATURAL JOIN Laptop requires equal model and equal maker, and returns nothing (no model is both a PC and a laptop). This is why the slides write the condition explicitly with ON.

2. The running example and the inner join (slide 3)

Lecture 09 uses two small tables (a simplified version of the computer database, with the maker stored in each table):

Slide 3: PC INNER JOIN Laptop ON PC.maker = Laptop.maker pairs each PC with each laptop of the same maker. A and B make both, so the result has two rows. C (only PCs) and D (only laptops) have no partner and disappear.

Dangling tuple
A row of one table that matches no row of the other table under the join condition. PC (1003, C) and Laptop (2003, D) are dangling. An inner join drops dangling tuples.
Outer join
A join that keeps dangling tuples too, padding the other table's columns with NULL. LEFT keeps the dangling tuples of the left table, RIGHT those of the right table, FULL those of both (slides 4–6: "common tuples plus dangling tuples from the left / right / both tables").

3. Left, right and full outer joins (slides 4–6)

Pick a join type and step through it. In each frame one PC row (left) is compared with every laptop row (right). Green result rows come from matches; amber rows are dangling tuples, and the NULLs in them are the padding.

Where do the NULLs come from? Not from the data: neither table contains a NULL. They are created by the outer join to fill the columns of a table that had no matching row. This is how to read the results:

  • LEFT (slide 4): rows A and B as in the inner join, plus (1003, C, NULL, NULL): C makes a PC but no laptop.
  • RIGHT (slide 5): A and B, plus (NULL, NULL, 2003, D): D makes a laptop but no PC.
  • FULL (slide 6): A and B, plus both dangling rows.

Two facts follow and are worth memorising. (1) The number of rows: inner ≤ left, inner ≤ right, and full = left + right − inner (here 2 ≤ 3, 3 and 3 + 3 − 2 = 4). (2) A LEFT JOIN B is the same as B RIGHT JOIN A (with the columns in a different order), so RIGHT JOIN is never strictly needed; many people write only LEFT joins.

Question (worked answer): does the playground run FULL OUTER JOIN?

Yes. Oracle has supported FULL OUTER JOIN since Oracle 9i, and the playground's SQLite engine (version 3.45) supports RIGHT and FULL OUTER JOIN natively, so every query on slides 3–19 runs as written. Some systems (MySQL, for example) have no FULL OUTER JOIN; there it is emulated by a left join plus the right join's dangling rows:

SELECT PC.model, PC.maker, Laptop.model, Laptop.maker
FROM   PC LEFT JOIN Laptop ON PC.maker = Laptop.maker
UNION ALL
SELECT PC.model, PC.maker, Laptop.model, Laptop.maker
FROM   PC RIGHT JOIN Laptop ON PC.maker = Laptop.maker
WHERE  PC.maker IS NULL;          -- only the right-dangling rows

The second half adds only rows that the first half does not have, so UNION ALL is safe and no rows are lost or doubled.

4. Oracle's old outer-join operator (+)

Before ANSI join syntax (Oracle 9i), Oracle wrote outer joins in the WHERE clause with the operator (+). You will meet it in old exam answers and old code, so you should be able to read it. The playground does not understand it; the code below is Oracle-only.

-- LEFT OUTER JOIN: keep all PCs. (+) goes on the side that may be missing (Laptop)
SELECT PC.model, PC.maker, Laptop.model, Laptop.maker
FROM   PC, Laptop
WHERE  PC.maker = Laptop.maker(+);

-- RIGHT OUTER JOIN: keep all laptops. (+) on the PC side
SELECT PC.model, PC.maker, Laptop.model, Laptop.maker
FROM   PC, Laptop
WHERE  PC.maker(+) = Laptop.maker;

Read (+) as "add NULL rows to this table when needed". It is placed on the deficient side, the table whose columns become NULL, which is the opposite of the LEFT/RIGHT words. The old syntax has limits that ANSI syntax does not: (+) cannot be put on both sides, so there is no full outer join (it must be written as the union of a left and a right join); it cannot be combined with OR or IN in the same condition; and every condition on the deficient table must also carry (+), or the outer join silently becomes an inner join. Oracle recommends the ANSI form, and the slides use it.

5. ON versus WHERE in an outer join

For an inner join it does not matter whether a condition is written in ON or in WHERE. For an outer join it matters a great deal, and it is a classic source of bugs. Using the HR schema: list every department with its employees earning more than 10000.

-- (a) condition in ON: decides which employees MATCH; every department survives
SELECT d.DEPARTMENTNAME, e.LASTNAME
FROM   DEPARTMENT d LEFT JOIN EMPLOYEE e
       ON e.DEPARTMENTID = d.DEPARTMENTID AND e.SALARY > 10000;

-- (b) condition in WHERE: applied AFTER the join; NULL-padded rows fail it and vanish
SELECT d.DEPARTMENTNAME, e.LASTNAME
FROM   DEPARTMENT d LEFT JOIN EMPLOYEE e
       ON e.DEPARTMENTID = d.DEPARTMENTID
WHERE  e.SALARY > 10000;

In (b), a department with no well-paid employee only has rows where e.SALARY is either ≤ 10000 or NULL, and NULL > 10000 is not true, so the department disappears: the left join has turned into an inner join. Query (a) keeps all 8 departments, with NULL for those without a match. Rule: conditions on the optional table of an outer join belong in ON (unless you are deliberately testing IS NULL, as in the next chapter).

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2016–17 · 15 marks

Explain the application of left, right and full outer joins with examples.

Show worked answer

An outer join returns the rows of the inner join plus the dangling tuples (rows with no match) of one or both tables, padding the other table's columns with NULL. Example tables (HR schema, simplified): EMPLOYEE(id, name, deptid) with rows (1, Abel, 80), (2, Fay, 20), (3, Grant, NULL); DEPARTMENT(deptid, dname) with rows (20, Marketing), (80, Sales), (190, Contracting).

Left outer join: all rows of the left table. Application: a report that must list every employee, including those not yet assigned a department.

SELECT e.name, d.dname
FROM   EMPLOYEE e LEFT OUTER JOIN DEPARTMENT d ON e.deptid = d.deptid;
-- Abel Sales | Fay Marketing | Grant NULL

Right outer join: all rows of the right table. Application: list every department with its employees, so that empty departments are visible (for example to find departments that need staff).

SELECT e.name, d.dname
FROM   EMPLOYEE e RIGHT OUTER JOIN DEPARTMENT d ON e.deptid = d.deptid;
-- Fay Marketing | Abel Sales | NULL Contracting

Full outer join: all rows of both. Application: reconciliation and auditing, finding in one query both employees without a department and departments without employees, or comparing two lists (e.g. makers of PCs vs makers of laptops, slide 17 of Lecture 09).

SELECT e.name, d.dname
FROM   EMPLOYEE e FULL OUTER JOIN DEPARTMENT d ON e.deptid = d.deptid;
-- Abel Sales | Fay Marketing | Grant NULL | NULL Contracting

A further application of all three: finding unmatched rows ("anti-join"). Adding WHERE d.deptid IS NULL to the left join returns only employees without a department; Lecture 09 slides 10–15 use exactly this to find makers of PCs but not laptops. Counting with outer joins (COUNT(e.id) after a left join from DEPARTMENT) gives 0 for empty departments instead of omitting them. In old Oracle syntax the left join is written WHERE e.deptid = d.deptid(+). The question bank's answer is correct.

With R(A, B) = {(1, x), (2, y)} and S(B, C) = {(x, 5), (y, 6), (z, 7)}: R CROSS JOIN S has 6 rows; R NATURAL JOIN S has (1, x, 5), (2, y, 6); R RIGHT OUTER JOIN S ON R.B = S.B adds (NULL, NULL, z, 7).

When outer joins are useful: whenever the rows that have no match must still be reported or counted: every student with the number of courses taken (0 for none), every department with its manager even if the manager is not yet appointed, or every PC maker together with any laptop they make. Example: SELECT d.DEPARTMENTNAME, COUNT(e.EMPLOYEEID) FROM DEPARTMENT d LEFT JOIN EMPLOYEE e ON e.DEPARTMENTID = d.DEPARTMENTID GROUP BY d.DEPARTMENTNAME lists Contracting with 0, which an inner join would omit.

Note: the question bank's comparison table says a natural join has at most min(M, N) rows and an outer join at least max(M, N). Both are wrong. A natural join can have up to M · N rows when the join values repeat (every row of R matches every row of S if they all share one B value). A left outer join has at least M rows, which can be fewer than N (e.g. 1 employee left-joined with 3 departments gives 1 row).

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2010–11 · 7 marks

Define natural joins and outer joins.

Show worked answer

Natural join (R ⋈ S): the join of R and S on equality of all attributes with the same name in both schemas, with one copy of each common attribute kept. If R(A, B, C) and S(C, D), R ⋈ S has schema (A, B, C, D) and contains a tuple for every pair of an R-tuple and an S-tuple with equal C. Tuples with no partner are dropped. If R and S share no attribute names, the natural join is the cross product. SQL: SELECT * FROM R NATURAL JOIN S.

Outer join: a join that also keeps the dangling tuples (tuples matching nothing) of one or both operands, padding the missing attributes with NULL. Left outer join (R ⟕ S) keeps all of R, right outer join (R ⟖ S) all of S, full outer join (R ⟗ S) both. Example with the PC/Laptop tables of Lecture 09: PC LEFT OUTER JOIN Laptop ON PC.maker = Laptop.maker contains (1003, C, NULL, NULL) because maker C has no laptop. The natural versions (NATURAL LEFT OUTER JOIN, …) also exist.

Summary: an inner join keeps only matching pairs; rows with no partner (dangling tuples) are dropped. LEFT, RIGHT and FULL OUTER JOIN add the dangling tuples of the left, right or both tables, filling the other table's columns with NULL. Full = left + right − inner in row count. Oracle's old notation puts (+) on the deficient side in the WHERE clause and cannot express a full join. In an outer join, conditions on the optional table belong in ON; in WHERE they discard the NULL-padded rows and turn the join back into an inner join.
