Nested and Correlated Subqueries

Slides 8–12 nest subqueries more deeply and, in slide 12, let the inner query refer to the outer row: a correlated subquery, evaluated once per outer row. This chapter unwinds slide 8's double nesting, explains why slide 9 compares (dept, salary) pairs and changes the data, contrasts "above the overall average" with "above the department average", animates the correlated evaluation, and works a 20-mark exam question.

1. Two levels of nesting (slide 8)

Find the employee(s) with the highest paid salary, a third way:

SELECT id
FROM   Employee
WHERE  salary > ALL (SELECT salary
                     FROM   Employee
                     WHERE  salary < ANY (SELECT salary
                                          FROM   Employee));

Read it from the inside out. The innermost query returns all salaries {50, 55, 60, 75} (thousands). The middle query keeps the salaries that are less than some salary: every salary except the largest, {50, 55, 60}. The outer query keeps salaries greater than all of those: only 75. Answer: 5004.

The slide's title calls this "correlated", but none of these subqueries mentions a column of an outer query, so each is evaluated just once; it is a nested (non-correlated) subquery. One quirk: if the table has a single employee, the middle query is empty, and > ALL of an empty set is TRUE, so that employee is (correctly) returned.

2. The highest paid in each department: row comparison (slide 9)

Find the highest paid employee(s) in each department. For slide 9 the data changes: employee 5003 (CSE) now earns 55,000, the same as the EEE maximum.

SELECT id, dept
FROM   Employee
WHERE  (dept, salary) IN (SELECT dept, MAX(salary)
                          FROM   Employee
                          GROUP BY dept);

The subquery returns one row per department with its maximum: (EEE, 55000) and (CSE, 75000). The outer query keeps an employee when the pair (dept, salary) is in that set. Answer: 5002 (EEE, 55,000) and 5004 (CSE, 75,000).

Question (worked answer): why compare the pair, and why did the slide change 5003's salary?

Suppose we compared only salaries: WHERE salary IN (SELECT MAX(salary) FROM Employee GROUP BY dept). The subquery gives {55000, 75000}, and 5003 earns 55,000, so 5003 would be returned too, although 55,000 is the maximum of EEE, not of CSE. With the original data (5003 earning 60,000) the bug is invisible; changing 5003 to 55,000 exposes it. Comparing (dept, salary) as a pair forces the maximum to be the maximum of the employee's own department. Oracle allows such multi-column (row) comparisons with IN; the playground does too.

3. Above the average (slides 10–11)

Find the employees that earn above average. Slide 10 again hard-codes the value (the average of 50, 55, 60 and 75 thousand is 60,000): WHERE salary > 60000. Slide 11 computes it:

SELECT *
FROM   Employee
WHERE  salary > (SELECT AVG(salary) FROM Employee);       -- 5004

The inner query is still independent of the outer row: it is computed once, 60,000, and each employee is compared with it. Note that an aggregate cannot be used directly in WHERE (WHERE salary > AVG(salary) is ORA-00934); the subquery is how you compare rows with an aggregate.

4. Correlated subqueries (slide 12)

Find the employees that earn above the average of the employees within their respective department.

SELECT *
FROM   Employee s
WHERE  salary > (SELECT AVG(salary)
                 FROM   Employee t
                 WHERE  s.dept = t.dept);
Correlated subquery
A subquery that refers to a column of the outer query (here s.dept). Its value depends on the current outer row, so conceptually it is re-evaluated once for every row of the outer query, with the outer row's values plugged in.

The two tuple variables are essential: s ranges over the outer Employee, t over the inner one, and s.dept = t.dept ties the inner average to the outer employee's department. (Inside the subquery, an unqualified column name refers to the nearest table, t; to reach the outer table you must qualify with s.) For an EEE employee the subquery returns AVG(50,000, 55,000) = 52,500; for a CSE employee AVG(60,000, 75,000) = 67,500. Answer: 5002 and 5004.

"Evaluated once per row" describes the meaning, not necessarily the execution: the optimizer may compute each department's average only once, or rewrite the query as a join with a grouped subquery in FROM. All produce the same result. An equivalent uncorrelated formulation, useful when you also want to print the average:

SELECT s.id, s.dept, s.salary, a.avg_sal
FROM   Employee s, (SELECT dept, AVG(salary) AS avg_sal
                    FROM Employee GROUP BY dept) a
WHERE  s.dept = a.dept AND s.salary > a.avg_sal;

Exam questions

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2010–11 · 20 marks

Consider the following schema: Product(pid, name, price, category, year, maker_cid), Purchase(buyer_ssn, seller_ssn, store, pid), Company(cid, name, stock_price, country), Person(ssn, name, phone_number, city). In Purchase, buyer_ssn and seller_ssn are foreign keys to Person and pid is a foreign key to Product; in Product, maker_cid is a foreign key to Company. Write SQL statements to answer the following queries:

(a) Find names of people buying telephony products.
(b) Find products (and their manufacturers) that are more expensive than all products made by the same manufacturer before 1972.
(c) Find names of people who bought Bangladeshi products and did not buy Indian products.
(d) Find names of products (and their manufacturers) whose prices are less than the average product price of that company.

Show worked answer

(a) Join buyer → purchase → product; DISTINCT because a person may buy several telephony products.

SELECT DISTINCT pe.name
FROM   Person pe, Purchase pu, Product pr
WHERE  pe.ssn = pu.buyer_ssn
AND    pu.pid = pr.pid
AND    pr.category = 'telephony';

(b) Correlated subquery: for each product p1, the prices of the same company's pre-1972 products.

SELECT p1.name, c.name AS manufacturer
FROM   Product p1, Company c
WHERE  p1.maker_cid = c.cid
AND    p1.price > ALL (SELECT p2.price
                       FROM   Product p2
                       WHERE  p2.maker_cid = p1.maker_cid
                       AND    p2.year < 1972);

If a company made nothing before 1972, the subquery is empty and > ALL is TRUE, so all its products are listed: they are (vacuously) more expensive than all its pre-1972 products. If the intended reading is "only companies that did make something before 1972", replace > ALL by > (SELECT MAX(p2.price) …), which is NULL (so FALSE) for an empty set. State the reading you choose.

(c) People who bought something Bangladeshi, minus people who bought something Indian. Work with ssn, not name, because two different people may share a name:

SELECT pe.name
FROM   Person pe
WHERE  pe.ssn IN (SELECT pu.buyer_ssn
                  FROM   Purchase pu, Product pr, Company c
                  WHERE  pu.pid = pr.pid AND pr.maker_cid = c.cid
                  AND    c.country = 'Bangladesh')
AND    pe.ssn NOT IN (SELECT pu.buyer_ssn
                      FROM   Purchase pu, Product pr, Company c
                      WHERE  pu.pid = pr.pid AND pr.maker_cid = c.cid
                      AND    c.country = 'India');

(buyer_ssn is part of every purchase, so it is never NULL and NOT IN is safe here.)

(d) Correlated subquery with AVG:

SELECT p.name, c.name AS manufacturer
FROM   Product p, Company c
WHERE  p.maker_cid = c.cid
AND    p.price < (SELECT AVG(p2.price)
                  FROM   Product p2
                  WHERE  p2.maker_cid = p.maker_cid);

The question bank's answers are correct (its (c) uses a join plus NOT IN, which is equivalent). The checks below run each answer on invented data: Walton (Bangladesh), Tata and Micromax (India), Nokia (Finland), with products from 1965 to 2020.

Summary: a subquery may contain further subqueries; evaluate them from the inside out. Comparing a row (dept, salary) with IN keeps the pairing that a single-column comparison would lose. A subquery that mentions a column of the outer query (s.dept) is correlated: it is evaluated once per outer row with that row's values, which is how "above the average of the employee's own department" is expressed. Use different aliases for the outer and inner copies of a table.
