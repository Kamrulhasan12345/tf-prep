Exam Practice II: University, Flights, Hospital, Projects

More full exam questions on joins, grouping and subqueries: three sets on the textbook's university schema (including the restricted versions "without IN or set operations" and "without WITH"), the airline database, a hospital database and an employee–project database. Every part has a model answer and an auto-checked version on invented data.

1. The university schema

Three of the questions use the university schema of Silberschatz, Korth and Sudarshan (also used in Lectures 13–15). The relevant tables and links:

department(dept_name, building, budget)
instructor(ID, name, dept_name → department, salary)
course    (course_id, title, dept_name → department, credits)
section   (course_id, sec_id, semester, year, building, room_number, time_slot_id)
teaches   (ID → instructor, course_id, sec_id, semester, year → section)
student   (ID, name, dept_name → department, tot_cred)
takes     (ID → student, course_id, sec_id, semester, year → section, grade)

The playground data is invented: 13 instructors in 6 departments (plus Elec. Eng. with no instructors or students), 25 students, sections in 2021 and 2022. Instructor John Doe teaches CS-101 and CS-190; Jane Doe teaches CS-315 and CS-319; section CS-101/1/Fall/2021 has 22 students.

Exam questions

DB QB · 19-20 7b · 10 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2019–20 · 20 marks

Consider the university schema (classroom, department, course, instructor, section, teaches, student, takes, advisor, time_slot, prereq). Write SQL queries for the following:
i. Find all instructors whose salary is above average for their department.
ii. Find all courses taught in both the Fall 2022 semester and in the Spring 2022 semester.
iii. Find all students who have taken all courses taught by instructor named 'John Doe'.
iv. List all departments along with the number of students in each department.
v. Find the departments that have the lowest average salary.

Show worked answer
-- i. correlated comparison with the department average
SELECT i1.ID, i1.name
FROM   instructor i1
WHERE  i1.salary > (SELECT AVG(i2.salary) FROM instructor i2
                    WHERE  i2.dept_name = i1.dept_name);

-- ii. intersection of the two semesters' course lists
SELECT course_id FROM section WHERE semester = 'Fall'   AND year = 2022
INTERSECT
SELECT course_id FROM section WHERE semester = 'Spring' AND year = 2022;

-- iii. division: no John Doe course that the student has not taken
SELECT s.ID, s.name
FROM   student s
WHERE  NOT EXISTS (
         SELECT t.course_id FROM teaches t, instructor i
         WHERE  t.ID = i.ID AND i.name = 'John Doe'
         MINUS
         SELECT tk.course_id FROM takes tk WHERE tk.ID = s.ID);

-- iv. left join so that departments without students show 0
SELECT   d.dept_name, COUNT(s.ID) AS num_students
FROM     department d LEFT JOIN student s ON s.dept_name = d.dept_name
GROUP BY d.dept_name;

-- v. minimum average (ties included)
SELECT   dept_name
FROM     instructor
GROUP BY dept_name
HAVING   AVG(salary) <= ALL (SELECT AVG(salary) FROM instructor GROUP BY dept_name);

Notes. (iii) If John Doe teaches several sections of one course, the MINUS works on course_ids, so a student needs to have taken each course once (any section). If no instructor is called John Doe, every student qualifies vacuously; mention it. (iv) COUNT(s.ID), not COUNT(*), so Elec. Eng. shows 0. (v) Only departments with instructors have an average. The question bank's answers are correct (it uses EXCEPT in (iii); write MINUS in Oracle).

DB QB · 18 CT2(A) · 4 × 5 marks (first set)

Given the university schema (classroom, department, course, instructor, section, teaches, student, takes, time_slot, prereq), write SQL queries for the following:
(1) Find the departments that have the highest average salary.
(2) Find all courses taught in both the Spring 2021 semester and in the Fall 2021 semester (you are not allowed to use the 'in' operator and any set operation).
(3) Find all students who have taken all courses taught by instructor named 'Jane Doe'.
(4) Find all departments where the average salary is greater than the average of the total salary at all departments (you are not allowed to use the 'with' clause).
(5) For each course section offered in 2021, find the average total credits of all students enrolled in the section, if the section has at least 20 students.

Show worked answer
-- (1)
SELECT   dept_name FROM instructor
GROUP BY dept_name
HAVING   AVG(salary) >= ALL (SELECT AVG(salary) FROM instructor GROUP BY dept_name);

-- (2) no IN, no INTERSECT: self-join of section (or EXISTS)
SELECT DISTINCT s1.course_id
FROM   section s1, section s2
WHERE  s1.course_id = s2.course_id
AND    s1.semester = 'Spring' AND s1.year = 2021
AND    s2.semester = 'Fall'   AND s2.year = 2021;
--   equivalently: … FROM section s1 WHERE s1.semester = 'Spring' AND s1.year = 2021
--                 AND EXISTS (SELECT * FROM section s2 WHERE s2.course_id = s1.course_id
--                             AND s2.semester = 'Fall' AND s2.year = 2021)

-- (3) division without set operations: double NOT EXISTS
SELECT s.ID, s.name FROM student s
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
        SELECT * FROM teaches t, instructor i
        WHERE  t.ID = i.ID AND i.name = 'Jane Doe'
        AND    NOT EXISTS (SELECT * FROM takes tk
                           WHERE tk.ID = s.ID AND tk.course_id = t.course_id));

-- (4) as worded: department average vs the average of the department totals (no WITH: derived table)
SELECT   dept_name FROM instructor
GROUP BY dept_name
HAVING   AVG(salary) > (SELECT AVG(total) FROM (SELECT SUM(salary) AS total
                                                FROM instructor GROUP BY dept_name));

-- (5)
SELECT   tk.course_id, tk.sec_id, tk.semester, AVG(st.tot_cred) AS avg_tot_cred
FROM     takes tk, student st
WHERE    tk.ID = st.ID AND tk.year = 2021
GROUP BY tk.course_id, tk.sec_id, tk.semester, tk.year
HAVING   COUNT(tk.ID) >= 20;

Notes. (2) DISTINCT, because a course with two Spring or two Fall sections would otherwise appear several times. (3) The question's set version forbids only IN and set operations in part (2), but the double NOT EXISTS is a useful form to know; the MINUS form of the previous question is equally correct here. (4) As worded, the condition compares an average salary with an average of total salaries, which are many times larger, so it is almost never true (on our data no department qualifies). The question is a variant of the textbook's "departments whose total salary is greater than the average of the total salary at all departments" (the next question asks exactly that); state which reading you answer. (5) A section is identified by (course_id, sec_id, semester, year), so group by all four.

DB QB · 18 CT2(A) · 4 × 5 marks (second set)

Given the same schema, write SQL queries for the following:
(1) Find all instructors whose salary is above average for their department.
(2) Find all courses taught in both the Fall 2021 semester and in the Spring 2021 semester (you are not allowed to use the 'in' operator and any set operation).
(3) Find all students who have taken all courses taught by instructor named 'John Doe'.
(4) Find all departments where the total salary is greater than the average of the total salary at all departments (you are not allowed to use the 'with' clause).
(5) List all departments along with the number of students in each department.

Show worked answer
-- (1)
SELECT i1.ID, i1.name FROM instructor i1
WHERE  i1.salary > (SELECT AVG(i2.salary) FROM instructor i2 WHERE i2.dept_name = i1.dept_name);

-- (2) EXISTS instead of IN / INTERSECT
SELECT DISTINCT s1.course_id
FROM   section s1
WHERE  s1.semester = 'Fall' AND s1.year = 2021
AND    EXISTS (SELECT * FROM section s2
               WHERE  s2.course_id = s1.course_id
               AND    s2.semester = 'Spring' AND s2.year = 2021);

-- (3)
SELECT s.ID, s.name FROM student s
WHERE  NOT EXISTS (SELECT t.course_id FROM teaches t, instructor i
                   WHERE  t.ID = i.ID AND i.name = 'John Doe'
                   MINUS
                   SELECT tk.course_id FROM takes tk WHERE tk.ID = s.ID);

-- (4) total per department vs the average of those totals; derived tables instead of WITH
SELECT   dept_name FROM instructor
GROUP BY dept_name
HAVING   SUM(salary) > (SELECT AVG(total) FROM (SELECT SUM(salary) AS total
                                                FROM instructor GROUP BY dept_name));

-- (5)
SELECT   d.dept_name, COUNT(s.ID) AS num_students
FROM     department d LEFT JOIN student s ON s.dept_name = d.dept_name
GROUP BY d.dept_name;

(4) is the textbook's WITH example rewritten: the inner derived table lists each department's total, the scalar subquery averages them, and HAVING compares each department's total with it. On our data the totals are Comp. Sci. 374,000, Physics 182,000, Finance 170,000, History 122,000, Biology 72,000 and Music 40,000; their average is 160,000, so Comp. Sci., Physics and Finance qualify.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 25 marks

Consider the following schema: Flights(flno: integer, from: string, to: string, distance: integer, departs: time, arrives: time, price: real), Aircraft(aid: integer, aname: string, cruisingrange: integer), Certified(eid: integer, aid: integer), Employees(eid: integer, ename: string, salary: integer). Write SQL to implement the following queries:
(a) Find the names of aircraft such that all pilots certified to operate them have salaries more than $80,000.
(b) For each pilot certified for more than three aircraft, find the eid and the maximum cruising range of the aircraft for which she or he is certified.
(c) Find the names of pilots whose salary is less than the price of the cheapest route from 'Los Angeles' to 'Honolulu'.
(d) Find the aids of all aircraft that can be used on routes from 'Los Angeles' to 'Chicago'.
(e) Identify the routes that can be piloted by every pilot who makes more than $100,000.

Show worked answer

Oracle note: FROM and TO are reserved words, so columns with those names would have to be quoted ("FROM") everywhere. We call them from_city and to_city. A pilot is an employee certified for at least one aircraft (Employees also contains other staff).

-- (a) aircraft whose certified pilots all earn more than 80,000 (and that have at least one)
SELECT DISTINCT a.aname
FROM   Aircraft a, Certified c
WHERE  a.aid = c.aid
AND    NOT EXISTS (SELECT * FROM Certified c2, Employees e
                   WHERE  c2.aid = a.aid AND c2.eid = e.eid AND e.salary <= 80000);
--   or: GROUP BY a.aid, a.aname HAVING MIN(e.salary) > 80000 over Aircraft ⋈ Certified ⋈ Employees

-- (b)
SELECT   c.eid, MAX(a.cruisingrange) AS max_range
FROM     Certified c, Aircraft a
WHERE    c.aid = a.aid
GROUP BY c.eid
HAVING   COUNT(*) > 3;

-- (c) pilots only (certified for something)
SELECT DISTINCT e.ename
FROM   Employees e, Certified c
WHERE  e.eid = c.eid
AND    e.salary < (SELECT MIN(f.price) FROM Flights f
                    WHERE  f.from_city = 'Los Angeles' AND f.to_city = 'Honolulu');

-- (d) an aircraft can be used on a route if its range covers the route's distance
SELECT a.aid
FROM   Aircraft a
WHERE  EXISTS (SELECT * FROM Flights f
               WHERE  f.from_city = 'Los Angeles' AND f.to_city = 'Chicago'
               AND    a.cruisingrange >= f.distance);

-- (e) division: no high-paid pilot who cannot fly this route
SELECT f.flno, f.from_city, f.to_city
FROM   Flights f
WHERE  NOT EXISTS (
         SELECT e.eid FROM Employees e, Certified c
         WHERE  e.eid = c.eid AND e.salary > 100000
         MINUS
         SELECT c.eid FROM Certified c, Aircraft a
         WHERE  c.aid = a.aid AND a.cruisingrange >= f.distance);

Notes. (a) Without the join to Certified in the outer query, an aircraft nobody is certified for would satisfy NOT EXISTS vacuously; the join requires at least one pilot. (c) The question bank's answer compares every employee, not only pilots; the cheapest price subquery is the same. (d) "Can be used on routes" is read as "can fly at least one Los Angeles → Chicago flight"; if all such flights must be possible, compare with MAX(distance) instead. The bank's version uses MIN(distance) with >=, which is equivalent to the EXISTS form. (e) A pilot "can pilot" a route if he is certified for some aircraft whose range covers its distance.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2013–14 · 25 marks

Consider the following schema: Patient(ID, FIRSTNAME, SURNAME, ADMISSION_DATE, DR_ID, ward_no), AILMENT(AILMENTID, NAME, PATIENTID), Ward(ward_no, ward_name), Doctor(ID, SURNAME, FIRSTNAME, no_of_patient, AILMENTID). Write SQL to implement the following queries:
(a) List the surnames of all patients of 'Dr. Jones'.
(b) List all the doctors who specialise in the ailment suffered by the patient whose surname is 'Thomas'.
(c) List the ward number and ward name of the ward(s) which have the most patients.
(d) Provide a list of all patients who have not suffered any ailment.
(e) Show the Doctor's name who has the third highest no_of_patient.

Show worked answer

Links: Patient.DR_ID = Doctor.ID, Patient.ward_no = Ward.ward_no, AILMENT.PATIENTID = Patient.ID, Doctor.AILMENTID = AILMENT.AILMENTID (the doctor's speciality).

-- (a)
SELECT p.SURNAME
FROM   Patient p, Doctor d
WHERE  p.DR_ID = d.ID AND d.SURNAME = 'Jones';

-- (b)
SELECT d.FIRSTNAME, d.SURNAME
FROM   Doctor d, AILMENT a, Patient p
WHERE  d.AILMENTID = a.AILMENTID AND a.PATIENTID = p.ID
AND    p.SURNAME = 'Thomas';

-- (c)
SELECT   w.ward_no, w.ward_name
FROM     Ward w, Patient p
WHERE    w.ward_no = p.ward_no
GROUP BY w.ward_no, w.ward_name
HAVING   COUNT(*) >= ALL (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM Patient GROUP BY ward_no);

-- (d)
SELECT p.ID, p.FIRSTNAME, p.SURNAME
FROM   Patient p
WHERE  NOT EXISTS (SELECT * FROM AILMENT a WHERE a.PATIENTID = p.ID);

-- (e) third highest distinct value: exactly 2 larger distinct values exist
SELECT d1.FIRSTNAME, d1.SURNAME
FROM   Doctor d1
WHERE  2 = (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT d2.no_of_patient)
            FROM   Doctor d2
            WHERE  d2.no_of_patient > d1.no_of_patient);

Notes. (a) The doctor's name is stored as surname 'Jones'; 'Dr.' is a title, not data. (c) A Patient row with a NULL ward_no would form a NULL group in the subquery; add WHERE ward_no IS NOT NULL there if that can happen. (e) "Third highest" means the third highest distinct value, so ties at the top do not push it down; every doctor with that value is listed. With analytic functions (beyond the slides, Chapter 7) it is WHERE DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY no_of_patient DESC) = 3 inside a derived table. The bank's alternative ORDER BY no_of_patient DESC OFFSET 2 ROWS FETCH NEXT 1 ROWS ONLY (Oracle 12c+) returns the third row, which is wrong when there are ties. The rest of the bank's answer is correct. The schema has a design oddity: AILMENT has one PATIENTID per AILMENTID, so a doctor's speciality points at one patient's ailment record; we follow the schema as given.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2011–12 · 25 marks

Consider the following relations: Emp(eno, ename, title, city), Proj(pno, pname, budget, city), Works(eno, pno, resp, dur), Pay(title, salary), where Emp.title is a foreign key to Pay.title, Works.eno is a foreign key to Emp.eno and Works.pno is a foreign key to Proj.pno. Write SQL statements to answer the following queries:
(a) For each city, how many projects are located in that city and what is the total budget over all projects in the city?
(b) For each project, what fraction of the budget is spent (in total) on salaries for the people working on that project? Sort your answer by the value of the budget.
(c) Remove the work assignment of all persons to any projects for which more than 2 persons share the same responsibility.
(d) List all projects located in Toronto city and include for each one the number of persons working on the project.
(e) Find the names of projects whose budgets are greater than all projects located in Waterloo city.

Show worked answer
-- (a)
SELECT   city, COUNT(*) AS num_projects, SUM(budget) AS total_budget
FROM     Proj
GROUP BY city;

-- (b) projects without workers spend 0: left joins and NVL
SELECT   p.pno, p.pname, NVL(SUM(y.salary), 0) / p.budget AS fraction
FROM     Proj p LEFT JOIN Works w ON w.pno = p.pno
                LEFT JOIN Emp e   ON e.eno = w.eno
                LEFT JOIN Pay y   ON y.title = e.title
GROUP BY p.pno, p.pname, p.budget
ORDER BY p.budget;

-- (c) delete every assignment to a project in which some responsibility is shared by more than 2 persons
DELETE FROM Works
WHERE  pno IN (SELECT pno FROM Works
               GROUP BY pno, resp
               HAVING COUNT(*) > 2);

-- (d)
SELECT   p.pno, p.pname, COUNT(w.eno) AS num_persons
FROM     Proj p LEFT JOIN Works w ON w.pno = p.pno
WHERE    p.city = 'Toronto'
GROUP BY p.pno, p.pname;

-- (e)
SELECT pname FROM Proj
WHERE  budget > ALL (SELECT budget FROM Proj WHERE city = 'Waterloo');

Notes. (b) Salary depends on the title (Pay); the left joins and NVL give 0 for a project nobody works on (an inner join would omit it). ORDER BY can use p.budget because it is a grouping column. (c) Two readings exist. The sentence says to remove the assignments of all persons to projects in which more than 2 persons share a responsibility, which is the query above. The narrower reading removes only the assignments that have the over-shared responsibility: DELETE FROM Works WHERE (pno, resp) IN (SELECT pno, resp FROM Works GROUP BY pno, resp HAVING COUNT(*) > 2), which is the question bank's answer. State your reading. (e) If no project is in Waterloo, > ALL is TRUE for every project, while > (SELECT MAX(budget) …) returns none. The bank's answers to (a), (d) and (e) are correct; its (b) uses inner joins and so omits projects without workers.

More practice

Six more exercises on these databases, from easy to exam level.

Summary: the same handful of patterns covers every question here: correlated comparison with a group average; INTERSECT, a self-join or EXISTS for "both"; NOT EXISTS (… MINUS …) or a double NOT EXISTS for "all"; GROUP BY with HAVING … >= ALL / <= ALL for extremes with ties; derived tables instead of WITH; LEFT JOIN with COUNT(column) and NVL to keep zero rows; and DELETE … WHERE … IN (grouped subquery). Watch Oracle's reserved words (LEVEL, FROM, TO), and state your reading when a question is ambiguous.
