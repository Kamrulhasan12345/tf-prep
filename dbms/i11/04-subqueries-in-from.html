Subqueries in FROM and SELECT

A query's result is a table, so it can stand wherever a table can: in the FROM clause. Slides 17–22 use this to count computers (PCs or laptops) per maker. This chapter explains derived tables, works the slides' three versions on the textbook data, shows why "think combined" (slide 22) matters, adds scalar subqueries in the SELECT list, and works the 25-mark HR exam question that uses both.

1. Derived tables (slide 17)

Derived table (inline view)
A subquery in the FROM clause. It is evaluated to a temporary table, which the outer query then uses like any stored table: join it, filter it, group it. It can be given an alias after the closing parenthesis (in Oracle without AS), and its columns are named by the subquery's SELECT list (use column aliases for computed columns, e.g. AVG(salary) AS avg_sal).

Slide 17's title says "correlated sub queries in the FROM clause", but the subqueries on slides 19 and 22 are ordinary, uncorrelated ones: they do not refer to the outer query, and a plain derived table is not allowed to. (Oracle 12c added LATERAL and CROSS APPLY for correlated derived tables; they are beyond this course.)

2. Makers of at least two fast computers (slides 18–19)

Find manufacturers of at least two different computers (PCs or laptops) with speeds of at least 2.0. Slide 18 shows parts of Product, PC and Laptop. The difficulty: a maker's fast computers are split over two tables, PC and Laptop, so we cannot simply join and count. Slide 19 first builds one table of all fast computers, then counts per maker:

SELECT maker
FROM  (SELECT model, maker
       FROM   Product NATURAL JOIN PC
       WHERE  speed >= 2
       UNION
       SELECT model, maker
       FROM   Product NATURAL JOIN Laptop
       WHERE  speed >= 2)
GROUP BY maker
HAVING COUNT(model) >= 2;

The derived table has one row (model, maker) per fast PC or laptop. NATURAL JOIN works here because Product and PC share only the column model (and likewise Product and Laptop). The outer query groups it by maker and keeps makers with at least 2 models. Oracle allows a derived table without an alias, as here; you need one only to qualify its columns.

3. Why not count PCs and laptops separately? (slides 20–22)

Slides 20 and 21 answer the narrower questions, with ordinary joins:

-- slide 20: at least two fast PCs                 -- slide 21: at least two fast laptops
SELECT maker                                       SELECT maker
FROM   Product, PC                                 FROM   Product, Laptop
WHERE  Product.model = PC.model                    WHERE  Product.model = Laptop.model
AND    speed >= 2.0                                AND    speed >= 2.0
GROUP BY maker                                     GROUP BY maker
HAVING COUNT(PC.model) >= 2;                       HAVING COUNT(Laptop.model) >= 2;

On the textbook data slide 20 gives A, B, D, E and slide 21 gives A. Their union (A, B, D, E) happens to equal the right answer here, but it is not the right method: a maker with one fast PC and one fast laptop has two fast computers, yet passes neither query. Slide 22 says "think combined": put PCs and laptops into one table first, then count once.

SELECT maker
FROM   Product, (SELECT model, speed FROM PC
                 UNION
                 SELECT model, speed FROM Laptop) T
WHERE  Product.model = T.model
AND    speed >= 2.0
GROUP BY maker
HAVING COUNT(T.model) >= 2;

Here the derived table T is all computers (model, speed), joined with Product like a stored table. The alias T is needed because the outer query writes T.model. On the sample data: A has 5 fast computers, B 3, D 3, E 3, C and G 1 each: answer A, B, D, E. The playground below adds an invented maker X with one fast PC and one fast laptop: X appears in slide 22's answer but in neither slide 20's nor slide 21's.

4. Scalar subqueries in the SELECT list

A scalar subquery (Chapter 1) may also appear in the SELECT list, where it computes one value per output row. Correlated with the outer row, it is a compact way to show a per-row count or total next to each row:

SELECT e1.LASTNAME,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM EMPLOYEE e2 WHERE e2.HIREDATE < e1.HIREDATE) AS hired_before,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM EMPLOYEE e2 WHERE e2.HIREDATE > e1.HIREDATE) AS hired_after
FROM   EMPLOYEE e1;

For each employee e1, the two subqueries count the colleagues hired earlier and later. Employees hired on the same day as e1 count in neither (Higgins and Gietz both started on 7 June 2002). This answers part (i) of the exam question below.

EMPLOYEE (EMPLOYEEID, LASTNAME, FIRSTNAME, PHONE, HIREDATE, JOBID, SALARY, DEPARTMENTID)

JOB_HISTORY (EMPLOYEEID, JOBID, DEPARTMENTID, STARTDATE, ENDDATE)

For the schema above, write SQL to implement the following queries:
(i) For each employee, find the total number of employees that were hired before him/her and those that were hired after him/her. Print employee last name, total employees hired before him/her, and total employees hired after him/her.
(ii) Print the last name of the employees who never changed their job or department.
(iii) Print the last name of the employees and the difference between their salary and the maximum salary of their department, such that the difference is not more than 5000.
(iv) For each department, print the department name, corresponding manager's last name, and the number of employees working under that manager in that department.
(v) Print the last name of those employees who get a higher salary than the average salary of their department. Also print the difference of their salary with the average salary of their department.

Show worked answer

(i) Correlated scalar subqueries in SELECT (section 4):

SELECT e1.LASTNAME,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM EMPLOYEE e2 WHERE e2.HIREDATE < e1.HIREDATE) AS hired_before,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM EMPLOYEE e2 WHERE e2.HIREDATE > e1.HIREDATE) AS hired_after
FROM   EMPLOYEE e1;

(A self-join with GROUP BY and SUM(CASE WHEN e2.HIREDATE < e1.HIREDATE THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) also works.)

(ii) An employee who changed job or department has a row in JOB_HISTORY; we want those without one (anti-join; JOB_HISTORY.EMPLOYEEID is part of its key, so NOT IN is NULL-safe here, and NOT EXISTS works too):

SELECT LASTNAME
FROM   EMPLOYEE
WHERE  EMPLOYEEID NOT IN (SELECT EMPLOYEEID FROM JOB_HISTORY);

(iii) Department maxima in a derived table, joined back to the employees:

SELECT e.LASTNAME, m.max_sal - e.SALARY AS diff
FROM   EMPLOYEE e,
       (SELECT DEPARTMENTID, MAX(SALARY) AS max_sal
        FROM EMPLOYEE GROUP BY DEPARTMENTID) m
WHERE  e.DEPARTMENTID = m.DEPARTMENTID
AND    m.max_sal - e.SALARY <= 5000;

The difference is never negative (nobody earns more than the maximum), so "not more than 5000" is just ≤ 5000; the best-paid employee of each department appears with difference 0. Employees without a department drop out of the join, as they have no department maximum.

(iv) In this schema EMPLOYEE has no manager column, so "working under that manager in that department" means the department's employees other than the manager. Join DEPARTMENT to the manager's EMPLOYEE row for the name, and to the department's employees for the count; outer joins keep departments without a manager or without employees, with a count of 0:

SELECT   d.DEPARTMENTNAME, m.LASTNAME AS manager, COUNT(e.EMPLOYEEID) AS num_emp
FROM     DEPARTMENT d
         LEFT JOIN EMPLOYEE m ON m.EMPLOYEEID = d.MANAGERID
         LEFT JOIN EMPLOYEE e ON e.DEPARTMENTID = d.DEPARTMENTID
                             AND e.EMPLOYEEID <> NVL(d.MANAGERID, -1)
GROUP BY d.DEPARTMENTID, d.DEPARTMENTNAME, m.LASTNAME;

Group by DEPARTMENTID too (names could repeat), and count e.EMPLOYEEID, not *, so an empty department gives 0. If your schema has EMPLOYEE.MANAGERID (Oracle's HR does), you could instead count the manager's direct reports in the department (e.MANAGERID = d.MANAGERID); state the interpretation you use. Note: the question bank's answer counts all employees of the department, including the manager himself; that is defensible only if the manager is considered to work "under" himself.

(v) Department averages in a derived table:

SELECT e.LASTNAME, e.SALARY - a.avg_sal AS diff
FROM   EMPLOYEE e,
       (SELECT DEPARTMENTID, AVG(SALARY) AS avg_sal
        FROM EMPLOYEE GROUP BY DEPARTMENTID) a
WHERE  e.DEPARTMENTID = a.DEPARTMENTID
AND    e.SALARY > a.avg_sal;

The correlated form of Chapter 2 finds the same employees, but the derived table is needed to print the average (or use a scalar subquery in SELECT as well as in WHERE). The bank's answers to (i), (ii), (iii) and (v) are correct. The checks below run on the HR sample data (shared with the rest of these chapters; it has extra columns MANAGERID and COMMISSIONPCT, which the answers do not use).

Summary: a subquery in FROM (derived table, inline view) is a temporary table the outer query can join, filter and group; give it an alias (no AS in Oracle) and name computed columns. It solves "combine first, then aggregate" problems such as counting PCs and laptops together (slide 22), and "compare each row with a group value" problems (salary vs department average). A correlated scalar subquery in SELECT computes one value per output row.
