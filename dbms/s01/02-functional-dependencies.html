Functional Dependencies

A functional dependency says “if two rows agree here, they must agree there”. This chapter defines it, reads the FDs of the slides' Movies1 relation, explains why one table instance can refute an FD but never prove it, and answers slide 14's “Why?”.

1. FDs are constraints (slide 6)

Design theory needs a precise way to say how attributes relate. The functional dependency (FD) is that tool. It lets us analyze a relation schema and improve it. FDs come from the real-world rules the data must obey, so they are database constraints. For the slides' running relation

Movies1(title, year, length, genre, studioName, starName)

the rules are:

  • Two movies with the same title are not produced in the same year (so title and year together identify a movie).
  • A movie has one genre.
  • A movie is made by only one studio.
  • A movie can have multiple stars.

Each rule except the last becomes an FD. The last rule is the absence of an FD, and it matters just as much.

2. Definition (slide 7)

Functional dependency A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ
A statement about relation R: if two tuples of R agree on all of the attributes A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ (have the same values for them), then they must also agree on all of B₁, B₂, …, Bₘ. It is read “A₁A₂…Aₙ functionally determine B₁B₂…Bₘ”. The A's are the left side (determinant), the B's the right side.

Notation: the slides write A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ with the attributes run together, or with braces and commas, {A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ} → {B₁, …, Bₘ}. Both mean the same. With single-letter attributes, AB → C means {A, B} → {C}.

3. When does a relation satisfy an FD? (slide 8)

R satisfies f
Relation schema R satisfies FD f if every instance of R that can legally occur obeys f. An FD is a constraint on the schema, not an observation about one table.

The direction of an FD matters. The slide's example:

  • StudentID → PhoneNumber: a student has only one phone number. Two students may share a number (siblings at home).
  • PhoneNumber → StudentID: a phone number belongs to only one student. A student may have several numbers.

These are different rules about the world, and a designer must decide which is true.

Why “functional”?

A → B behaves like a mathematical function from A-values to B-values: give it the A-value and it returns one B-value. It is not usually a function you can compute by a formula (no formula turns a title and year into a length); it is a function in the sense that the database can store only one answer. If the FD did not hold, one A-value could map to several B-values, and that is not a function.

4. FDs of Movies1 (slides 9–12)

Is this a good design? (slide 10) Seemingly not. It holds information about three kinds of thing (movies, studios, stars) in one relation, and the year, length and genre of Star Wars are stored three times. To judge a design properly we need to know its FDs. Turning the rules into FDs (slide 11):

RuleFD
Two movies with the same title are not produced in the same yeartitle year → length
A movie has one genretitle year → genre
A movie is made by only one studiotitle year → studioName

All three have the same left side because “a movie” means “a (title, year) pair”: the first rule says (title, year) identifies a movie. With commas: title, year → length, and so on.

The combined FD (slide 12). The slides claim title year → length genre studioName. Check it against the definition: if two tuples agree on title and year, they are about the same movie, and one movie has one length, one genre and one studio. So they agree on all three. The claim is true, and it says exactly what the three separate FDs say together (Chapter 4 calls this the combining rule).

5. An FD that does not hold (slide 13)

Does title year → starName hold? Take the first two tuples: both are (Star Wars, 1977), but one has Carrie Fisher and the other Mark Hamill. They agree on the left side and disagree on the right, so the FD is false. A movie has many stars.

Slide 13: “What if your existing instance does not violate the FD?”

Suppose the table held only one tuple per movie, so no two tuples share a title and year. Then no pair of tuples violates title year → starName in that instance. It is still not an FD of Movies1: tomorrow someone may insert a second star for a movie, and that is a perfectly legal tuple. An FD is a rule about every legal instance. One instance can disprove an FD (one violating pair is enough) but can never prove it. FDs come from the meaning of the data.

6. Testing FDs on an instance (slide 14)

Slide 14 gives a relation R(A, B, C, D) and asks which FDs the instance obeys. The method: group the rows by their left-side values, and check that each group has a single right-side value.

Slide 14: “Does A → C hold? Yes. Why?”

Group the rows by A. The two a₁ rows both have C = c₁. The two a₂ rows both have C = c₂. The single a₃ row cannot conflict with anything. No two rows agree on A and differ on C, so this instance satisfies A → C. In contrast, A → B fails (the a₁ rows have b₁ and b₂) and C → A fails (the c₂ rows have a₂ and a₃). Following section 5, “A → C holds” here means “is not contradicted by this instance”; whether it is a real FD of R depends on what A and C mean.
