Third Normal Form and the 3NF Synthesis Algorithm

3NF relaxes BCNF just enough that every relation can be decomposed losslessly and with all dependencies preserved. This chapter defines 3NF and prime attributes (correcting a slip on slide 57), runs the synthesis algorithm on the slides' example with a tool, and works the “3NF but not BCNF” exam questions.

1. Definition (slide 57)

Third normal form (3NF)
A relation R is in 3NF if, whenever A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ is a nontrivial FD, either {A₁, …, Aₙ} is a superkey, or each of the B's that is not among the A's is a member of some key (not necessarily the same key).
Prime attribute
An attribute that is a member of some key (candidate key).

Slide 57 says “a member of a superkey”. That is a slip: every attribute is a member of some superkey (the set of all attributes is a superkey), so every attribute would be prime and every relation would be in 3NF. The textbook's definition says “a member of some key”.

So the 3NF condition reads: for every nontrivial FD, either the left side is a superkey, or the right side consists of prime attributes only. Compare BCNF: the left side must be a superkey, no exceptions. The extra clause “excuses” FDs whose right side is part of a key.

Bookings is in 3NF

Keys {title, city}, {title, theatre}, so all three attributes are prime. theatre → city violates BCNF, but city is prime: allowed in 3NF. title city → theatre has a key on the left. 3NF, not BCNF.

The studio relation is not in 3NF

{title, year, studioName, president, presAddr}, key {title, year}. studioName → president: studioName is not a superkey and president is not prime. Not 3NF (so not BCNF either).

Every BCNF relation is in 3NF (the first clause already holds for every FD), but not the other way round.

2. The 3NF synthesis algorithm (slide 58)

3NF_synthesis(R, F):
  Step 1. Find a minimal basis G of F (Chapter 8).
  Step 2. Merge FDs with the same left side: X → A, X → B, … become X → AB…
  Step 3. For each FD X → A in G, make a relation with schema XA.
  Step 4. If none of these schemas is a superkey of R, add one more relation
          whose schema is a key of R.
  Step 5. If a schema is a subset of another, remove it.
  • Step 3 preserves every FD: each FD of the minimal basis lies entirely inside one relation, so it can be checked there.
  • Step 4 makes the join lossless: a relation containing a key lets the chase reach an all-plain row (book §3.5.3).
  • Each resulting relation is in 3NF (proof in the book, beyond the syllabus). The book's version has three steps: minimal basis, one schema per FD, add a key; merging left sides and dropping subsets are tidying steps that change nothing important.

Slide numbering slip: slide 58's Step 4 says “relation schemas from Step 2”, meaning the schemas made in Step 3; slide 59 then numbers the same steps 1–4 differently (its Step 2 is the algorithm's Step 3 + Step 5, its Step 3 is the algorithm's Step 4).

3. Slide 59's example

R(A, B, C, D, E) with AB → C, C → B, A → D.

  1. Minimal basis. No FD is implied by the other two: (AB)⁺ with {C → B, A → D} is ABD (no C); C⁺ with {AB → C, A → D} is C; A⁺ with {AB → C, C → B} is A. No left side can shrink: A⁺ = AD and B⁺ = B under all three FDs, so neither contains C. (The slide writes “if we remove A1 or B”; it means A or B.)
  2. Schemas: S₁(A, B, C), S₂(B, C), S₃(A, D). S₂ ⊂ S₁, so drop it.
  3. Keys of R: A and E are on no right side; D is only on a right side. (AE)⁺ = ADE. Adding B: (ABE)⁺ = ABCDE. Adding C: (ACE)⁺ = ACE + B (C → B) + D = ABCDE. So the keys are {A, B, E} and {A, C, E} (slide: “verify?”: verified). Neither S₁ nor S₃ contains a key (E is missing), so add S₄(A, B, E).
  4. Final: S₁(A, B, C), S₃(A, D), S₄(A, B, E).

Note that S₁(A, B, C) with AB → C and C → B is in 3NF but not BCNF: C → B has a non-superkey on the left, but B is prime in S₁ (S₁'s keys are AB and AC). The textbook's Exercise 3.5.4 asks to verify losslessness with the chase; enter A B C D E, AB -> C; C -> B; A -> D and ABC; AD; ABE into the chase tool of Chapter 12.

DB QB · 23-24 1c · 9 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-1 | 2023–24 · 9 marks

Discuss an example where a table is in 3NF, but not in BCNF. Then, modify the design to satisfy BCNF.

Show worked answer

Example: Bookings(title, theatre, city) with FDs theatre → city (a theatre is in one city) and title city → theatre (a movie plays in at most one theatre per city).

Keys: {title, city} (determines theatre) and {title, theatre} (determines city). {theatre, city}⁺ = {theatre, city}, so it is not a key. Prime attributes: title, theatre, city (all of them).

  • 3NF: title city → theatre has a key on the left. theatre → city has a non-superkey on the left, but city is prime. So every FD passes: 3NF.
  • Not BCNF: theatre → city has theatre⁺ = {theatre, city} ≠ R on the left.

Sample data shows the redundancy BCNF would remove: (Antz, Guild, Menlo Park), (Shrek, Guild, Menlo Park): the city of Guild is stored once per movie.

BCNF design: split on theatre → city: Theatre(theatre, city) with key theatre, and Showing(theatre, title) with key {theatre, title}. Both are in BCNF and the join is lossless (theatre is the key of Theatre). The price: title city → theatre is no longer enforceable inside one table (Chapter 13); it must be checked with a join, a trigger or an assertion. A designer chooses between the 3NF table (all FDs enforceable, a little redundancy) and the BCNF tables (no redundancy, one FD needs a join).

DB QB · Jan 20 14 · 10 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2018–19 · 10 marks

“A table in 3NF may fail to meet the criteria of BCNF” do you agree? Show an appropriate example to validate your claim.

Show worked answer

Yes. BCNF requires a superkey on the left of every nontrivial FD. 3NF also accepts an FD whose left side is not a superkey, provided every attribute on its right is prime. An FD of that kind is fine in 3NF and breaks BCNF.

Example: Teaching(student, course, teacher). Rules: each teacher teaches one course (teacher → course); for each course a student has one teacher (student course → teacher).

  • Keys: {student, course} ((student, course)⁺ adds teacher) and {student, teacher} (teacher gives course). All three attributes are prime.
  • 3NF: student course → teacher has a key on the left; teacher → course has a prime right side. In 3NF.
  • BCNF: teacher → course, and teacher⁺ = {teacher, course} is not a superkey. Not in BCNF.

The redundancy: “Dr. Rahman teaches DB” is stored once per student he teaches. Decomposing into (teacher, course) and (student, teacher) reaches BCNF but loses student course → teacher.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 10 marks (2013–14: 5 marks, first part only)

Show an example where a table is in 3NF, but not in BCNF. Then modify the design to satisfy BCNF.

Show worked answer

Abstract example: R(A, B, C) with AB → C and C → B.

  • Keys: (AB)⁺ = ABC, so AB is a key; (AC)⁺ = ABC (C → B), so AC is a key. Neither A, B nor C alone is a superkey. Prime attributes: A, B, C.
  • 3NF: AB → C has a key on the left; C → B has a prime right side (B is in the key AB). In 3NF.
  • BCNF: C → B, C⁺ = {B, C} is not a superkey. Not BCNF.

Instance showing the redundancy: (a1, b1, c1), (a2, b1, c1), (a3, b1, c1): the fact c1 → b1 is stored three times.

BCNF design: split on C → B: R₁(C, B) with key C, and R₂(A, C) with key AC. Lossless, since the common attribute C is a key of R₁. Both pieces are in BCNF (two attributes each). AB → C is not preserved: it spans both pieces.

DB QB · 19-20 8b · 10 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2019–20 · 10 marks

What do you know by BCNF and 3NF? What is the major difference between them? Let R be a schema that is not in BCNF and α → β be the functional dependency that causes a violation of BCNF. How can R be decomposed such that R will be in BCNF?

Show worked answer

BCNF: R is in BCNF if for every nontrivial FD α → β that holds on R, α is a superkey of R.

3NF: R is in 3NF if for every nontrivial FD α → β, either α is a superkey, or every attribute of β − α is prime (belongs to some candidate key).

Major difference: 3NF has the extra escape clause “or β − α is prime”, so 3NF allows some redundancy that BCNF forbids (example: Bookings, theatre → city). In exchange, every relation has a 3NF decomposition that is both lossless and dependency preserving (synthesis algorithm), while a BCNF decomposition is always lossless but may lose dependencies. BCNF ⊂ 3NF: every BCNF relation is in 3NF.

Decomposing on the violation α → β: replace R by

  • R₁ = α ∪ β (slides: use α⁺ instead of α ∪ β, which moves everything α determines at once), and
  • R₂ = R − (β − α) (slides: α ∪ (R − α⁺)).

α is in both pieces and is a key of R₁ (α → β), so the decomposition is lossless. Project the FDs onto R₁ and R₂ and, if either still violates BCNF, repeat on it. The process stops because each piece is smaller, and two-attribute relations are always in BCNF. Example: R(A, B, C) with A → B, key AC: α → β = A → B gives R₁(A, B) and R₂(A, C), both BCNF.

Summary: 3NF: every nontrivial FD has a superkey on the left or only prime attributes (members of some key) on the right. It is weaker than BCNF and allows FDs like theatre → city. The synthesis algorithm (minimal basis, one relation per left side, add a key relation if needed, drop subsets) always produces 3NF relations with a lossless join and every FD preserved.
