Workshop: Normalizing Real Tables from Sample Data

Many exam questions hand you a table or a report and ask for the FDs, a dependency diagram, and the 1NF/2NF/3NF/BCNF schemas. This chapter gives a fixed procedure and works four such questions in full: an invoice, a booking table, a restaurant report and a book shop.

1. Why normalize?

Normalization replaces a relation that mixes several kinds of facts by smaller relations that each store one kind of fact once. The payoff is the removal of redundancy and of the update, insertion and deletion anomalies (Chapter 9), done without losing information (lossless join, Chapter 12) and, ideally, while keeping every FD checkable (Chapter 13).

2. The procedure

  1. Name the attributes and find the FDs from the meaning of the data. Use the sample rows only to reject FDs (Chapter 2): two rows that agree on X and differ on Y kill X → Y.
  2. Find the key(s) with closures (Chapter 6). Attributes on no right side are in every key.
  3. Draw the dependency diagram: attributes in a row, key underlined; full dependencies on the key drawn above, partial and transitive dependencies below.
  4. 1NF: make every value atomic. A repeating group (several values per entity) moves to its own relation together with the key of the entity.
  5. 2NF: for each partial dependency (part of a key → non-key attributes), move those attributes out with that part of the key.
  6. 3NF: for each transitive dependency (non-key → non-key), move the dependent attributes out with their determinant.
  7. BCNF: check every remaining FD has a superkey on its left (Chapter 11).

DB QB · 23-24 2a · 15 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-1 | 2023–24 · 15 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2013–14 · 15 marks (same table)

Why is the normalization technique used in database design? Normalize the following table by drawing dependency diagram and identifying all dependencies to derive up to 3NF. Show the schema in 3NF.

The 2013–14 version shows the same invoice table in row form (with GH-7781 / ToughGood in the last row); the answer is the same.

Show worked answer

Why normalize: to remove redundancy and with it the update, insertion and deletion anomalies. In this table, “Rotary sander, vendor 211, NeverFail, $49.95” is stored once per sale of that product; changing the vendor's name means updating many rows (update anomaly); a product cannot be recorded until it is sold (insertion anomaly); deleting invoice 211349 deletes everything known about the power drill and vendor 157 (deletion anomaly).

Dependencies. Each row is one product line of one invoice.

  • INV_NUM → SALE_DATE (an invoice has one date; 211347's three rows agree)
  • PROD_NUM → PROD_LABEL, VEND_CODE, PROD_PRICE (AA-E3422QW has the same label, vendor and price on both invoices)
  • VEND_CODE → VEND_NAME
  • INV_NUM, PROD_NUM → QUANT_SOLD

Key: INV_NUM and PROD_NUM appear on no right side and together determine everything: {INV_NUM, PROD_NUM}.

1NF: the table (one row per invoice line) has atomic values and the key {INV_NUM, PROD_NUM}: INVOICE_LINE(INV_NUM, PROD_NUM, SALE_DATE, PROD_LABEL, VEND_CODE, VEND_NAME, QUANT_SOLD, PROD_PRICE).

2NF: remove the partial dependencies on INV_NUM and on PROD_NUM:

  • INVOICE(INV_NUM, SALE_DATE)
  • PRODUCT(PROD_NUM, PROD_LABEL, VEND_CODE, VEND_NAME, PROD_PRICE)
  • LINE(INV_NUM, PROD_NUM, QUANT_SOLD)

3NF: PRODUCT still has the transitive dependency PROD_NUM → VEND_CODE → VEND_NAME. Split it:

  • INVOICE(INV_NUM, SALE_DATE)
  • PRODUCT(PROD_NUM, PROD_LABEL, VEND_CODE, PROD_PRICE), VEND_CODE references VENDOR
  • VENDOR(VEND_CODE, VEND_NAME)
  • LINE(INV_NUM, PROD_NUM, QUANT_SOLD), INV_NUM and PROD_NUM reference INVOICE and PRODUCT

Each relation's only FD has its key on the left, so these are also in BCNF; the decomposition is lossless and preserves every FD (checked with the chase and preservation tools). Design note: if a product's price can change over time, the price charged belongs to the sale, so PROD_PRICE (or a SALE_PRICE) would move to LINE.

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2014–15 · 13 marks

Normalize the following booking table up to 3NF by drawing a dependency diagram and identifying all dependencies. Show the resulting 3NF schema.

Show worked answer

Reading the data. Each row is a customer's booking for an event session; Total is the number of people in the booking; Person is the staff member running the event; Price is the event's price.

  • Customer → Contact (each customer has one number)
  • Event → Person, Price (Lunch is always Dora Smith at 60; Master Class 120; Dinner 150)
  • Date, Time, Customer → Event, Total (a customer has one booking per session)

Rejected by the data: Date Time → Event (12 Aug, 12.30 PM has Lunch and Master Class), Event → Total. Key: Date, Time and Customer are on no right side and together determine everything: {Date, Time, Customer}. (Assumption: Person depends on the event alone, as in every row of the sample; if the host could change per day it would be Date, Time, Event → Person, and the same steps apply.)

1NF: already flat, key {Date, Time, Customer}.

2NF: remove the partial dependency Customer → Contact: CUSTOMER(Customer, Contact), BOOKING(Date, Time, Customer, Event, Person, Total, Price).

3NF: remove the transitive dependency Event → Person, Price from BOOKING:

  • CUSTOMER(Customer, Contact)
  • EVENT(Event, Person, Price)
  • BOOKING(Date, Time, Customer, Event, Total)

All three are in BCNF, the join is lossless and every FD is preserved.

DB QB · 22-23 7a · 20 marks

You have been tasked to prepare a database for a restaurant. The restaurant has provided the following report for you. In the above table, “Qnty” means “quantity of the item”. Now, create a relational schema, find its functional dependencies and normalize the schema. Write down all normalized forms up to BCNF (1NF, 2NF, 3NF, BCNF).

Show worked answer

Attributes. FoodItem, MarketedPrice; for packages: SimpleItem, ContQty (how many of the simple item the package contains); for simple items: Ingredient, UnitCost, IngQty (amount of the ingredient used); for sales: Buyer, SaleDate, SaleQty.

Unnormalized form. The report has one entry per food item with three repeating groups:

FOOD(FoodItem, MarketedPrice, {SimpleItem, ContQty}, {Ingredient, UnitCost, IngQty}, {Buyer, SaleDate, SaleQty})

FDs.

  • FoodItem → MarketedPrice
  • FoodItem, SimpleItem → ContQty
  • Ingredient → UnitCost (an ingredient has one unit cost whatever it is used in)
  • FoodItem, Ingredient → IngQty
  • FoodItem, Buyer, SaleDate → SaleQty (assuming a buyer's purchases of an item on one date are recorded as one line; C1's two burger purchases are on different dates)

1NF. Each repeating group moves to its own relation with the key FoodItem. (Flattening everything into one table would be wrong: the three groups are independent of each other, so every simple item would have to be paired with every ingredient and every sale, the multivalued-dependency redundancy of Chapter 15.)

  • FOOD(FoodItem, MarketedPrice)
  • CONTAINS(FoodItem, SimpleItem, ContQty)
  • RECIPE(FoodItem, Ingredient, UnitCost, IngQty)
  • SALE(FoodItem, Buyer, SaleDate, SaleQty)

2NF. RECIPE has a partial dependency Ingredient → UnitCost (Ingredient is part of its key): the unit cost of Egg would repeat for every dish using egg. Split:

  • INGREDIENT(Ingredient, UnitCost)
  • RECIPE(FoodItem, Ingredient, IngQty)

FOOD, CONTAINS and SALE have no partial dependency.

3NF. No relation has a non-key attribute determining another non-key attribute, so the 2NF schema is already in 3NF.

BCNF. Each relation's only nontrivial FD has the relation's key on its left (FoodItem → MarketedPrice; FoodItem SimpleItem → ContQty; Ingredient → UnitCost; FoodItem Ingredient → IngQty; FoodItem Buyer SaleDate → SaleQty). Final BCNF schema:

RelationKeySample rows
FOOD(FoodItem, MarketedPrice)FoodItem(Chicken Burger, 200), (Fried Rice, 80), (Set Menu 1, 500)
CONTAINS(FoodItem, SimpleItem, ContQty)FoodItem, SimpleItem(Set Menu 1, Fried Rice, 1), (Set Menu 1, Vegetable, 1), (Set Menu 1, Chicken Steak, 1)
INGREDIENT(Ingredient, UnitCost)Ingredient(Chicken Patty, 60), (Lettuce, 30), …, (Oil, 500)
RECIPE(FoodItem, Ingredient, IngQty)FoodItem, Ingredient(Chicken Burger, Pickle, 4), (Fried Rice, Egg, 1), …
SALE(FoodItem, Buyer, SaleDate, SaleQty)FoodItem, Buyer, SaleDate(Chicken Burger, C1, 01/02/24, 2), (Set Menu 1, C1, 05/02/24, 2), …

CONTAINS.SimpleItem is a foreign key to FOOD (Fried Rice is itself a food item; Vegetable and Chicken Steak would be added as food items too). If one buyer could buy the same item twice on one day, add a sale number to SALE's key.

DB QB · 21 CT2 1 · 14 marks

You have been provided the following unnormalized schema for a Book Shop. Sales (Transaction Time, Transaction Type, Transaction Amount, Username, Order Number, Order Date, Book Name, Book Edition, Book ISBN, Book Price, Order Count, Author Name, Author Rating). The dependencies are listed below. Transaction Time, Username, Order Number → Transaction Amount; Transaction Time → Transaction Type; Username, Order Number → Order Date; Username, Order Number, Book Name → Order Count; Book Name → Book Price, Author Name; Book ISBN → Book Name, Book Edition; Author Name → Author Rating. Now, normalize the schema to BCNF. Mention the 2NF and 3NF that you create in the process.

Show worked answer

Abbreviations: TT = Transaction Time, TType, TAmt, U = Username, ON = Order Number, OD = Order Date, BN = Book Name, BE = Book Edition, ISBN, BP = Book Price, OC = Order Count, AN = Author Name, AR = Author Rating.

Key. TT, U, ON and ISBN appear on no right side, so they are in every key. {TT, U, ON, ISBN}⁺: TT → TType; TT U ON → TAmt; U ON → OD; ISBN → BN, BE; BN → BP, AN; AN → AR; U ON BN → OC. That is everything, so the only key is {TT, U, ON, ISBN}.

2NF (remove partial dependencies: parts of the key determining non-key attributes):

  • TT → TType ⇒ R1(TT, TType)
  • U ON → OD ⇒ R2(U, ON, OD)
  • TT U ON → TAmt ⇒ R3(TT, U, ON, TAmt)
  • ISBN → BN BE BP AN AR (BP, AN, AR via BN) ⇒ R4(ISBN, BN, BE, BP, AN, AR)
  • U ON ISBN → OC, but the real FD is U ON BN → OC; keep it with its own determinant ⇒ R5(U, ON, BN, OC)
  • what remains is the key itself ⇒ R6(TT, U, ON, ISBN), which links transactions, orders and books

R4 is in 2NF (single-attribute key) but not 3NF.

3NF (remove transitive dependencies in R4: ISBN → BN → BP, AN and AN → AR):

  • R4a(ISBN, BN, BE)
  • R4b(BN, BP, AN)
  • R4c(AN, AR)

with R1, R2, R3, R5, R6 unchanged. (This is exactly what the 3NF synthesis algorithm of Chapter 14 produces from these FDs, including R6 as the added key relation.)

BCNF. Checked relation by relation with projected FDs: R1 (TT → TType), R2 (U ON → OD), R3 (TT U ON → TAmt), R4a (ISBN → BN BE), R4b (BN → BP AN), R4c (AN → AR), R5 (U ON BN → OC): every left side is the relation's key; R6 has no nontrivial FD. All eight relations are in BCNF, the join is lossless (chase) and all seven FDs are preserved, since each lies inside one relation.

Running the BCNF analysis algorithm directly on Sales (Tool 4 of Chapter 11) also reaches BCNF, but depending on the order of splits it can end with (U, ON, ISBN, OC) in place of (U, ON, BN, OC) and lose U ON BN → OC. The synthesis route above avoids that.

Summary: to normalize a sample table, derive FDs from meaning (use the rows only to reject candidates), find the key with closures, and draw the dependency diagram. Then remove repeating groups (1NF), partial dependencies (2NF) and transitive dependencies (3NF), each time moving the dependent attributes out with their determinant, and finish by checking every left side is a key (BCNF).
