Anomalies, and Removing Them by Decomposition

Redundancy is not just wasted space: it makes updates, deletions and insertions go wrong. This chapter animates each anomaly on the Movies1 table, then decomposes Movies1 into Movies2 and Movies3 and shows which anomalies disappear.

1. Four problems with Movies1 (slides 37–39)

The textbook calls the problems that arise from cramming too much into one relation anomalies. Using Movies1 (title, year, length, genre, studioName, starName), with key {title, year, starName}:

Redundancy
Information is repeated unnecessarily in several tuples. The length and genre of Star Wars (124, SciFi) are stored three times, once per star.
Update anomaly
We change information in one tuple but forget the other copies, leaving the database inconsistent.
Deletion anomaly
When a set of values becomes empty, we lose other information as a side effect.
Insertion anomaly
We cannot insert a fact without also supplying unrelated (or unknown) information.

Insertion anomaly (slide 39)

Two cases on slide 39. A new movie with no stars yet cannot be inserted: starName is part of the key, and a key attribute cannot be null, so there is no legal tuple to store. Adding a star to an existing movie forces us to repeat the movie's year, length, genre and studio, which is more redundancy and another chance to type a value inconsistently.

2. Decomposition (slide 40)

The standard cure is to decompose the relation. Given R(A₁, …, Aₙ), decompose it into S(B₁, …, Bₘ) and T(C₁, …, Cₖ) where

  1. {A₁, …, Aₙ} = {B₁, …, Bₘ} ∪ {C₁, …, Cₖ} (every attribute is kept somewhere; the two sets may overlap),
  2. S = πB₁,…,Bₘ(R): the tuples of S are the projections of R's tuples onto the B's,
  3. T = πC₁,…,Cₖ(R).

Replacing a relation that is not in “good form” by smaller relations, each in an appropriate normal form (BCNF, 3NF: Chapters 11 and 14), is called normalization. The decomposition must be lossless (Chapter 1, Chapter 12).

3. Movies1 → Movies2 + Movies3 (slides 41–42)

Movies2 has every attribute except starName; Movies3 has title, year and starName.

What this fixes (slide 42):

  • Redundancy of length, genre (and studio) is gone: each movie's facts appear once, in Movies2.
  • Update anomaly resolved: changing the length of Star Wars touches one tuple.
  • Deletion anomaly resolved: deleting Vivien Leigh from Movies3 removes a star, while Gone With the Wind stays in Movies2.
  • Insertion anomaly resolved too: a movie without stars is a Movies2 tuple with no Movies3 tuples.

Slide 42: “update anomaly is still present in Movies3 for year update”?

Title and year still repeat in Movies3 (Star Wars, 1977 appears three times). The slide says we cannot remove the year from Movies3 because {title, year} is what identifies a movie: it is the link to Movies2. The textbook goes further: this is not really an anomaly. If someone changes the year in one Star Wars tuple of Movies3 to 2008, the result is a statement that Carrie Fisher starred in a (different) Star Wars made in 2008, which is legal. No FD says the three Star Wars tuples must share a year. Repeating a key to link tables is unavoidable and harmless. Movies2 and Movies3 are both in BCNF (Chapter 11).

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2012–13 · 10 marks (same question 2013–14)

What are the three anomalies in relational database schemas? Discuss them.

Show worked answer

Anomalies arise when a relation stores facts about several things together, so some facts are repeated. Example: Movies1(title, year, length, genre, studioName, starName) with key {title, year, starName}; the length and genre of a movie are repeated once per star.

  1. Update anomaly. A fact stored in several tuples must be changed in all of them. If Star Wars is found to be 125 minutes and only the first of its three tuples is updated, the database says Star Wars has two different lengths.
  2. Deletion anomaly. Deleting one fact deletes others with it. Removing Vivien Leigh from Gone With the Wind deletes the only tuple for that movie, so its year, length, genre and studio vanish.
  3. Insertion anomaly. A fact cannot be stored without unrelated facts. A new movie with no stars cannot be inserted, since starName is part of the key and cannot be null; adding a star to a movie forces re-entering the movie's details.

All three are caused by redundancy and are removed by decomposing into relations in BCNF (or 3NF): Movies2(title, year, length, genre, studioName) and Movies3(title, year, starName).

BUET QBank · CSE 303 | L-3/T-1 | 2011–12 · 10 marks

What are redundancy, deletion and update anomalies?

Show worked answer

Take teacher_in_dept(ID, name, salary, dept_name, building, budget) from Chapter 1, where dept_name → building budget.

Redundancy: the same fact stored in several tuples. CSE's building (ECE Building) and budget (5,000,000) appear in the row of every CSE teacher.

Update anomaly: because of the copies, changing the fact requires changing every copy. If CSE's budget is raised and only Rahim Ahmed's row is updated, Tanvir Islam's row still shows the old budget, and the database contradicts itself.

Deletion anomaly: deleting a tuple to remove one fact also removes another. If Ayesha Khan, the only EEE teacher, leaves and her row is deleted, the database no longer knows that EEE is in Main Building with budget 4,000,000.

Fix: decompose into teacher(ID, name, salary, dept_name) and department(dept_name, building, budget). Each department fact is stored once, and departments exist independently of teachers.

Summary: redundancy causes update anomalies (copies disagree after a partial update), deletion anomalies (deleting one fact loses another) and insertion anomalies (a fact cannot be stored without unrelated data). Decomposing Movies1 into Movies2(title, year, length, genre, studioName) and Movies3(title, year, starName) stores each movie fact once and removes all three. The repeated title and year in Movies3 are the link between the tables and are not an anomaly.
