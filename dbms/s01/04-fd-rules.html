Reasoning About FDs: Transitivity, Splitting, Combining, Trivial FDs

Some FDs follow from others. This chapter proves the transitive rule with two tuples, shows why right sides can be split and combined while left sides cannot (with a counterexample table), and defines trivial FDs.

1. Why reason about FDs?

A designer writes down some FDs, but the relation then satisfies many more FDs that follow from them. We need those hidden FDs to find keys and to check normal forms. The textbook says an FD f follows from a set S of FDs if every relation instance that satisfies all the FDs in S also satisfies f. Each rule in this chapter is a shortcut for proving that.

2. The transitive rule (slide 20)

If A → B and B → C hold in R(A, B, C), then A → C holds.

Proof (slide 20). Take any two tuples that agree on A, say (a, b₁, c₁) and (a, b₂, c₂). By A → B they agree on B, so b₁ = b₂. Now they agree on B, so by B → C they agree on C: c₁ = c₂. We started from “agree on A” and ended at “agree on C”. Since the two tuples were arbitrary, A → C holds.

The general form (book §3.2.6): if A₁…Aₙ → B₁…Bₘ and B₁…Bₘ → C₁…Cₖ, then A₁…Aₙ → C₁…Cₖ. The textbook's example: a relation about movies and their studios has title year → studioName (a movie has one studio) and studioName → studioAddr (a studio has one address). By transitivity, title year → studioAddr: a movie determines the address of its studio.

3. Splitting and combining right sides (slides 21–22)

Splitting rule
A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ can be replaced by the m FDs A₁A₂…Aₙ → Bᵢ, for i = 1, 2, …, m.
Combining rule
The m FDs A₁A₂…Aₙ → Bᵢ (i = 1, …, m) can be replaced by the single FD A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ.

Both directions are safe, so the two sets of FDs are equivalent: any instance satisfying one set satisfies the other. Reason: “agree on the A's implies agree on B₁ and on B₂ and … on Bₘ” says exactly the same as “agree on the A's implies agree on all the B's”. Slide 22's example:

Left sides cannot be split

The slide warns: title year → length is not equivalent to the pair title → length and year → length. The left side is a combination: only the pair (title, year) identifies a movie. A legal instance shows the difference:

Rows 1 and 2 share the title King Kong but have different lengths, so title → length fails. Rows 3 and 4 share the year 1977 but have different lengths, so year → length fails. Yet no two rows share both title and year, so title year → length holds. The FD with the combined left side is weaker (easier to satisfy) than each split piece.

4. Trivial FDs (slide 23)

Trivial FD
A₁A₂…Aₙ → B₁B₂…Bₘ is trivial if {B₁, …, Bₘ} ⊆ {A₁, …, Aₙ}: the right side is a subset of the left side. Examples: title year → title, title year → year.
Nontrivial / completely nontrivial
Nontrivial: at least one B is not among the A's. Completely nontrivial (book): none of the B's is among the A's.

A trivial FD holds in every relation: if two tuples agree on all the A's, they certainly agree on any subset of the A's. It says nothing about the data, so normal-form tests ignore trivial FDs.

Trivial dependency rule. A₁…Aₙ → B₁…Bₘ is equivalent to A₁…Aₙ → C₁…Cₖ, where the C's are the B's that are not among the A's. We may drop from the right side anything that is already on the left. For example, title year → year length is equivalent to title year → length.

Key terms

Quick check

Summary: FDs imply other FDs. Transitivity: X → Y and Y → Z give X → Z (two tuples that agree on X agree on Y, hence on Z). The right side of an FD may be split into single attributes or combined freely; the left side may not, because a combination of attributes can be unique when no single attribute is. A trivial FD has its right side inside its left side; it always holds, and right-side attributes that also appear on the left can be dropped.
