Deadlock Prevention by Timestamps: Wait-Die and Wound-Wait

Instead of maintaining a waits-for graph, give each transaction an age and let age decide who may wait for whom. This chapter defines deadlock timestamps, the wait-die and wound-wait rules, animates both slide examples, proves why neither can deadlock, and provides a side-by-side simulator for exam questions.

1. Why timestamps (slide 25)

Preventing deadlocks with the waits-for graph is time-consuming: every time a lock request must wait, the scheduler has to search a possibly large graph for a cycle. A cheaper alternative is to compare just two numbers whenever a transaction must wait.

Deadlock timestamp
A number given to each transaction when it starts, increasing over time. A smaller timestamp means an older transaction. It is used only for deadlock handling.

Two important details from the slide:

  • It is different from the timestamp used for timestamp-based concurrency control (the RT/WT scheme of the previous lecture), even if that scheme is also in use.
  • If a transaction is rolled back and restarts because of deadlock handling, it keeps its old deadlock timestamp. (A concurrency-control timestamp, by contrast, is renewed on restart.) Keeping the old timestamp is what prevents starvation (Chapter 8).

2. The two schemes (slide 26)

Both rules apply at the moment a transaction T requests a lock and would have to wait for a lock held by another transaction U. (The slide says the policies "detect" deadlocks; strictly they prevent them, since a cycle never forms. It also writes "would-wait" for wound-wait.)

T requests a lock held by UWait-dieWound-wait
T is older (TS(T) < TS(U))T waits for UT wounds U: U is rolled back, its locks are released and granted to T
T is younger (TS(T) > TS(U))T dies: T is rolled backT waits for U

Memory aid, always from the point of view of the requester: in wait-die "old waits, young dies"; in wound-wait "old wounds, young waits". In both, the older transaction always wins; the schemes differ in whether the loser is the young requester (wait-die) or the young holder (wound-wait). If U holds a conflicting lock together with others, the rule is applied against each of them: in wait-die T may wait only if it is older than all of them; in wound-wait T wounds every younger holder and waits for any older ones.

One exception from the textbook: a wound "usually is fatal", but if by the time the wound takes effect U has already finished and released its locks, U survives and is not rolled back.

3. Example under wait-die (slide 27, textbook Fig. 19.9)

The transactions are the reordered ones from Chapter 6. Timestamps follow the numbering: T1 is the oldest, T4 the youngest. Rolled-back transactions restart later with their old timestamps (here T4 happens to restart first, at row 9).

The slide's highlights: step 2, T2 asks for A, held by the older T1, so T2 dies. Step 4, T4 asks for A held by T1, and dies for the same reason. T3 and then T1 complete. Step 10, the restarted T2 asks for A, now held by the restarted T4. T2 is older than T4 (timestamps never change), so this time T2 waits instead of dying. T4 finishes and T2 runs to completion.

4. Example under wound-wait (slide 28, textbook Fig. 19.10)

The slide's highlights: step 2, T2 asks for A held by the older T1, so the younger requester waits. Step 4, T4 also waits for T1. Step 5, T1 asks for B, held by the younger T3; T3 has not finished, so the wound is fatal: T3 is rolled back and B goes to T1. T1 completes, the lock on A goes to T2, then to T4, and finally T3 restarts and completes without interference.

5. Why neither scheme can deadlock (slide 29)

A deadlock needs a cycle in the waits-for graph. Look at which edges each scheme allows:

  • Wait-die: a transaction may wait only for a younger one. Every edge T → U has TS(T) < TS(U).
  • Wound-wait: a transaction may wait only for an older one. Every edge T → U has TS(T) > TS(U).

So if T1 waits for T2, then T2 can never wait for T1. More generally (the textbook's argument): suppose there were a cycle, and let T be the oldest transaction on it. In wait-die, T's predecessor on the cycle waits for T, which is older than it: forbidden. In wound-wait, T itself waits for its successor, which is younger: forbidden. Either way the cycle cannot exist. Timestamps strictly increase (or strictly decrease) along every path, and a path that returns to its start would need a timestamp smaller than itself.

6. Interactive simulator: wait-die and wound-wait side by side

Enter lock requests in the same format as the waits-for tool (l1(A), sl1(A), xl1(A), u1(A), c1, or bare r1(A)/w1(A) which take shared/exclusive locks automatically) and, optionally, timestamps such as T1=100, T2=150 (otherwise Ti gets timestamp i). Exam questions usually say rolled-back transactions do not restart, which is the default; tick the box to let a rolled-back transaction restart (with its old timestamp) when its next action appears. Actions of a waiting transaction are delayed until it gets its lock.

DB QB · 22-23 3d · 10 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-1 | 2022–23 · 10 marks

Consider the following schedule of actions involving four transactions T1, T2, T3 and T4:

r1(A); r2(B); w1(C); r3(D); w3(B); w2(C); w4(A); w1(D); w1(B);

Assume that the deadlock-timestamps of T1, T2, T3 and T4 are 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively. For each action of the given schedule, determine what happens ("wound" or "wait") under the wound-wait deadlock avoidance system. If the action is a "wound", then don't restart the transaction.

Result: T1 continues holding A (S), C, D, B (X); T4 waits for A (on T1); T2 and T3 are rolled back and not restarted. Compare Chapter 5: with plain waiting the same actions deadlock (T1 → T3 → T2 → T1), while wound-wait never lets the cycle form. The question bank's answer agrees.

Summary: each transaction gets a deadlock timestamp (smaller = older) that it keeps across restarts. When T must wait for U: under wait-die, T waits if older and dies if younger; under wound-wait, T wounds (rolls back) U if T is older and waits if younger. Waits then only go from older to younger (wait-die) or younger to older (wound-wait), so the waits-for graph can never have a cycle and no deadlock forms.
