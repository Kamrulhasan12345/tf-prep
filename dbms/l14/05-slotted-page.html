Slotted Page Structure

Chapter 4 showed how to lay out one variable-length record. This chapter shows how to fit many of them into one block, so they can be found, grown, shrunk and deleted. Includes the step-by-step example and Question w2-3.

1. The problem

With fixed-length records, slot i of a block was always at the same place. With variable-length records that no longer works. Records have different sizes, so you cannot compute where the 3rd record starts. A deleted record leaves a hole of some odd size that the next record may not fit into. And a record that grows (a longer name after an UPDATE) may no longer fit where it was.

The standard solution is the slotted page structure. A page is the same thing as a block: the unit that is read from disk in one go.

2. How a slotted page is organised

The block is divided into three regions:

  1. A block header at the start of the block.
  2. The records, packed together at the end of the block.
  3. Free space in the middle.

The header grows forward (left to right) as entries are added, and the records grow backward (right to left) as records are added. The free space shrinks from both sides. The block is full when the two meet.

The slide lists what the header contains:

Number of record entries
How many slots the header currently has.
End of free space
The address where the free space stops and the records begin. A new record is placed just before this address.
Location and size of each record
One entry (a slot) per record, saying where the record is in the block and how many bytes it uses.

Two more rules from the slide make this work:

  • Records can be moved around within the page to keep them contiguous (packed together with no empty space between them). When a record moves, only its header entry has to be updated.
  • Pointers should not point directly to a record. They should point to the record's entry in the header. An index, for example, stores “block 7, slot 2” instead of “block 7, byte 3596”. Then records can move freely inside the block without breaking anything outside it. Only the slot entry changes.

That second rule is the key idea. It is the same trick as a building directory: people remember “Room 2”, and if Room 2 moves to another floor, only the directory sign changes.

3. Worked example (slides 15–16)

Three relations are given:

Student (id, NID, name, f-name, f-NID, m-name, m-NID, DOB, cgpa, tot-cred, uni-id,
         uni-name, uni-street, uni-city, house-no, street, city, d-no, d-name, building)
Takes   (id, course-no, semester, year, grade)
Course  (course-no, title, credit, pre-req)

A student record is 400 bytes, a takes record 100 bytes and a course record 80 bytes. The block is 4 KB. The task: show the slotted page after inserting one record of each, in the order student, takes, course.

Two details about the slide's numbers. The slide text says “4000 byte” but its pictures use 4096 (4 KB = 4 × 1024 = 4096 bytes), so this page uses 4096. Also, in the slide's pictures the location stored for each record is the address where that record ends (just after its last byte), and the record occupies the size bytes before that. This chapter follows the same convention so that the numbers match the slides.

Step 1: insert the student record (400 bytes)

The block is empty, so free space ends at 4096. The record is placed in the last 400 bytes: 4096 − 400 = 3696, so it occupies bytes 3696–4095. The header is updated: 1 entry, end of free space = 3696, and entry 1 = (size 400, location 4096).

Step 2: insert the takes record (100 bytes)

The new record goes right before the current end of free space: 3696 − 100 = 3596, so it occupies bytes 3596–3695. The header now has 2 entries, end of free space = 3596, and entry 2 = (100, 3696).

Question w2-3 (worked answer)

(a) Insert the course record (80 bytes). Free space ends at 3596, so the record goes at 3596 − 80 = 3516 and occupies bytes 3516–3595. The header becomes: 3 entries, end of free space = 3516, entries (400, 4096), (100, 3696), (80, 3596).

(b) Deleting a record, three cases. In every case the deleted record's slot is marked as empty (for example its size is set to −1) instead of being removed, because something outside the block may still refer to the slot numbers of the other records. Then the records are kept contiguous:

Case 1: delete the last record (course, the record nearest the free space). Nothing has to move. Its bytes 3516–3595 simply become free space, slot 3 is marked empty, and the end of free space moves back to 3596.

Case 2: delete the first record (student, bytes 3696–4095). This leaves a 400-byte hole at the very end of the block, with takes and course in front of it. To close the hole, every record between the free space and the hole is shifted 400 bytes toward the end: takes moves to 3996–4095 and course to 3916–3995. Their slot locations become 4096 and 3996. Slot 1 is marked empty, and the end of free space becomes 3916.

Case 3: delete a record in between (takes, bytes 3596–3695). Only the records between the free space and the hole move, here just course. It shifts 100 bytes toward the end, to 3616–3695, and its slot location becomes 3696. Student does not move. Slot 2 is marked empty, and the end of free space becomes 3616.

The general rule: when a record of size s is deleted, every record stored between the free space and the hole moves s bytes toward the end of the block, their slot entries are updated, and the end of free space increases by s. Anything outside the block keeps pointing at slot numbers, which have not changed, so nothing outside needs updating.

4. Try it yourself: slotted page simulator

Exams ask for this procedure in many variations: different block sizes, different record sizes, different deletion orders. Type a block size and a list of operations, press Run, and step through the result. Write insert NAME SIZE to add a record and delete K to delete the record in slot K, separated by commas. Like the slides, the simulator ignores the bytes the header itself uses, stores each record's end address as its location, and reuses an empty slot for the next insert.

The following table stores information about courses offered by BUET.

DB QB · 20-21 1b · 15 marks  |  BUET QBank · CSE 215 | L-2/T-2 | 2020–21 · 15 marks

Show and explain the slotted-page structure by inserting two variable length records with size S1 and S2. The block size is 4000 bytes. Show the structure after deletion of the record of size S1.

Show worked answer

Structure. The block header holds the number of entries, the end of free space, and one (size, location) slot per record. Records are stored from the end of the block toward the front, and free space sits in between (Section 2).

Insert S1. Free space ends at 4000, so record 1 occupies [4000 − S1, 4000). Header: 1 entry, end of free space = 4000 − S1, slot 1 = (S1, 4000).

Insert S2. Record 2 occupies [4000 − S1 − S2, 4000 − S1). Header: 2 entries, end of free space = 4000 − S1 − S2, slot 2 = (S2, 4000 − S1).

Delete S1. Slot 1 is marked empty (size −1) and kept, because pointers to slot 2 must keep working. Record 1 was the first record, so its removal leaves a hole of S1 bytes at the end of the block. Record 2 is moved S1 bytes toward the end, to [4000 − S2, 4000), and slot 2 becomes (S2, 4000). End of free space = 4000 − S2.

DB QB · 18 CT3 3 · 6 marks

The sizes of tuples of relations r1, r2 and r3 are 1000, 500 and 1500 respectively. The three insert statements: insert into r2 values (…), insert into r3 values (…) and insert into r1 values (…) are executed consecutively. The block size is 4KB (4096 Byte). Show the slotted page block structures after every insertion.

Show worked answer

The order of insertion is r2, r3, r1.

  1. Insert r2 (500 B): 4096 − 500 = 3596, bytes 3596–4095. Header: 1 entry, end of free space 3596, slot 1 = (500, 4096).
  2. Insert r3 (1500 B): 3596 − 1500 = 2096, bytes 2096–3595. Header: 2 entries, end of free space 2096, slot 2 = (1500, 3596).
  3. Insert r1 (1000 B): 2096 − 1000 = 1096, bytes 1096–2095. Header: 3 entries, end of free space 1096, slot 3 = (1000, 2096).

Summary: a slotted page has a header (number of entries, end of free space, and one (size, location) slot per record) at the front, records packed at the back, and free space in between. Records can move inside the block to stay contiguous. Only their slot entries change, because outside pointers refer to slots, not to byte addresses.
