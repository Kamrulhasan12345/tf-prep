Binomial Heap Operations

Asked in 2023–24 Q13(c) (union of 6 and 7 nodes, 10 marks), 2022–23 Q8 (23 marks: build, meld, decrease, credits; plus 12 marks for the amortized-insert proof), 2021–22 Q5 (min to max conversion 15, then 5+5+5+5), 2019–20 Q2(b) (delete a node, 17 marks), 2018–19 Q6(c) and 2019–20 Q4(a) (running times), and CT-4 (insert 3, 13, 7, 15, 21, 24, 10). Every one of these is "draw the heap after each step".

1. The structure

  • A binomial heap is a list of binomial trees. Each tree is heap-ordered: a min-heap has the smallest key at the root of every subtree.
  • At most one tree of each order. {B3, B1, B0} is legal; {B3, B1, B1, B0} is not.
  • Roots sit in a singly linked root list, in increasing order of degree. The orders present are the 1-bits of n (chapter 2): 19 nodes = 10011 = B4, B1, B0.
  • The minimum is one of the roots. Find-min scans the root list: O(log n), because there are at most \(\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor + 1\) roots. (Binary heap find-min is O(1); this is the only thing binomial heaps give up.)

Link. To join two trees of the same order k, compare the two roots. The larger root becomes the leftmost child of the smaller root (for a max-heap, the smaller goes under the larger). The result is a Bk+1. Everything below is built from this one step.

2. Union is binary addition

To merge heaps H1 and H2, add their sizes in binary. Wherever the addition carries, you link two trees. Walk from bit 0 upward and look at the trees of order k you hold: H1's, H2's, and the carry from the bit below.

Method · union, one bit at a time

  1. Write both root lists by order. Write the sum of the sizes in binary: that tells you the answer's orders.
  2. At order k count how many Bk you have, including the carry.
  3. 0 or 1 tree: leave it in the result.
  4. 2 trees: link them (larger root under smaller root). The result is a Bk+1, the carry for the next order.
  5. 3 trees: keep any one in the result, link the other two into the carry (the notes say "keep any one of the three").
  6. Go up one order. Stop when nothing is left. The result roots are in increasing order of degree.
BINOMIAL-LINK(y, z)                   // y, z roots of equal order, key[z] <= key[y]
  y.parent = z; y.sibling = z.child; z.child = y; z.degree = z.degree + 1

BINOMIAL-HEAP-UNION(H1, H2)
  H.head = MERGE(H1, H2)              // merge the root lists by degree, no linking yet
  x = H.head; prev = NIL; next = x.sibling
  while next != NIL
    if x.degree != next.degree or (next.sibling != NIL and next.sibling.degree == x.degree)
      prev = x; x = next                       // 1 tree here, or 3 in a row: skip the first
    else if x.key <= next.key
      x.sibling = next.sibling; BINOMIAL-LINK(next, x)
    else
      if prev == NIL then H.head = next else prev.sibling = next
      BINOMIAL-LINK(x, next); x = next
    next = x.sibling
  return H

3. Insert, extract-min, decrease-key, delete

Method · each operation

  1. Insert x: make a one-node heap (a B0) and union it into H. Like adding 1 to a binary counter: carries ripple while a B0, then B1, ... are already present.
  2. Extract-min: scan the root list for the smallest root r. Remove r. Its children are Bk−1, ..., B0 (chapter 2, property 4). Reverse them so they are in increasing order, giving a heap H'. Union H (the rest) with H'.
  3. Decrease-key(x, new): set the key, then swap with the parent while the parent is larger (bubble up), exactly as in a binary heap. The tree has height at most \(\log_2 n\).
  4. Delete x: decrease x to \(-\infty\) (it bubbles to its root), then extract-min. You cannot "swap with the last node" as in a binary heap, because there is no last node and it would break the shape.

4. Max-heap versions

Swap "smaller" and "larger" everywhere. Link puts the smaller root under the larger. Extract-max scans for the largest root. Increase-key bubbles up (the max-heap twin of decrease-key). Delete raises the key to \(+\infty\), then extract-max.

Where marks go: decreasing a key in a max-heap

  • In a max-heap, decreasing a key moves it down, not up. Repeatedly swap it with its largest child until it is at least as large as all its children. The 2022–23 question does this to the key 20.
  • The cost is O(log² n) worst case, not O(log n): the node sinks at most the height of its tree, but each level scans up to log n children to find the largest (k(k+1)/2 comparisons from the root of a Bk).

(iii) Extract-max twice and count credits. The question's rules: unlinking a node from its children costs 1 credit, a node addition costs 1, an insertion costs 1, a comparison costs 1, all else 0. The wording "two node addition operation takes one credit" is unclear in the scan; I read it as one credit per node addition. Counting rule I use: finding the max among r roots costs r−1 comparisons; each child of the removed root costs 1 unlink; each link costs 1 comparison (which root is larger) plus 1 node addition (hanging one root under the other).

First extract-max. Roots are 18(…) (B2) and 25(…) (B4): 1 comparison to find 25. Remove 25: its 4 children, 19(…) (B3, the tree whose root was 20 before the decrease), 17(…) (B2), 13(10) (B1) and 21 (B0), are unlinked: 4 credits. Union with the old B2 18: order 2 has two trees (18 and 17): 18 is larger, link 17 under 18: 2 credits. Order 3 now has two trees (19's and the carry 18's): link 18 under 19: 2 credits. Total 1 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 9.

Second extract-max. Three roots 21, 13, 19: 2 comparisons to find 21. It has no children (0 unlinks), and the rest is unioned with an empty heap (0 links). Total 2.

Grand total: 9 + 2 = 11 credits under this rule.

6. 2021–22 Q5: convert a min binomial heap to a max binomial heap (15 marks)

Asked: write pseudocode that converts a min priority queue to a max priority queue in amortized O(n), and analyse it with the potential method. The scan of pq2's figure is too small to read the keys, so I show the method on the CT-4 heap instead; follow the same steps on your figure. The pq1 inserts 210, 201, 220, 202, 221, 205, 209 are readable and appear in the practice trace below.

Idea. A min-heap's tree shapes are right but its order is wrong. Do not repair it in place. Copy every key into a new, empty max-heap by n inserts. Each insert costs O(1) amortized, so n inserts are O(n).

CONVERT-MIN-TO-MAX(Hmin)
  Hmax = MAKE-BINOMIAL-HEAP()          // empty, uses "larger root on top" in LINK
  for each tree T in Hmin.root list
    for each node x in T                // any traversal, e.g. DFS
      MAX-INSERT(Hmax, x.key)           // one-node heap + union
  return Hmax

Proof · amortized O(n), potential method

  1. Potential: \(\Phi(H) = t(H)\), the number of trees in the root list of the max-heap being built. \(\Phi(\text{empty}) = 0\) and \(\Phi \ge 0\) always.
  2. One insert is a binary-counter increment that stops at the first free order, and causes c links (the carries). Actual cost \(= 1 + c\).
  3. Each link merges two trees into one, so the tree count drops by 1 per link, and the new B0 adds 1: \(\Delta\Phi = 1 - c\).
  4. Amortized cost of one insert \(= (1+c) + (1-c) = 2 = O(1)\).
  5. For n inserts the amortized total is \(2n\). Actual total \(=\) amortized total \(- (\Phi_{\text{final}} - \Phi_{\text{start}}) \le 2n\), since \(\Phi_{\text{final}} \ge 0 = \Phi_{\text{start}}\).
  6. Traversing Hmin visits each of the n nodes once: O(n). Total O(n). ∎

7. Amortized insert and worst-case bounds (2022–23 Q8(b), 12 marks)

Claim. In a binomial heap, INSERT has amortized cost O(1); EXTRACT-MIN and DECREASE-KEY have worst-case cost O(log n).

Proof

  1. Potential. \(\Phi(H) = t(H)\), the number of trees in H. \(\Phi \ge 0\), and \(\Phi = 0\) for the empty heap.
  2. Insert. Make a B0 and add it like a binary-counter increment: while the root list holds a tree of the carry's order, link (O(1) each); stop at the first order with no tree. The rest of the root list is never touched. Suppose c links happen (the carry runs through c orders). Actual cost \(= 1 + c\) (one unit to create the B0 and place the carry, one per link).
  3. Trees after \(= t(H) + 1 - c\), so \(\Delta\Phi = 1 - c\).
  4. Amortized \(= (1+c) + (1-c) = 2 = O(1)\) (the c cancels, so a long carry is paid for by the trees it removes).
  5. Why amortized bounds actual. Over any m operations from the empty heap, \(\sum \hat c_i = \sum c_i + \Phi(H_m) - \Phi(H_0)\). Since \(\Phi(H_m)\ge 0 = \Phi(H_0)\), \(\sum c_i \le \sum \hat c_i\), so m inserts cost at most 2m in total, O(1) each on average. ∎
  6. Extract-min. H has n nodes, so at most \(\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor + 1\) roots (one per bit of n), and the removed root is the root of some Bk with \(2^k\le n\), so it has \(k\le\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\) children. Scanning for the min: O(log n). Reversing the children: O(log n). Union of two heaps with O(log n) trees each: O(log n) (one pass over the merged root list). Total O(log n).
  7. Decrease-key. The node is at depth at most the tree height \(\le \lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\) (chapter 2, property 2). Each bubble-up step is a constant-time swap, so O(log n). ∎

Why union is the point (notes p.9, slides 2 to 3 and 44). A binary heap merges by joining the two arrays and re-running build-heap, which is O(n₁ + n₂). Build-heap itself is O(n): sink down from index n/2 to 1 (slide 41). Slide 44 asks "how to union two heaps?" and answers "no easy solution". Bayzid sir's own summary (notes p.17): the binomial heap sacrifices only find-min (O(log n) instead of O(1)) and gains merge in O(log n) instead of O(n).

Fibonacci heaps improve this table; see chapter 4.

Where marks go

  • Linking in the wrong direction: min-heap, larger root goes under the smaller. Max-heap, the opposite.
  • Forgetting to reverse the children in extract-min. They come out as Bk−1, ..., B0 and the union needs increasing order.
  • Putting two trees of the same order in the result. After union every order appears at most once.
  • Three trees at one order: you must keep one and link only two. Do not link all three.
  • Writing the new child on the right. This book puts it leftmost; either is accepted if the shape is right, but be consistent.
  • Giving only the final heap in a "show intermediate steps" question. Draw every step.

10. Mandatory practice

Draw every step. The union write-up and the amortized proof are the two things most likely to be asked in words.

If you got one wrong: open the steps and find the first frame where your forest differs. It is almost always a union with three trees at one order, or a missing reversal of the children.
