Analog Computer for Differential Equations

"Design an op-amp circuit that solves this differential equation" is the biggest single op-amp question type: it appeared in 2014–15, 2015–16, 2018–19, 2019–20, 2022–23 and 2024–25 (the 2024–25 one in the compulsory Q1), 17 to 30 marks each, about 132 marks in total. Every version is solved by the same five-step recipe: solve for the highest derivative, build a summer that outputs it, integrate it down to \(v\), feed the integrator outputs back to the summer with resistor ratios equal to the coefficients, and get the constant from a ±15 V rail. Once you have done it a few times it is close to free marks.

1. The three building blocks

Everything comes from the inverting summer and the integrator (chapter 2 and chapter 3 of this part). With the + input grounded, the − input is a virtual ground, so each input resistor carries \(v_i/R_i\) and all of it flows into the feedback element.

Inverting summer
\(v_o = -\left(\dfrac{R_f}{R_1}v_1 + \dfrac{R_f}{R_2}v_2 + \cdots\right)\). Each input gets its own weight \(R_f/R_i\), always with a minus sign.
Integrator
R in, C in feedback: \(v_o(t) = -\dfrac{1}{RC}\displaystyle\int_0^t v_i\,dt + v_o(0)\). With \(RC = 1\) s (1 MΩ, 1 µF) it is just "\(-\int\)".
Summing integrator
Several input resistors into one integrator: \(v_o = -\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{v_1}{R_1C} + \frac{v_2}{R_2C}\right)dt\). Useful for first-order equations (one op-amp).
Unity inverter
10 kΩ in, 10 kΩ feedback: \(v_o = -v_i\). Used whenever a signal is needed with the other sign.

The key idea: if some node holds \(v''\), one integrator turns it into \(-v'\) and a second one turns that into \(+v\). The differential equation says how \(v''\) is made from \(v'\), \(v\) and the forcing, so a summer fed from those integrator outputs can make \(v''\). The loop closes, and the only waveform that satisfies all three blocks at once is the solution of the equation.

\[ v'' \xrightarrow{\;-\!\int\;} -v' \xrightarrow{\;-\!\int\;} +v \]

2. The method that always works

Method · analog computer for a second-order equation

  1. Solve for the highest derivative. Divide by \(a\): \(v'' = \alpha v' + \beta v + \gamma + \delta x\) with \(\alpha = -b/a\), \(\beta = -c/a\), \(\gamma = -d/a\), \(\delta = e/a\). Write this line on the script: examiners look for it.
  2. Chain: summer U1 outputs \(v''\); integrator U2 gives \(-v'\); integrator U3 gives \(+v\) (the solution node). Integrators: \(R\) = 1 MΩ, \(C\) = 1 µF.
  3. Pick the signal for each term. U1 inverts, so a term with a positive coefficient needs the negative signal and vice versa:
    • \(\alpha > 0\): feed \(-v'\) (U2 directly). \(\alpha < 0\): feed \(+v'\) (add an inverter after U2).
    • \(\beta > 0\): feed \(-v\) (add an inverter after U3). \(\beta < 0\): feed \(+v\) (U3 directly).
    • \(\gamma > 0\): from the \(-15\) V rail. \(\gamma < 0\): from the \(+15\) V rail.
    • \(\delta > 0\): feed \(-x\) (input inverter). \(\delta < 0\): feed \(x\) directly.
  4. Resistors: choose \(R_f\) (100 kΩ is convenient). Then \(R_{v'} = R_f/|\alpha|\), \(R_v = R_f/|\beta|\), \(R_x = R_f/|\delta|\), and for the constant \(R_c = R_f\cdot 15/|\gamma|\) (the rail is 15 V, not 1 V).
  5. Initial conditions: pre-charge U3's capacitor so its output starts at \(v(0)\), and U2's so its output starts at \(-v'(0)\). "All initial conditions zero" means both capacitors are shorted by reset switches until \(t\) = 0.
  6. Finish: draw the full diagram with every value, mark the solution node, and write two lines of explanation (below). Count the op-amps.

The explanation examiners want ("briefly discuss the operation", 2018–19 and 2019–20): the summer forms the highest derivative from the lower derivatives and the forcing, exactly as the equation says; the two integrators produce \(-v'\) and \(v\) from it; feeding them back closes the loop, so the voltage at the solution node is forced to obey the equation. The integrator capacitors hold the initial conditions; the ±15 V rail supplies the constant term.

Inverters you cannot avoid. Whether the summer outputs \(v''\) or \(-v''\), you need an inverter for the \(v'\) term exactly when \(\alpha < 0\) and for the \(v\) term exactly when \(\beta > 0\). A normal (stable, damped) equation has \(\alpha < 0\), \(\beta < 0\): one inverter, four op-amps. Choosing \(-v''\) at the summer only moves the remaining sign problems to the input \(x\) and the output node (2022–23 below).

Worked example: a first-order equation with one op-amp

For a first-order equation one summing integrator does the whole job: its output is \(v\), and the equation tells you what to integrate.

Worked example: Khairul s.21–22, \(v'' + 20v' + 100v - 25 = 0\)

Drill (a): equation → resistor values

Random equation every time. Answer with the canonical architecture of the method box (U1 outputs \(v''\), U2 → \(-v'\), U3 → \(v\), inverters only where a sign is missing).

3. Reading a given circuit: which equation does it solve?

The reverse question (and a good check on your own design): write the summer equation, then replace each fed-back node by what it holds.

Method · circuit → differential equation

  1. Name the nodes along the chain: summer output \(s\), U2 output \(= -\frac{1}{RC}\int s\), U3 output. With \(RC\) = 1 s and U3 output called \(v\): \(s = v''\) and U2 output \(= -v'\).
  2. Write the summer: \(v'' = -\sum (R_f/R_i)\,(\text{signal}_i)\), with each signal written in terms of \(v'\), \(v\), the rail voltage or \(x\).
  3. Move everything to the left: \(v'' + p\,v' + q\,v = r\). Stable (settles) if \(p > 0\) and \(q > 0\); then the final value is \(r/q\).

Variant (2016–17 Sec A Q1(a)): "determine the differential equation solved by the circuit" for a non-inverting amplifier with RC networks. There you write KCL at the capacitor node and replace \(s\) with \(d/dt\); it is worked out in Active filters.

4. Scaling, forcing functions and initial conditions

Time scaling. If every integrator has \(RC = \tau\) instead of 1 s and the summer ratios stay the same, the circuit produces \(v(t/\tau)\): the same waveform with the time axis stretched by \(\tau\) (\(\tau\) = 0.1 s runs ten times faster). So "use \(RC\) = 1 s" is what makes the circuit solve the equation in real seconds; say so.

Magnitude scaling. The solution must stay inside ±15 V. If it would not (or if a resistor comes out below about 1 kΩ or above 10 MΩ), solve for \(u = v/k\): divide the constant and forcing terms by \(k\) and read \(v = k\,u\). Changing \(R_f\) for all inputs together (e.g. 1 MΩ) rescales every resistor without changing the equation.

Sinusoidal forcing from a given oscillator. If \(\sin\omega t\) is available but \(\cos\omega t\) is needed, use a differentiator with \(RC = 1/\omega\): \(-RC\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = -\cos\omega t\), exact from \(t\) = 0. An uncharged integrator gives \(-\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t\sin\omega t\,dt = \frac{\cos\omega t - 1}{\omega RC}\): the extra \(-1\) adds a false constant forcing, unless the capacitor is pre-charged to cancel it.

Initial conditions. The capacitor sits between the virtual ground and the output, so the integrator output equals the capacitor voltage (output side positive). To start at \(v(0) = 2\) V, charge U3's capacitor to 2 V through a switch that opens at \(t\) = 0. A homogeneous equation (no constant, no input) with zero initial conditions has the solution \(v \equiv 0\): you must give a non-zero initial condition or nothing happens (2014–15).

Stability remark. Roots of \(as^2 + bs + c = 0\): if any has a positive real part (\(b/a < 0\) or \(c/a < 0\)), the solution grows and a real circuit runs into the ±15 V rails. 2019–20, 2022–23 and 2024–25 all set such equations. One line saying so shows understanding; the design itself is unchanged.

Traps (where marks go)

  • Forgetting to divide by the coefficient of \(v''\) (2024–25: \(2v''\), so every term halves).
  • Sign of each fed-back signal: the summer inverts, so a positive coefficient needs the negative signal. Check one term by substitution before drawing.
  • Constant term: \(R_c = R_f\cdot15/|\gamma|\) from the rail with the opposite sign to \(\gamma\). Writing \(R_f/|\gamma|\) assumes a 1 V source that the question did not give.
  • Not marking the solution node, or leaving it at \(-v\) without saying so.
  • Integrator \(RC\) not equal to 1 s while claiming the circuit solves the equation in real time.
  • Zero initial conditions on a homogeneous equation: the answer is 0; say which capacitor you pre-charge.
  • Generating \(\cos\) from \(\sin\) with an uncharged integrator (adds a DC term).

Exam questions

2024–25 · Sec A · Q1(b) · 17 marks · compulsory

Design a circuit using op-amps and other passive elements that can solve \(2\dfrac{d^2v}{dt^2} - 3\dfrac{dv}{dt} + 4v + 5 = 0\). Draw the overall connection diagram with proper element values. Op-amps are biased with ±15 V. All initial conditions are zero.

Show worked answer

Highest derivative: \(v'' = 1.5v' - 2v - 2.5\).

Chain: U1 (summer) → \(v''\); U2 integrator (1 MΩ, 1 µF) → \(-v'\); U3 integrator (1 MΩ, 1 µF) → \(v\).

Signals: \(+1.5v'\) needs \(-v'\): U2 output, available. \(-2v\) needs \(+v\): U3 output, available. \(-2.5\) needs the \(+15\) V rail. No inverters: three op-amps.

Summer with \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ: \(v'' = -\left[\dfrac{100}{R_1}(-v') + \dfrac{100}{R_2}(v) + \dfrac{100}{R_3}(15)\right]\) needs \(R_1 = 100/1.5 = 66.7\) kΩ, \(R_2 = 100/2 = 50\) kΩ, \(R_3 = 100\times15/2.5 = 600\) kΩ.

Check: \(-[1.5(-v') + 2v + 2.5] = 1.5v' - 2v - 2.5\) ✓. Solution \(v(t)\) at U3's output. Zero initial conditions: reset switches across both capacitors open at \(t\) = 0.

Remark: the roots of \(2s^2 - 3s + 4\) are \(0.75 \pm j1.20\) (right half-plane), so the forced value \(v = -1.25\) V is unstable: the output oscillates with growing amplitude and ends at the ±15 V rails. The circuit still "solves" the equation as long as it stays linear. (The figure above is fully wired: open it in the simulator and watch this happen.)

2022–23 · Sec B · Q8(a) · 20 marks

Using op-amps, design a circuit to solve \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dt^2} - 2\dfrac{dy}{dt} + 2y = 8x\), where \(x\) is the input and \(y\) the output.

Show worked answer

\(y'' = 2y' - 2y + 8x\). Here it is neater to let the summer output \(-y''\), because then \(x\) can be fed directly:

\(-y'' = -2y' + 2y - 8x = -\left[2y' + 2(-y) + 8x\right]\).

Chain: U1 summer → \(-y''\); U2 integrator → \(-\int(-y'') = +y'\); U3 integrator → \(-y\). Both needed signals (\(+y'\), \(-y\)) are integrator outputs, and \(x\) goes in directly.

Summer \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ: from \(y'\) (U2) \(R_1\) = 100/2 = 50 kΩ; from \(-y\) (U3) \(R_2\) = 50 kΩ; from \(x\) \(R_x\) = 100/8 = 12.5 kΩ. Integrators 1 MΩ, 1 µF.

Output: U3 holds \(-y\); a unity inverter U4 (10 kΩ/10 kΩ) gives \(y\). Four op-amps. (With the summer at \(+y''\) you would need an inverter on \(x\) instead and read \(y\) at U3: also four.)

Remark: roots \(1 \pm j\): unstable, so for any input the output eventually saturates.

2019–20 · Sec B · Q5(b) · 29⅔ marks (exam held Jan 2021)

Design a circuit using op-amps that can provide the solution to \(3\dfrac{d^2v}{dt^2} + K\dfrac{dv}{dt} - 3v + 8 = 0\), where \(K\) is the last digit of your student ID. Briefly discuss the operation of your circuit.

Show worked answer

\(v'' = -\dfrac{K}{3}v' + v - \dfrac{8}{3}\).

Chain: U1 → \(v''\), U2 → \(-v'\), U3 → \(v\) (1 MΩ, 1 µF each).

Signals: \(-\frac{K}{3}v'\) needs \(+v'\): inverter U4 after U2. \(+v\) needs \(-v\): inverter U5 after U3. \(-\frac83\) needs the \(+15\) V rail.

Summer \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ: \(R_1 = 100/(K/3) = 300/K\) kΩ (from \(+v'\)); \(R_2\) = 100 kΩ (from \(-v\)); \(R_3 = 100\times15/(8/3) = 562.5\) kΩ (from +15 V). Inverters 10 kΩ/10 kΩ. Five op-amps.

Check: \(-[(K/3)v' + 1\cdot(-v) + (8/3)]\) ✓.

Operation: U1 adds \(-\frac K3 v'\), \(+v\) and \(-\frac83\) to form \(v''\); U2 and U3 integrate it to \(-v'\) and \(v\); U4, U5 supply the signs U1 needs. 2018–19 · Sec B · Q5(b) · 26⅔ marks

Design an electronic analog computer using op-amps that can solve \(\dfrac{d^2v}{dt^2} + 4\dfrac{dv}{dt} + 5v - 5 = 0\). Give a brief explanation of the operation of the designed computer.

Show worked answer

\(v'' = -4v' - 5v + 5\).

Signals: \(-4v'\) needs \(+v'\) (inverter U4 after U2); \(-5v\) needs \(+v\) (U3 directly); \(+5\) needs the \(-15\) V rail.

Summer \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ: \(R_1\) = 100/4 = 25 kΩ (from \(+v'\)), \(R_2\) = 100/5 = 20 kΩ (from \(v\)), \(R_3\) = 100 × 15/5 = 300 kΩ (from −15 V). Integrators 1 MΩ, 1 µF; inverter 10 kΩ/10 kΩ. Four op-amps.

Check: \(-\left[4v' + 5v + \tfrac{100}{300}(-15)\right] = -4v' - 5v + 5\) ✓.

Explanation: as in the method box. This equation is stable (roots \(-2 \pm j\)): \(v\) rises with a small overshoot and settles at \(5/5 = 1\) V.

2015–16 · Sec B · Q6(b) · 20 marks

Design an analog computer circuit to solve, for \(t > 0\), \(\dfrac{d^2v}{dt^2} + 3\dfrac{dv}{dt} + 2v + 4\cos 10t = 0\), with \(v(0) = 2\) and \(v'(0) = 0\). An oscillator providing \(\sin 10t\) is available.

Show worked answer

\(v'' = -3v' - 2v - 4\cos10t\).

Forcing: differentiator U5 on the oscillator (C = 1 µF, R = 100 kΩ, \(RC\) = 0.1 s = \(1/\omega\)): \(-0.1\frac{d}{dt}\sin10t = -\cos10t\). Inverter U6 → \(+\cos10t\).

Signals: \(-3v'\) needs \(+v'\) (inverter U4 after U2); \(-2v\) needs \(v\) (U3); \(-4\cos10t\) needs \(+\cos10t\) (U6).

Summer \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ: 33.3 kΩ from \(+v'\), 50 kΩ from \(v\), 25 kΩ from \(+\cos10t\). Integrators 1 MΩ, 1 µF.

Initial conditions: U2's capacitor uncharged (\(-v'(0) = 0\)); U3's capacitor pre-charged to 2 V, output side positive, released at \(t\) = 0, so \(v(0)\) = 2 V. Solution at U3's output.

Alternative: replace U5 + U6 by one integrator on \(\sin10t\) with \(RC\) = 0.1 s whose capacitor is pre-charged so its output starts at +1 V: output \(= 1 + (\cos10t - 1) = \cos10t\). Without the pre-charge it gives \(\cos10t - 1\), a false constant forcing of \(-4\) (the bank's Gemini answer has this flaw). The equation is stable (roots −1, −2): \(v\) decays from 2 V to a small steady sinusoid of amplitude 0.039 V.

2014–15 · Sec B · Q6(a) · 20 marks

Design a circuit using op-amps that can solve \(3\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 4x = 0\). Point out the node that represents the solution \(x(t)\). Specify all resistor and capacitor values.

Show worked answer

\(x'' = -\frac43 x\). U1 (summer/scaler, \(R_f\) = 100 kΩ) → \(x''\); U2 integrator → \(-x'\); U3 integrator → \(x\) (1 MΩ, 1 µF). The term \(-\frac43x\) needs \(+x\): U3 output through \(R = 100/(4/3) = 75\) kΩ. Three op-amps; the solution node is U3's output.

Essential remark: with zero initial conditions the solution is \(x \equiv 0\). Pre-charge U3's capacitor (e.g. \(x(0)\) = 1 V): then \(x(t) = \cos(1.155t)\) V, a sinusoid at \(\omega = \sqrt{4/3} = 1.155\) rad/s (0.184 Hz). This circuit is a sine-wave oscillator.
