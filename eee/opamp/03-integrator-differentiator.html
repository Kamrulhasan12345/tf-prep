The Integrator and the Differentiator

"Here are the input and output waveforms: design the circuit" is a near-annual op-amp question: 2014–15, 2015–16, 2016–17, 2017–18, 2018–19, 2019–20 (12 to 17 marks each), plus a summing integrator in 2020–21 (20 marks). The maths is one slope and one RC product. This chapter also covers the trap behind two wrong answers in the question bank: an uncharged integrator fed with a sine gives a cosine plus a DC offset.

1. The Miller integrator

Derivation. Virtual ground at \(v_-\), so the input current is \(i = v_I/R\). It cannot enter the op-amp, so it charges \(C\). The capacitor voltage (left plate minus right plate) is \(v_C = 0 - v_O\), and \(i = C\,dv_C/dt\):

\[ \frac{v_I}{R} = C\frac{d(-v_O)}{dt} \;\Rightarrow\; v_O(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t v_I(\tau)\,d\tau + v_O(0) \]
\(RC\)
Integrator time constant. A DC input \(V\) makes a ramp of slope \(-V/RC\).
\(v_O(0)\)
Initial output \(= -V_C(0)\). A reset switch across \(C\) opened at \(t = 0\) means \(v_O(0) = 0\) (uncharged). It is part of the answer: always state it.
Frequency domain
\(V_o/V_i = -1/(sRC)\): \(|H| = 1/(\omega RC)\), phase \(+90°\) (inversion −180°, integration −90°). Unity gain at \(\omega = 1/RC\).

Standard inputs.

  • DC \(V\): ramp \(v_O = v_O(0) - Vt/RC\) until it hits the rail \(\mp L\), then it stays there (2014–15 uses this on purpose).
  • Square wave \(\pm V\), half-period \(t_h\): straight segments of slope \(\mp V/RC\), so a triangle of peak-to-peak \(V t_h/RC\). If the switch opens in the middle of a half-cycle (first segment \(t_h/2\) long), the triangle is centred on 0 V.
  • Pulse then zero: ramp during the pulse, then the output holds (no current, capacitor keeps its charge).

Method · integrator output for a piecewise-constant input

  1. Start at \(v_O(0)\) (0 after a reset).
  2. For each interval of constant input \(V_k\) and length \(\Delta t_k\): \(\Delta v_O = -V_k\Delta t_k/RC\). Keep \(\Delta t\) in ms and \(RC\) in ms.
  3. Add up the vertices; join them with straight lines; clip at \(\pm L\).
  4. Design version: \(RC = |V|\,t_h/V_{pp}\) for a required peak-to-peak; pick \(R\) (or \(C\)), compute the other.

Worked example: 2017–18 Sec B Q5(b)

2. A sine into an integrator: the DC term (the bank's two mistakes)

Feed \(v_I = V_p\sin\omega t\) into an integrator whose capacitor is uncharged at \(t = 0\):

\[ v_O = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t V_p\sin\omega\tau\,d\tau = -\frac{V_p}{\omega RC}\Big[1 - \cos\omega t\Big] = \underbrace{\frac{V_p}{\omega RC}\cos\omega t}_{\text{wanted}}\;\underbrace{-\;\frac{V_p}{\omega RC}}_{\text{DC offset}} \]

The output is the expected cosine (amplitude \(V_p/\omega RC\), leading the input by 90°) shifted down by its own amplitude: it swings between 0 and \(-2V_p/\omega RC\). The phasor result \(-1/(j\omega RC)\) is the steady state with the "right" initial charge only. An ideal integrator never forgets the offset.

The question bank's answers to 2023–24 Q5(b) and 2021–22 Q1(a) both made a cosine this way and so are off by a constant (+4 V and +0.5 V). Fixes, in order of preference:

  1. Use a differentiator to make the cosine: \(-RC\dfrac{d}{dt}V_p\sin\omega t = -\omega RC\,V_p\cos\omega t\). No constant appears.
  2. Pre-charge the integrator capacitor so that \(v_O(0) = +V_p/\omega RC\) (state it explicitly).
  3. Practical integrator (resistor \(R_f\) across \(C\)): the offset decays with time constant \(R_fC\), but the gain and phase are then no longer exactly \(1/\omega RC\) and 90°.

3. The differentiator

Derivation. Virtual ground: the capacitor voltage is \(v_I\), so \(i = C\,dv_I/dt\); it flows through \(R\): \(0 - v_O = iR\):

\[ v_O(t) = -RC\,\frac{dv_I}{dt}, \qquad \frac{V_o}{V_i} = -sRC,\;\; |H| = \omega RC \]
  • Triangle in → square out: each straight segment of slope \(S\) gives a constant \(-RC\,S\).
  • Ramp in → step out, constant in → zero out.
  • Sine in: \(V_p\sin\omega t \to -\omega RC\,V_p\cos\omega t\), amplitude \(\omega RCV_p\), no DC term.

Practical versions. The ideal differentiator's gain grows with frequency, so it amplifies noise (and tends to ring). Add a small resistor \(R_s\) in series with \(C\): gain levels off at \(-R/R_s\) above \(\omega = 1/(R_sC)\). The ideal integrator has infinite DC gain, so any offset voltage or bias current ramps it into saturation. Add \(R_f\) across \(C\): DC gain becomes \(-R_f/R\), and it integrates only above \(\omega = 1/(R_fC)\). Khairul s.17–18 also asks "can you build them with an inductor?": \(L\) at the input and \(R\) in feedback gives \(v_O = -\dfrac{R}{L}\int v_I\,dt\); \(R\) at the input and \(L\) in feedback gives \(v_O = -\dfrac{L}{R}\dfrac{dv_I}{dt}\).

4. Design from waveforms (type O-06)

Method · given vin and vout, design the circuit

  1. Write both waveforms piece by piece (slopes in V/ms).
  2. Output constant where input is a ramp → differentiator: \(K = v_{out}/(dv_{in}/dt)\). Output a ramp where input is constant → integrator: \(1/RC = |dv_{out}/dt|/|v_{in}|\).
  3. Sign. The basic circuits invert. If \(K\) (or the integration sign) comes out positive, add a unity inverter (10 kΩ/10 kΩ) before or after, or use the opposite rail as the input.
  4. Two inputs (2016–17): fit \(v_{out} = K_1\,dv_1/dt + K_2v_2\) from two intervals, check on a third, then build with a summer.
  5. Pick \(C\) (0.01 to 10 µF), compute \(R\) (keep it between 1 kΩ and a few MΩ). Check the peak output against the rails.

Worked example: 2015–16 Sec B Q5(c), triangle to square

5. Summing integrator

Several inputs \(v_k\) through \(R_k\) into the virtual ground, \(C\) in feedback. The currents add before they charge \(C\):

\[ v_O = -\frac{1}{C}\int_0^t\left(\frac{v_1}{R_1} + \frac{v_2}{R_2}\right)d\tau + v_O(0) \;\xrightarrow{R_1 = R_2 = R}\; -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t (v_1 + v_2)\,d\tau \]

This is how one op-amp produces a weighted sum of integrals; it is used again in expression synthesis and in the analog computer.

Traps (where marks go)

  • A sine through an uncharged integrator gives \((V_p/\omega RC)(\cos\omega t - 1)\), not a pure cosine. Use a differentiator, or state the pre-charge.
  • Units: slopes in V/ms with \(RC\) in ms, or V/s with \(RC\) in s; never mix.
  • Forgetting the inversion: both basic circuits have a minus sign. Check the sign on the first segment.
  • Half-length first segment (2017–18): the first ramp is only 1 ms, so the triangle is centred on 0, between −5 and +5 V.
  • After a pulse ends the integrator holds its last value (2019–20); it does not return to 0.
  • A ramp stops at the rail; the time to saturate is \(L\,RC/|V|\).

Exam questions

2020–21 · Sec B · Q7(a) · 20 marks · type O-05

Design a circuit using ideal op-amps that would implement \(v_o \propto \int(v_1 + v_2)\,dt\). Write the final output expression in terms of the circuit components used in your design.

Show worked answer

Circuit: summing integrator: \(v_1\) through \(R_1\) and \(v_2\) through \(R_2\) into the inverting input; \(C\) from the inverting input to the output; \(+\) grounded.

Derivation: virtual ground, so \(i_1 = v_1/R_1\), \(i_2 = v_2/R_2\); no current enters the op-amp, so \(i_C = i_1 + i_2\). With \(v_C = 0 - v_o\): \(C\dfrac{d(-v_o)}{dt} = \dfrac{v_1}{R_1} + \dfrac{v_2}{R_2}\).

\[ v_o(t) = -\frac{1}{C}\int_0^t\left(\frac{v_1}{R_1} + \frac{v_2}{R_2}\right)d\tau + v_o(0). \]

For a true \(\int(v_1 + v_2)\), make \(R_1 = R_2 = R\): \(v_o = -\dfrac{1}{RC}\int_0^t (v_1 + v_2)\,d\tau\) (with a reset, \(v_o(0) = 0\)). E.g. \(R\) = 100 kΩ, \(C\) = 1 µF gives \(-10\int(v_1 + v_2)dt\). If a positive constant is wanted, follow with a unity inverter: \(v_o = +\dfrac{1}{RC}\int(v_1 + v_2)dt\). In practice add a large resistor across \(C\) to stop offset drift.

2019–20 (January 2021 paper) · Sec B · Q5(a) · 17 marks

The input \(v_I\) is a 1 V pulse from \(t\) = 0 to 1 ms and 0 afterwards. The output \(v_O\) ramps linearly from 0 down to −10 V at \(t\) = 1 ms and then stays at −10 V. Design the circuit.

Show worked answer

Constant input → ramp output: an integrator. The output goes negative for positive input, so the inverting integrator needs no extra inverter. After the pulse the input is 0 and the output holds at −10 V: exactly what an integrator does.

Slope: \(\dfrac{dv_O}{dt} = \dfrac{-10\text{ V}}{1\text{ ms}} = -10^4\) V/s \(= -\dfrac{v_I}{RC} = -\dfrac{1}{RC}\), so \(RC = 10^{-4}\) s = 0.1 ms.

Choose \(R\) = 10 kΩ, \(C\) = 10 nF (or 100 kΩ, 1 nF). Reset switch across \(C\) so that \(v_O(0) = 0\). Supplies must exceed 10 V (e.g. ±15 V).

2018–19 · Sec B · Q6(a) · 16 marks

Design a circuit using op-amp(s) for the given input \(v_i(t)\) and output \(v_o(t)\): \(v_i\) rises linearly from 0 at \(t\) = 0 to 10 V at 1 ms, then stays at 10 V. \(v_o\) = +1 V for 0 < t < 1 ms, then 0. (The output level is hand-written on the paper; it reads as +1 V.)

Show worked answer

Ramp input → constant output; constant input → zero: a differentiator, \(v_o = K\,dv_i/dt\).

Slope \(= 10\text{ V}/1\text{ ms} = 10^4\) V/s. \(K = \dfrac{1}{10^4} = 10^{-4}\) s, and \(K\) is positive.

The inverting differentiator gives \(-RC\,dv_i/dt\), so use \(RC = 10^{-4}\) s (\(C\) = 0.1 µF in series at the input, \(R\) = 1 kΩ in feedback) giving \(-1\) V, followed by a unity inverter (10 kΩ/10 kΩ) giving +1 V. (Equivalent: invert \(v_i\) first, then differentiate.) If the hand-written level is a different value \(V\), only \(R\) changes: \(R = V/(10^4 C)\).

2017–18 · Sec B · Q5(b) · 16 marks

Inverting integrator (\(R\) from \(v_{in}\) to \(v_-\), \(C\) from \(v_-\) to \(v_o\), switch across \(C\) opened at \(t\) = 0), ±15 V. Input: +5 V for 0–1 ms, −5 V for 1–3 ms, +5 V for 3–5 ms, −5 V for 5–7 ms, +5 V after 7 ms. (i) If \(R\) = 10 kΩ and \(C\) = 0.1 µF, sketch \(v_o\) to scale. (ii) If \(R\) = 10 kΩ, what \(C\) gives a 2 V peak-to-peak output?

Show worked answer

(i) \(RC = 10\text{ k}\Omega\times0.1\text{ µF} = 1\) ms. Slope \(= \mp5\text{ V}/1\text{ ms} = \mp5\) V/ms. Switch opens at 0, so \(v_o(0) = 0\).

0–1 ms: \(\Delta v_o = -5\times1/1 = -5\) V → −5 V. 1–3 ms: \(+5\times2/1 = +10\) V → +5 V. 3–5 ms: → −5 V. 5–7 ms: → +5 V. A triangle between −5 V and +5 V (10 V p-p, period 4 ms), starting at 0 and going down first; within ±15 V.

(ii) In each full half-cycle (2 ms) \(\Delta v_o = 5\times2/RC\). Set it to 2 V: \(RC = 5\) ms, \(C = 5\text{ ms}/10\text{ k}\Omega = 0.5\) µF. The output is then a triangle between −1 V and +1 V (first ramp to −1 V at 1 ms).

2016–17 · Sec A · Q2(c) · 15 marks

Determine an op-amp circuit realisation for the given waveforms. \(V_1\): triangle, −4 V at \(t\) = 0, +4 V at 4 ms, −4 V at 8 ms, +4 V at 12 ms. \(V_2\): 0 for 0–1 ms, −5 V for 1–3 ms, 0 for 3–5 ms, +5 V for 5–7 ms, 0 for 7–9 ms, −5 V for 9–11 ms. \(V_{out}\): +10 (0–1), +4 (1–3), +10 (3–4), −10 (4–5), −4 (5–7), −10 (7–8), +10 (8–9), +4 (9–11), +10 V (11–12 ms).

Show worked answer

\(V_{out}\) is constant where \(V_1\) is a straight line and changes sign with \(V_1\)'s slope: try \(V_{out} = K_1\dfrac{dV_1}{dt} + K_2V_2\).

\(dV_1/dt = \pm8\text{ V}/4\text{ ms} = \pm2\) V/ms \(= \pm2000\) V/s.

0–1 ms (\(V_2 = 0\)): \(10 = K_1\times2000 \Rightarrow K_1 = 5\) ms.

1–3 ms: \(4 = 10 + K_2(-5) \Rightarrow K_2 = 1.2\). Check 5–7 ms: \(-10 + 1.2\times5 = -4\) ✓; 9–11 ms: \(10 - 6 = 4\) ✓.

\[ V_{out} = (5\text{ ms})\frac{dV_1}{dt} + 1.2\,V_2 \]

Circuit (2 op-amps): an inverting summer that produces \(-V_{out}\), followed by a unity inverter. The summer (feedback \(R_f\) = 12 kΩ) has two branches: \(V_1\) through a capacitor \(C\) with \(R_fC = 5\) ms (\(C\) = 0.417 µF; or \(R_f\) = 50 kΩ with \(C\) = 0.1 µF and a matching \(V_2\) resistor), and \(V_2\) through \(R_2 = R_f/1.2\) = 10 kΩ. Summer output: \(-5\text{ ms}\cdot dV_1/dt - 1.2V_2\); after the inverter: \(V_{out}\). (With the 50 kΩ choice: \(R_2 = 50/1.2 = 41.7\) kΩ.)

Alternative reading on the paper: \(10\,\text{sgn}(dV_1/dt)\) from a comparator on the slope; the differentiator form above gives the same waveform and is the intended linear answer.

2015–16 · Sec B · Q5(c) · 12 marks

Input \(V_{in}\): triangle 0 → 4 V, peaks at 2 ms and 6 ms, zero at 0, 4, 8 ms. Output \(V_{out}\): +2 V for 0–2 ms, −2 V for 2–4 ms, repeating (period 4 ms). Design the circuit using op-amps.

Show worked answer

Piecewise-linear in, piecewise-constant out: differentiator. Slope \(= \pm4\text{ V}/2\text{ ms} = \pm2000\) V/s. \(V_{out} = +2\) V when the slope is positive, so \(K = +\dfrac{2}{2000} = +10^{-3}\) s.

Inverting differentiator with \(RC = 1\) ms (\(C\) = 0.1 µF, \(R\) = 10 kΩ) gives \(-10^{-3}\,dV_{in}/dt = \mp2\) V; follow it with a unity inverter (10 kΩ/10 kΩ) to get \(\pm2\) V in the right phase. Or put the inverter first (\(-V_{in}\)) and then the differentiator.

2014–15 · Sec B · Q5(c) · 15 marks

Design a circuit using op-amps that gives the output waveform from a 1 V DC input: \(v_o\) rises linearly from 0 at \(t\) = 0 to +15 V at \(t\) = 5 s and stays at 15 V. Specify all R and C values. Use ±15 V for DC biasing.

Show worked answer

DC in → ramp out: integrator. Required slope \(= 15/5 = 3\) V/s, positive.

Design A (two op-amps): integrator on the 1 V input: slope \(-1/RC = -3\) V/s, so \(RC = 1/3\) s: \(C\) = 1 µF, \(R\) = 333.3 kΩ. Output \(-3t\). Unity inverter (10 kΩ/10 kΩ): \(+3t\). At \(t\) = 5 s the integrator reaches −15 V (the rail) and stops, so \(v_o\) stays at +15 V, which is exactly the flat part of the waveform.

Design B (one op-amp): feed the integrator from the −15 V rail instead: slope \(= +15/RC = 3 \Rightarrow RC = 5\) s (\(C\) = 1 µF, \(R\) = 5 MΩ); the output saturates at +15 V at \(t\) = 5 s. Both need a reset switch across \(C\) so that \(v_o(0) = 0\).

Practice

Key terms

Quick check

Summary: integrator \(v_O = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t v_I\,d\tau + v_O(0)\): DC → ramp of slope \(-V/RC\) to the rail, square → triangle of p-p \(Vt_h/RC\), sine → \((V_p/\omega RC)(\cos\omega t - 1)\) when uncharged. Differentiator \(v_O = -RC\,dv_I/dt\): triangle → square, sine → \(-\omega RCV_p\cos\omega t\) with no DC term. From waveforms: find the slope, take the ratio, fix the sign with an inverter, pick \(C\), compute \(R\).
