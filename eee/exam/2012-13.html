Question 1

2012–13 · Sec A · Q1(a) · 16 marks

An npn transistor is biased as shown. Its \(I_C\)–\(V_{CB}\) characteristic is given in Fig. 1(b): piecewise linear; in the active region a straight line through (−0.5 V, 1 mA) rising at 20° to the horizontal for \(V_{CB} > -0.5\) V (drawn to about 2.5 V); in saturation a steep line from \(I_C\) = 0 at \(V_{CB}\) ≈ −1.1 V up to (−0.5 V, 1 mA). To find \(V_{CB}\) at the bias point we draw another \(I_C\)–\(V_{CB}\) plot involving the 2 kΩ collector resistance over the characteristic and find the intersection. (a) Express \(I_C\) as a function of \(V_{CB}\) involving the 2 kΩ resistance (a straight-line equation), along path-1: 5 V → 2 kΩ → collector–base junction → 1 V battery → ground.

Answer key

KVL along path-1: \(5 = 2I_C + V_{CB} + 1\) ⇒ \(I_C = (4 - V_{CB})/2 = 2 - 0.5V_{CB}\) (mA, V). Intercepts: 2 mA at \(V_{CB}\) = 0, 0 at \(V_{CB}\) = 4 V.

Find the mathematical equation describing the \(I_C\)–\(V_{CB}\) characteristic graph of the BJT (piecewise linear).

Question 2

2012–13 · Sec A · Q2(a) · 20 marks

The figure is the basic structure of a transconductance amplifier, to be implemented by a BJT. Given \(A\cdot v_{in} = I_C\cdot R_{out}\) (as printed), along with the facts that \(R_{sig} \to 0\) and the load \(R_L\) can be much greater than \(R_{out}\), which of the three BJT configurations (CE, CB, CC) will you choose for best performance? Explain why. Comment on its gain and loading effect.

Answer key
  • Ideal transconductance amplifier: \(R_{in} \to \infty\) (takes the full voltage) and \(R_{out} \to \infty\) (current independent of load); \(i_L = G_mv_{in}R_{out}/(R_{out} + R_L)\).
  • Choose CE: \(i_c = g_mv_{be}\) is voltage-in, current-out by nature. Its \(R_{in} = r_\pi\) is modest but \(R_{sig} \to 0\), so no input loss. Output is the collector (high resistance \(r_o\)).
  • CC (emitter follower) is a voltage buffer with low \(R_{out}\): wrong type. CB has \(R_{in} \approx r_e\) (needs a current drive) and current gain ≈ 1.
  • Loading: if \(R_L\) is comparable to or larger than \(R_{out}\), current divides into \(R_{out}\) and \(i_L\) falls. Emitter degeneration raises \(R_{out}\) to about \(r_o(1 + g_mR_e)\) and makes \(G_m \approx 1/R_e\): stable but smaller gain.

Full method: Common-emitter amplifier

2012–13 · Sec A · Q2(b) · 16 marks

Graphically explain the benefit of having \(R_E > 0\) in the biasing scheme shown.

Answer key
  • On the \(I_C\)–\(V_{BE}\) plane the bias condition is the line \(I_C \approx (V_B - V_{BE})/R_E\), slope \(-1/R_E\), crossing the exponential device curve at the Q-point.
  • \(R_E\) = 0: the line is vertical at \(V_{BE} = V_B\). A temperature rise shifts the exponential curve left (about −2 mV/°C) and \(I_C\) rises a lot.
  • \(R_E\) > 0: the sloped line meets the shifted curve at only a slightly higher \(I_C\). Draw both curves and both lines; the change \(\Delta I_C\) is much smaller.
  • Reason: negative feedback (\(I_C\uparrow \Rightarrow V_E\uparrow \Rightarrow V_{BE}\downarrow\)); also reduces sensitivity to \(I_S\) and β spread.

Full method: Bias circuits, stability and bias design

2012–13 · Sec A · Q2(c) · 10⅔ marks · no longer examined

Make a NAND and a NOR gate using BJTs.

Answer key

RTL: each input through \(R_B\) to an npn base, one shared \(R_C\) to \(V_{CC}\), output at the collector node. NOR: transistors in parallel (any input high pulls the output to \(V_{CE,sat}\)). NAND: transistors in series (only both high pull the output low, ≈ 0.4 V). Give truth tables; size \(R_B\) so the transistor saturates (\(I_B \ge I_{C,sat}/\beta_{min}\)).

Question 3

2012–13 · Sec A · Q3(a) · 5 marks

Why do we need to bias a BJT at a proper Q-point before using it as an amplifier?

Answer key
  • Keeps the BJT in the active region over the whole signal swing.
  • Q-point near the middle of the load line: maximum symmetrical swing; too high → saturation clipping, too low → cut-off clipping.
  • Linearisation about the Q-point: \(g_m = I_C/V_T\) and the gain depend on the bias; \(v_{be} \ll V_T\) for linearity.

Full method: Bias circuits, stability and bias design

2012–13 · Sec A · Q3(e) · 5 marks

If the BJT in active mode shows a linear \(I_C\)–\(V_{BE}\) behaviour instead of an exponential one, does the voltage gain change with the bias voltage \(V_{in}\)? Does the output voltage swing change with \(V_{in}\)? Explain why.

Answer key
  • Gain: no. \(A_v = -g_mR_C\) and with a linear law \(g_m = dI_C/dV_{BE}\) is a constant (here 50 mA/V, so \(A_v = -24\) V/V), whatever the bias.
  • Swing: yes. The available undistorted swing is the distance from the Q-point to cut-off on one side and to saturation on the other, which changes as \(V_{in}\) moves the Q-point.

2012–13 · Sec A · Q4(a) · 20 marks

An AC voltage source is applied to a series combination of a diode and a resistance. In Fig. 4 the dotted line is the input voltage waveform \(V_{ac}\) and the solid line is the output voltage across the resistance \(V_R\). Copy \(V_R\) exactly to scale and draw the voltage waveform across the diode on the same graph. Given \(dV_{ac}/dt\) = 1 V/s when \(V_{ac}\) is rising and −1 V/s when falling.

Fig. 4: \(V_{ac}\) is a triangle: 0 V at 0 s → +3 V at 3 s → 0 at 6 s → −3 V at 9 s → 0 at 12 s. \(V_R\) is 0 until 1 s, rises to a peak at 3 s, falls to 0 at 5 s; 0 from 5 s to 8 s; falls to a negative peak at 9 s, back to 0 at 10 s; 0 to 12 s.

Answer key

\(V_D = V_{ac} - V_R\). \(V_R\) starts at 1 s (when \(V_{ac}\) = 1 V) and stops at 5 s (\(V_{ac}\) = 1 V again): forward conduction begins at 1 V. Negative conduction runs 8–10 s, where \(V_{ac}\) = −2 V: reverse breakdown at −2 V. With the 1 V/s slope, \(V_R\) peaks at +2 V (3 s) and −1 V (9 s).

\(V_D\): follows \(V_{ac}\) from 0 to 1 V (0–1 s); held at +1 V (1–5 s); follows \(V_{ac}\) from 1 V down to −2 V (5–8 s); held at −2 V (8–10 s); follows \(V_{ac}\) from −2 V back to 0 (10–12 s).

Question-bank error

The Gemini question-bank answer reads the peaks of \(V_R\) as 2.2 V and −1.2 V off the typeset figure and concludes the diode voltage drops to 0.8 V and −1.8 V while conducting. That needs a negative-resistance diode; \(V_R\) cannot rise faster than \(V_{ac}\) in a passive series loop. Use +2 V / −1 V peaks and the +1 V / −2 V diode clamps.

Question 5

2012–13 · Sec B · Q5(a) · 12 marks

What are the mutual advantages and disadvantages of BJT and MOSFET?

Answer key
  • MOSFET: voltage controlled, almost infinite input resistance, small, near-ideal switch, CMOS has negligible static power, scales for VLSI; but lower \(g_m\) per current and ESD-sensitive gate oxide.
  • BJT: higher \(g_m = I_C/V_T\) per unit current, higher gain and better high-frequency analog performance; but base current loads the source, larger area, thermal runaway.

Full method: Structure and operation

2012–13 · Sec B · Q5(b) · 20⅔ marks

Explain the concept of threshold voltage (\(V_t\)) of a MOSFET. What happens to \(V_t\) and the oxide capacitance (\(C_{ox}\)) if (i) the dielectric thickness is increased/decreased? (ii) the gate insulator is replaced with a higher-k dielectric of the same thickness?

Answer key
  • \(V_t\): the gate voltage at which the surface under the gate is strongly inverted (electron density equals the bulk hole density), so a conducting channel joins source and drain. \(V_t = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + Q_{dep}/C_{ox}\), with \(C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox} = k\varepsilon_0/t_{ox}\).
  • (i) Thicker oxide: \(C_{ox}\) falls, \(V_t\) rises (needs more gate voltage). Thinner: \(C_{ox}\) rises, \(V_t\) falls.
  • (ii) Higher k, same thickness: \(C_{ox}\) rises in proportion to k, \(V_t\) falls and drive current (\(\propto \mu C_{ox}\)) rises; it also allows a physically thicker oxide for the same \(C_{ox}\), cutting tunnelling leakage.

Full method: Structure and operation

Question 6

2012–13 · Sec B · Q6(a) · 26⅔ marks

For the CS amplifier, derive the expressions for input resistance (\(R_{in}\)), output resistance (\(R_{out}\)), open-circuit voltage gain (\(A_{vo}\)) and overall voltage gain (\(G_v\)).

Answer key
  • Circuit: \(v_{sig}\), \(R_{sig}\) into the gate (bias network \(R_G\)), source at AC ground, \(R_D\) at the drain, load \(R_L\). Replace the MOSFET by \(g_mv_{gs}\) and \(r_o\).
  • Gate draws no current: \(R_{in} = R_G\) (e.g. \(R_{G1}\parallel R_{G2}\)).
  • \(v_o = -g_mv_{gs}(R_D\parallel r_o)\) with \(R_L\) removed: \(A_{vo} = -g_m(R_D\parallel r_o)\).
  • Set \(v_{sig}\) = 0 ⇒ \(v_{gs}\) = 0, source off: \(R_{out} = R_D\parallel r_o\).
  • \(G_v = \dfrac{R_{in}}{R_{in}+R_{sig}}\,[-g_m(R_D\parallel r_o\parallel R_L)]\) \(= \dfrac{R_{in}}{R_{in}+R_{sig}}A_{vo}\dfrac{R_L}{R_L+R_{out}}\).

Full method: Common-source amplifier

2012–13 · Sec B · Q6(b) · 20 marks

Explain graphically the benefit of using a resistance at the MOSFET source terminal. How does it fight the problem of unpredictable variation in process parameters such as \(V_t\), \(C_{ox}\), \(\mu_n\)?

Answer key
  • Draw two \(i_D\)–\(v_{GS}\) parabolas for the two extreme devices (different \(V_t\) and \(k_n'\)).
  • Fixed \(V_{GS}\) (vertical line): the two currents differ a lot.
  • With \(R_S\): bias line \(i_D = (V_G - v_{GS})/R_S\), slope \(-1/R_S\). Its two intersections give a much smaller \(\Delta I_D\).
  • Reason: negative feedback (\(I_D\uparrow \Rightarrow V_S\uparrow \Rightarrow V_{GS}\downarrow\)). Larger \(R_S\) (steeper line) gives better stability but uses more supply voltage.

Question 7

2012–13 · Sec B · Q7(a) · 19⅔ marks

Draw the voltage transfer characteristic of a MOSFET (amplifier). In which part of this graph is a MOSFET used as an amplifier and why? What are the benefits and drawbacks of increasing the bias voltage?

Answer key
  • CS stage with \(R_D\): cut-off (\(v_I < V_t\), \(v_O = V_{DD}\)); saturation (steep, nearly linear fall); triode (\(v_O\) small, flattening). Mark the saturation–triode edge at \(v_O = v_I - V_t\).
  • Amplify in saturation: steepest slope, \(A_v = -g_mR_D = -2(V_{DD} - V_{DS})/V_{OV}\).
  • Raising \(V_{GS}\): more \(I_D\) and \(g_m\) (more gain), but the Q-point moves toward the triode edge, less negative swing, more power. Best bias balances swing both ways.

Full method: Common-source amplifier

2012–13 · Sec B · Q7(b) · 12 marks

Explain graphically why, in a closed-loop feedback op-amp circuit, the differential voltage \(E_d\) is taken as zero.

Answer key
  • Open-loop VTC: \(v_o = AE_d\) in a narrow linear strip, clipped at ±L.
  • Strip width \(\pm L/A\): with L ≈ 13 V and A ≈ 2×10⁵, \(|E_d|\) ≤ 65 µV, negligible next to signal volts.
  • Negative feedback keeps the op-amp inside this strip, so \(E_d \approx 0\) (virtual short). Without feedback (comparator) this is not true.

Full method: The ideal op-amp; inverting and non-inverting

2012–13 · Sec B · Q7(c) · 15 marks

Draw a zero-crossing detector circuit using an op-amp and briefly describe its operation.

Answer key
  • Open-loop comparator: \(v_{in}\) to the + input, − input grounded. Output +L for \(v_{in} > 0\), −L for \(v_{in} < 0\): a sine becomes a square wave switching at each zero crossing. (Input to the − side gives the inverted version.)
  • Noise near 0 V causes multiple false transitions; fix with hysteresis (Schmitt trigger). Speed limited by slew rate.
  • Uses: sine-to-square, timing/phase measurement, triac firing.

Full method: Comparators and interfaces

Question 8

2012–13 · Sec B · Q8(a) · 17 marks

Draw a Wien-bridge oscillator circuit and describe its operating principle.

Answer key
  • Circuit: non-inverting amplifier (\(R_f\), \(R_1\)); lead-lag network: series RC from output to the + input, parallel RC from + to ground.
  • \(\beta = 1/[3 + j(\omega RC - 1/\omega RC)]\): phase 0° only at \(f_0 = 1/(2\pi RC)\), where β = 1/3.
  • Barkhausen: amplifier gain \(1 + R_f/R_1 = 3\) (\(R_f = 2R_1\)); slightly more for start-up, with diode limiters (or lamp/thermistor/JFET AGC) to hold the amplitude.

Full method: Wien-bridge oscillator

2012–13 · Sec B · Q8(b) · 16 marks

Design an op-amp circuit that performs \(v_{out} = 5v_1 - 2\,\dfrac{dv_2}{dt} + 6\displaystyle\int v_3\,dt\), where \(v_1\), \(v_2\), \(v_3\) are three input signals.

Answer key
  • Inverting amplifier 10 kΩ / 50 kΩ: \(-5v_1\).
  • Differentiator \(R_dC_d\) = 2 s (\(C_d\) = 10 µF, \(R_d\) = 200 kΩ): \(-2\dot v_2\); then a unity inverter: \(+2\dot v_2\).
  • Integrator \(R_iC_i\) = 1/6 s (\(C_i\) = 10 µF, \(R_i\) = 16.67 kΩ): \(-6\int v_3dt\).
  • Unity inverting summer (all 10 kΩ) of the three: \(-(-5v_1 + 2\dot v_2 - 6\int v_3) = 5v_1 - 2\dot v_2 + 6\int v_3dt\) ✓. Integrator capacitor starts discharged.

Full method: Building a given expression

2012–13 · Sec B · Q8(c) · 13⅔ marks

Derive the closed-loop gain of an inverting op-amp amplifier as a function of its open-loop gain A, given that A is not infinite.

Answer key

\(v_- = -v_o/A\). KCL at the − input: \((v_i - v_-)/R_1 = (v_- - v_o)/R_2\). Substituting and solving:

\[ G = \frac{v_o}{v_i} = \frac{-R_2/R_1}{1 + (1 + R_2/R_1)/A} \]

→ \(-R_2/R_1\) as A → ∞. Example: \(R_2/R_1\) = 100, A = 10⁴ gives G = −99.0 (1% low).

Full method: The ideal op-amp; inverting and non-inverting
