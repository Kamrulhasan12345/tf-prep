Question 1

2011–12 · Sec A · Q1(a) · 20 marks · no longer examined

Explain the operating principle of a Silicon Controlled Rectifier (SCR) with the help of the "two-transistor model".

Answer key
  • The SCR is a four-layer pnpn device (anode, gate on the inner p layer, cathode). Split it into a pnp transistor Q₁ and an npn transistor Q₂ sharing the middle layers: Q₁'s collector feeds Q₂'s base and Q₂'s collector feeds Q₁'s base.
  • Forward blocking: with the anode positive and no gate current, both transistors have small α and the loop gain is below 1; only leakage flows.
  • Regeneration: a gate current \(I_G\) is Q₂'s base current. \(I_{C2} = \alpha_2I_K\), \(I_{C1} = \alpha_1I_A\), \(I_K = I_A + I_G\). Then \(I_A = \dfrac{\alpha_2I_G + I_{CO}}{1 - (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)}\). As the current rises, α's rise, and when \(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \to 1\) the current grows without limit: both transistors saturate and the SCR latches on, with only a small drop (about 1 V) between anode and cathode.
  • Once on, the gate loses control. It turns off only when the anode current falls below the holding current (anode voltage reduced or reversed, or the AC current crosses zero).

2011–12 · Sec A · Q1(b) · 10 marks · no longer examined

Draw the I–V characteristics of an SCR and a TRIAC.

Answer key
  • SCR: axes \(I_A\) against \(V_{AK}\). Reverse region (\(V_{AK} < 0\)): reverse blocking, tiny leakage, breakdown at \(V_{BR}\). Forward region: forward blocking (almost zero current) until the forward breakover voltage \(V_{BO}\); then the curve snaps back to the on-state branch (steep line, about 1 V). The holding current \(I_H\) marks the lowest on-state current. Larger gate current \(I_G\) lowers \(V_{BO}\) (family of curves).
  • TRIAC: a bidirectional switch (two SCRs in antiparallel with one gate). The characteristic in the first quadrant is that of the SCR, and the third quadrant is the same curve rotated by 180° (breakover in either direction, gate triggers it with either polarity). It conducts both half-cycles of an AC supply.

Full method: Special diodes and thyristors

Sketch the output waveform of the circuit in Fig. for Q.1(c)(i), the output being taken across the resistance. The input waveform and the gate-pulse sequence are given in Fig. for Q.1(c)(ii).

Fig. 1(c)(i): \(v_{in}\) in series with an SCR (anode at the source side, gate driven by the pulse train) and a resistance R; \(v_o\) across R. Fig. 1(c)(ii): \(v_{in}\) is a sinusoid of 10 V peak and period 10 s (positive half 0–5 s, negative half 5–10 s). Gate pulses of unit height, narrow, at about t = 3.7 s and t = 8.7 s.

Answer key

Question 2

2011–12 · Sec A · Q2(a) · 16 marks

Draw the circuit diagram of a −20 dB/dec low-pass filter. Also write down the design procedure. Design a −20 dB/dec low-pass filter which has a cut-off frequency of 100 kHz.

Answer key

Circuit: inverting amplifier with \(R_1\) from the input to the − node and \(R_f\) in parallel with \(C_f\) in the feedback; + input grounded. \(H(s) = -\dfrac{R_f/R_1}{1 + sR_fC_f}\), DC gain \(-R_f/R_1\), \(f_c = \dfrac{1}{2\pi R_fC_f}\), one pole (−20 dB/dec above \(f_c\)).

Procedure: (1) choose \(C_f\) (for example 100 pF to 1 nF); (2) \(R_f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f_cC_f}\); (3) set the DC gain \(A_0\): \(R_1 = R_f/|A_0|\); (4) check that the op-amp's gain–bandwidth is well above \(f_c\times|A_0|\).

Design (unity gain, \(f_c = 100\) kHz): \(C_f = 100\) pF ⇒ \(R_f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\times10^5\times10^{-10}} = \mathbf{15.9}\) kΩ, \(R_1 = R_f = 15.9\) kΩ. (A non-inverting RC network feeding a voltage follower works too: \(C = 1\) nF, \(R = 1.59\) kΩ.)

Full method: Active filters

2011–12 · Sec A · Q2(b) · 10 marks

Write down the properties of an ideal op-amp.

Answer key
  • Infinite open-loop gain \(A \to \infty\) (so with negative feedback \(v_+ = v_-\), the virtual short).
  • Infinite input resistance (input currents \(i_+ = i_- = 0\)).
  • Zero output resistance (the output is an ideal voltage source).
  • Infinite bandwidth (gain independent of frequency), infinite slew rate.
  • Infinite CMRR: only the difference \(v_+ - v_-\) is amplified; zero input offset voltage and bias/offset currents; no noise.
  • Infinite output swing in the ideal case (a real one is limited by the supply rails).

Full method: The ideal op-amp; inverting and non-inverting

  • Summer, \(R_f = 100\) kΩ: \(V_1\) through 25 kΩ (×4), \(v_d\) through 100 kΩ (×1), \(u\) through 50 kΩ (×2), \(v_i\) through 100 kΩ (×1).

\(V_{out} = -[4V_1 + (-7\dot V_1) + 2(-V_2) + 8\int V_2] = -4V_1 + 7\dot V_1 + 2V_2 - 8\int V_2\) ✓. Four op-amps. (The RC = 7 s is large because the coefficient 7 is in seconds; scale time if needed.)

Question 3

2011–12 · Sec A · Q3(a) · 26⅔ marks

Draw the circuit diagram of an inverting logarithmic amplifier and derive the expression of its output voltage.

Answer key

R from \(v_{in}\) to the inverting input; a diode in the feedback path with its anode at the inverting input and its cathode at the output; + input grounded.

Virtual ground: \(v_- = 0\), so the input current \(i = v_{in}/R\) (needs \(v_{in} > 0\)) flows into the diode. \(i_D = I_S\,e^{v_D/(nV_T)}\) with \(v_D = v_- - v_o = -v_o\):

\[ \frac{v_{in}}{R} = I_Se^{-v_o/(nV_T)} \;\Rightarrow\; v_o = -nV_T\ln\frac{v_{in}}{I_SR}. \]

Valid for \(v_{in} > 0\) and \(i_D \gg I_S\); the output is only a few hundred mV (diode drop), and depends on temperature through \(V_T\) and \(I_S\). With a BJT in the feedback (collector at the − node, base grounded) the same expression holds over a wider current range.

Full method: Log, antilog and nonlinear circuits

2011–12 · Sec B · Q7(b) · 12⅔ marks

For Fig. for Q.7(b), find out the maximum collector current that the device can support. If the operating point is set for that maximum current, what kind of problem will we face if we want to use the device as a linear amplifier?

Fig. for Q. 7(b): a DC load line on \(i_C\) (mA) versus \(v_{CE}\) (V) axes, from \(i_C\) = 8 mA at \(v_{CE} = 0\) to \(v_{CE} = 20\) V at \(i_C\) = 0.

Answer key

The load line limits the current: the largest collector current is at the saturation end, \(v_{CE} \approx 0\): \(I_{C,max} = 8\) mA (7.9 mA with \(V_{CE,sat} = 0.2\) V). The slope gives \(R_C + R_E = 20/8 = 2.5\) kΩ.

Q-point at 8 mA: the transistor sits at the edge of saturation (\(v_{CE} \approx 0\)). Any increase of the input drive cannot raise \(i_C\) further and \(v_{CE}\) cannot fall below \(V_{CE,sat}\): the positive half of the current swing is clipped (saturation distortion), and in saturation the BC junction is forward biased so the gain collapses. There is no room for output swing in that direction: the amplifier is not linear. The best linear operating point is near the middle of the load line (about 4 mA, \(v_{CE} = 10\) V), which allows the largest symmetrical swing.

Question 8

2011–12 · Sec B · Q8(a) · 10⅔ marks

Why is biasing needed for an amplifier using a BJT? Draw the output characteristics of a BJT connected in CE configuration. Define Early effect.

Answer key
  • Why bias: the BJT must stay in the active region for the whole signal swing (otherwise it cuts off or saturates and the output is clipped); the DC Q-point sets \(I_C\), so \(g_m = I_C/V_T\), \(r_\pi\) and the gain; a good bias network (an emitter resistor and a divider) keeps \(I_C\) constant against changes of β, \(V_{BE}\) and temperature.
  • Output characteristics (CE): \(i_C\) against \(v_{CE}\), one curve per base current \(i_B\). Each curve rises steeply (saturation, \(v_{CE} < V_{CE,sat}\)) then runs nearly flat in the active region with a slight upward slope; \(i_B = 0\) gives the cutoff curve (leakage \(I_{CEO}\)); the curves are spaced by \(\beta\,\Delta i_B\).
  • Early effect: as \(v_{CE}\) rises the collector–base depletion layer widens and the neutral base narrows, so \(i_C\) increases slightly: \(i_C = I_Se^{v_{BE}/V_T}(1 + v_{CE}/V_A)\). Extending the active-region lines backward, they meet the \(v_{CE}\) axis at the same point \(-V_A\) (Early voltage, 50–100 V). Output resistance \(r_o = V_A/I_C\).
