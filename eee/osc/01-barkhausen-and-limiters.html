The Barkhausen Criterion and Amplitude Limiters

"Write down the Barkhausen criterion" was asked on its own in 2014–15 and 2018–19 (6 to 11 marks), and it is part (a) of almost every oscillator derivation since: Wien 2023–24 and 2019–20, Colpitts 2022–23 and 2024–25. Learn the loop-gain idea and the three-step method here once; every later chapter (Wien, phase shift, Colpitts) is the same three steps on a different network. The diode limiter that holds the amplitude steady is the second half.

1. Feedback: negative, positive, and the loop gain

An amplifier \(A\) and a feedback network \(\beta\) form a loop. The signal that comes back is \(x_f = \beta x_o\). What happens depends on whether it is subtracted or added at the input (Sadat notes p.112–113):

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{negative feedback: } A_f = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta}, \qquad \text{positive feedback: } A_f = \frac{A}{1 - A\beta} \\ L \equiv A\beta \text{ (loop gain)} \end{gathered} \]
Loop gain \(L(s) = A(s)\beta(s)\)
The gain all the way round the loop: break the loop at the amplifier input, inject \(V_i\), and see what comes back. It is a complex number at each frequency.
Desensitivity \(D = 1 + A\beta\)
The factor by which negative feedback reduces gain and the sensitivity to changes in \(A\).
Characteristic equation
\(1 - L(s) = 0\) for the positive-feedback loop. Its roots are the closed-loop poles.

Desensitivity (2014–15)

Differentiate \(A_f = A/(1 + A\beta)\) with respect to \(A\):

\[ \frac{dA_f}{dA} = \frac{(1 + A\beta) - A\beta}{(1 + A\beta)^2} = \frac{1}{(1+A\beta)^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dA_f}{A_f} = \frac{1}{1 + A\beta}\cdot\frac{dA}{A} \]

So a 10% change in \(A\) becomes a \(10\%/D\) change in \(A_f\). With negative feedback \(A\beta > 0\), \(D > 1\): the gain is stable, the bandwidth widens, distortion falls. With positive feedback the denominator is \(1 - A\beta\), i.e. \(D < 1\): the closed-loop gain is more sensitive than \(A\), and when \(A\beta \to 1\) (\(D \to 0\)) the gain goes to infinity. A finite output with zero input: that is an oscillator.

2. The Barkhausen criterion

With no input, the loop must reproduce its own signal: \(x_o = A x_f = A\beta x_o\), so \(A\beta = 1\). Since \(A\beta\) is complex, this is two conditions at the oscillation frequency \(\omega_0\) (Khairul s.3–4):

\[ L(j\omega_0) = A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 1 \iff \big|A\beta\big| = 1 \;\text{ and }\; \angle A\beta = 0^\circ \;(\text{or } 360^\circ) \]
  • Phase condition sets the frequency. The loop oscillates at the one frequency where the total phase shift round the loop is 0° (360°). If several frequencies satisfied it, the output would not be a pure sine (Sadat p.114: frequencies that fail the criterion die out).
  • Magnitude condition sets the gain. \(|A\beta| < 1\): the oscillation decays. \(|A\beta| = 1\): it is sustained. \(|A\beta| > 1\): it grows.
  • Start-up: design \(|A\beta|\) slightly greater than 1 so that noise grows into an oscillation. The closed-loop poles are then just inside the right half-plane. The amplitude then grows until something non-linear (op-amp saturation, a diode limiter, a thermistor or JFET gain control) pulls the effective loop gain back to exactly 1. Without a limiter the sine saturates at the supply and looks squarish (Sadat p.116).

3. The three-step method (every oscillator in this book)

Method · find ω₀ and the start condition (Khairul s.5)

  1. Break the loop at the amplifier input. Write \(A\) (non-inverting op-amp: \(1 + R_2/R_1\); inverting: \(-R_2/R_1\)) and \(\beta(j\omega) = V_f/V_o\) of the network by voltage division or nodal analysis (ideal op-amp input draws no current).
  2. Phase condition → \(\omega_0\). Write \(A\beta\) as (numerator)/(real part + j·imaginary part). If the numerator is purely imaginary (like \(j\omega RL\)), set the real part of the denominator to zero; if the numerator is real, set the imaginary part to zero. Equivalent shortcut: write \(1/\beta\) and set \(\text{Im}(1/\beta) = 0\) for a non-inverting amplifier (or require \(\beta\) real and negative for an inverting one).
  3. Magnitude → gain condition. Substitute \(\omega_0\): \(\beta(\omega_0)\) is now real. Require \(A\beta(\omega_0) \ge 1\) (start), "= 1" (sustain). Solve for the resistor ratio.

Worked example: the LCR band-pass oscillator (Sedra Ex. 18.1, Khairul s.6–8, Sadat p.115)

A non-inverting amplifier (\(r_1\) to ground, \(r_2\) feedback) drives a resistor \(R\) into the + input; \(L\) and \(C\) in parallel go from the + input to ground. The parallel LC is a band-pass filter: it passes only frequencies near \(1/\sqrt{LC}\).

Derivation as an exam answer:

\[ Z_{LC} = \frac{1}{sC}\parallel sL = \frac{sL}{1 + s^2LC} \]
\[ \beta(s) = \frac{Z_{LC}}{R + Z_{LC}} = \frac{sL}{s^2RLC + sL + R} = \frac{s/RC}{s^2 + s/RC + 1/LC} \]
\[ A(j\omega)\beta(j\omega) = \Big(1 + \frac{r_2}{r_1}\Big)\frac{j\omega/RC}{\big(\tfrac{1}{LC} - \omega^2\big) + j\omega/RC} \]

The numerator is imaginary, so the phase is zero when the real part of the denominator is zero: \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). There \(\beta(\omega_0) = 1\) (the filter passes the centre frequency at full amplitude), so \(|A\beta| = 1 + r_2/r_1 \ge 1\), i.e. \(r_2/r_1 \ge 0\): any non-negative feedback ratio starts it. In Sadat's words, ideally \(R_2 = 0\) (a follower); in practice any \(R_2\) makes \(A\beta > 1\) and the output grows until it saturates.

The closed-loop poles show the growth. \(1 - L(s) = 0\) gives \(s^2 + s\,\dfrac{1 - K}{RC} + \dfrac{1}{LC} = 0\) with \(K = 1 + r_2/r_1\). For \(K\) slightly above 1 the poles are \(s \approx \sigma \pm j\omega_0\) with \(\sigma = \dfrac{K - 1}{2RC} > 0\): the envelope grows as \(e^{\sigma t}\).

4. Amplitude control: the diode limiter

A loop gain slightly above 1 makes the amplitude grow without bound until the op-amp saturates (distorted). A limiter makes the gain drop smoothly once the output reaches a set level \(L_+\) or \(L_-\). Sedra Fig. 18.4 (Khairul s.9–13) is the standard one.

How it works. With both diodes off, \(v_O = -(R_f/R_1)v_I\). The string nodes follow \(v_O\):

\[ v_A = V\frac{R_3}{R_2 + R_3} + v_O\frac{R_2}{R_2 + R_3}, \qquad v_B = -V\frac{R_4}{R_4 + R_5} + v_O\frac{R_5}{R_4 + R_5} \]

As \(v_O\) goes negative, \(v_A\) falls. When \(v_A = -V_D\) (the virtual-ground node is at 0), \(D_1\) conducts. Setting \(v_A = -V_D\) and solving for \(v_O\) gives the negative limiting level; \(D_2\) does the same for positive \(v_O\) when \(v_B = +V_D\):

\[ L_- = -V\frac{R_3}{R_2} - V_D\Big(1 + \frac{R_3}{R_2}\Big), \qquad L_+ = V\frac{R_4}{R_5} + V_D\Big(1 + \frac{R_4}{R_5}\Big) \]

Beyond a limit, \(v_A\) is pinned at \(-V_D\), so the current in \(R_2\) is constant and every extra bit of current goes through \(R_3\): \(R_3\) appears in parallel with \(R_f\). Slopes of the transfer characteristic:

\[ \text{linear: } -\frac{R_f}{R_1}, \qquad \text{below } L_-: \; -\frac{R_f\parallel R_3}{R_1}, \qquad \text{above } L_+: \; -\frac{R_f\parallel R_4}{R_1} \]

Remove \(R_f\) and the gain becomes huge: the same circuit is a comparator with clean output levels \(L_\pm\) (used later for Schmitt triggers).

Method · limiter levels and slopes

  1. \(L_- = -V R_3/R_2 - V_D(1 + R_3/R_2)\) and \(L_+ = V R_4/R_5 + V_D(1 + R_4/R_5)\).
  2. Input where limiting starts: divide by the linear gain, \(v_I = L/(-R_f/R_1)\).
  3. Limiting slopes: replace \(R_f\) by \(R_f\parallel R_3\) (negative side) or \(R_f\parallel R_4\) (positive side).
  4. In an oscillator, the diodes hang on the feedback divider of the amplifier instead of the virtual ground (Wien example in the next chapter); the turn-on condition is the same idea: diode node = other node ± \(V_D\).

Traps (where marks go)

  • Writing only "\(A\beta = 1\)". Write both parts: \(|A\beta| = 1\) and \(\angle A\beta = 0^\circ\) (360°), and add the practical start-up remark (\(|A\beta|\) slightly > 1, then non-linear amplitude control). That remark is a mark.
  • Mixing sign conventions: in the positive-feedback form the criterion is \(A\beta = +1\); in the negative-feedback form \(A_f = A/(1 + A\beta)\) it is \(A\beta = -1\). State which one you use.
  • Setting the wrong part of the denominator to zero. Check the numerator first: imaginary numerator → real part of denominator = 0.
  • Limiter levels: \(R_3/R_2\) (not \(R_3/(R_2 + R_3)\)), and the \(V_D\) term is multiplied by \(1 + R_3/R_2\).
  • Desensitivity: \(dA_f/A_f = (dA/A)/(1 + A\beta)\); do not forget it is a relative change.

Exam questions

2018–19 · Sec B · Q6(c) · 10⅔ marks  |  also part (a) of 2023–24 Sec B Q5(a) and 2019–20 Sec B Q7(a)(i)

Write down the Barkhausen criterion.

Show worked answer

Draw Figure 1: amplifier \(A(s)\) with a frequency-selective feedback network \(\beta(s)\) connected for positive feedback. Closed-loop gain \(A_f = A/(1 - A\beta)\); loop gain \(L(s) = A(s)\beta(s)\).

If at some frequency \(\omega_0\) the loop gain equals 1, \(A_f \to \infty\): the circuit gives a finite output with zero input, i.e. it oscillates. With no input, \(x_o = A\beta x_o\), so \(A\beta = 1\).

Barkhausen criterion: \(L(j\omega_0) = A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 1\), i.e. (i) magnitude \(|A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0)| = 1\); (ii) phase \(\angle A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 0^\circ\) or \(360^\circ\). The phase condition fixes \(\omega_0\) (it must hold at one frequency only, for a pure sine); the magnitude condition fixes the amplifier gain.

Practical note: if \(|A\beta| < 1\) oscillations die out; if \(|A\beta| > 1\) they grow. A real oscillator is designed with \(|A\beta|\) slightly above 1 (poles slightly in the right half-plane) so it starts from noise, and a non-linear mechanism (op-amp saturation or a diode limiter) reduces the effective gain until \(|A\beta| = 1\) at the final amplitude.

2014–15 · Sec B · Q8(b) · 15 marks (10 + 5)

Derive the expression of desensitivity. Explain positive and negative feedback from desensitivity.

Show worked answer

Derivation (10). Negative-feedback amplifier: \(x_o = A(x_s - \beta x_o)\), so \(A_f = \dfrac{A}{1 + A\beta}\). Differentiate with respect to \(A\):

\(\dfrac{dA_f}{dA} = \dfrac{1}{(1 + A\beta)^2}\). Divide by \(A_f = A/(1 + A\beta)\): \(\dfrac{dA_f}{A_f} = \dfrac{1}{1 + A\beta}\cdot\dfrac{dA}{A}\).

The factor \(D = 1 + A\beta\) is the desensitivity factor: a fractional change in the open-loop gain is reduced by \(D\) in the closed-loop gain. (Sensitivity \(S = 1/D\).) Example: \(A = 10^4\), \(\beta = 0.01\): \(D = 101\), \(A_f = 99.0\); a 10% drop in \(A\) changes \(A_f\) by only \(10\%/101 \approx 0.1\%\).

Explanation (5). Negative feedback: \(A\beta > 0\), \(D > 1\). Gain is reduced but becomes insensitive to \(A\) (transistor, temperature), bandwidth increases and distortion falls by the same factor. Positive feedback: the fed-back signal adds, \(A_f = A/(1 - A\beta)\), so effectively \(D = 1 - A\beta < 1\): the gain increases and becomes more sensitive to \(A\). As \(A\beta \to 1\), \(D \to 0\) and \(A_f \to \infty\): output without input, which is the Barkhausen (oscillation) condition. Hence amplifiers use negative feedback; oscillators use positive feedback.

2014–15 · Sec B · Q7(c) · 6⅔ marks

Write down the Barkhausen stability criterion for an oscillation in a linear electronic circuit to sustain.

Show worked answer

For a loop with amplifier \(A(s)\) and feedback network \(\beta(s)\) (positive feedback), the characteristic equation is \(1 - A(s)\beta(s) = 0\). Sustained oscillation at \(\omega_0\) needs a pair of closed-loop poles exactly on the \(j\omega\) axis at \(\pm j\omega_0\), i.e.

\(A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 1\): \(|A\beta| = 1\) and \(\angle A\beta = 0^\circ\) (or an integer multiple of \(360^\circ\)).

Poles in the left half-plane (\(|A\beta| < 1\)) give a decaying response; poles in the right half-plane (\(|A\beta| > 1\)) give a growing one. In practice the poles are placed slightly in the right half-plane for reliable start-up, and amplitude control (limiter, AGC, saturation) brings them back to the \(j\omega\) axis.

Quick check

Summary: oscillation needs \(A\beta = 1\): magnitude 1 and phase 0° at one frequency \(\omega_0\). Method: break the loop, write \(A\) and \(\beta(j\omega)\), set the phase to zero to get \(\omega_0\), then require \(A\beta(\omega_0) \ge 1\) for the gain. Design slightly above 1 so it starts; a limiter (\(L_\pm = \pm V R_3/R_2 \pm V_D(1 + R_3/R_2)\)) holds the amplitude. Desensitivity \(1 + A\beta\) explains why negative feedback stabilises and positive feedback oscillates.
