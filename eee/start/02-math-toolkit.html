The Maths Toolkit

Every EEE 263 numerical is built from about ten small moves: a divider, a Thevenin equivalent, a KVL loop, a log, a quadratic, an assume-and-check, a DC/AC split, a phasor, an integral. Get these automatic and the big questions become a chain of small ones. Each section ends with a practice box that generates fresh numbers every time.

1. Work in mA, kΩ, V and mS

Most marks are lost to powers of ten, not to physics. Pick one unit system and never leave it:

\[ \text{V} = \text{mA} \times \text{k}\Omega, \qquad \text{mA} = \frac{\text{V}}{\text{k}\Omega}, \qquad g_m\,[\text{mA/V} = \text{mS}] \times R\,[\text{k}\Omega] = \text{gain (V/V)} \]

So a 2.2 kΩ resistor carrying 1.5 mA drops 3.3 V, with no \(10^{-3}\) anywhere. A transconductance of 40 mA/V into 5 kΩ gives a gain of 200. Write units on every line of the exam script; examiners give method marks for lines they can follow.

2. Dividers, parallel resistors and Thevenin

The base of a BJT or the gate of a MOSFET is almost always fed by a two-resistor divider. Replace it by its Thevenin equivalent before doing anything else:

\[ V_{TH} = V_{CC}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}, \qquad R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2} \]

\(R_1\) is the resistor to the supply, \(R_2\) the resistor to ground. For a MOSFET no gate current flows, so the gate voltage is just \(V_{TH}\). For a BJT the base current flows through \(R_{TH}\) and drops \(I_B R_{TH}\); that is why you need the Thevenin form.

3. KVL around the base–emitter loop

The one equation that solves almost every BJT bias question. Walk from the Thevenin source through \(R_{TH}\), across \(V_{BE}\), down through \(R_E\) to ground:

\[ V_{TH} = I_B R_{TH} + V_{BE} + I_E R_E, \quad I_E = (\beta+1) I_B \;\Rightarrow\; I_E = \frac{V_{TH}-V_{BE}}{R_E + R_{TH}/(\beta+1)} \]

Read it as: the base resistance, seen from the emitter, shrinks by \((\beta+1)\). The same "reflection" rule gives the input resistance of an emitter follower in the opposite direction: emitter resistance seen from the base grows by \((\beta+1)\).

4. Exponentials and logs

The diode and BJT laws are exponential, \(I = I_S e^{V/V_T}\). Two forms come up again and again:

\[ V_2 - V_1 = V_T \ln\frac{I_2}{I_1} = 2.3\,V_T \log_{10}\frac{I_2}{I_1} \]

With \(V_T = 25\) mV and \(n = 1\), \(2.3V_T \approx 60\) mV: a tenfold change in current changes the voltage by about 60 mV. With ideality factor \(n\), multiply by \(n\). Watch which log your calculator uses: ln is natural, log is base 10.

5. Quadratics: keep the physical root

A MOSFET with a source resistor gives a quadratic, because the current depends on \(V_{GS}\) squared and \(V_{GS}\) depends on the current. With \(V_{OV} = V_{GS} - V_t\) and \(V_G\) fixed:

\[ V_G = V_{GS} + I_D R_S, \quad I_D = \tfrac12 k_n' \tfrac{W}{L} V_{OV}^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \tfrac12 k_n R_S\,V_{OV}^2 + V_{OV} - (V_G - V_t) = 0 \]

Two roots appear. One gives \(V_{OV} < 0\) (that is \(V_{GS} < V_t\): the transistor would be off, so no current could flow). Reject it and keep the positive root. Say so in one line on the script; it is worth a mark. On a Casio fx-991, use MODE → EQN → aX²+bX+c.

6. Assume, solve, verify

Nonlinear devices have regions (diode on/off, BJT active/saturation/cutoff, MOSFET saturation/triode/cutoff). You never know the region in advance. The method is always the same:

Method · assume and verify

  1. Guess the region (diode on; BJT active; MOSFET saturation is the usual first guess).
  2. Replace the device by that region's model and solve the linear circuit.
  3. Check the condition the guess requires: diode current \(> 0\) (or reverse voltage for an off diode \(< V_{D0}\)); BJT \(V_{CE} > 0.2\) V (or \(V_{CB} \ge 0\) for strict active); MOSFET \(V_{DS} \ge V_{OV}\).
  4. If the check fails, change the guess and solve again. State the check explicitly in the answer.

7. Split DC from AC

An amplifier question is two problems glued together. Solve them separately (superposition):

  • DC (bias): capacitors are open circuits, signal sources are zero. Find \(I_C\) or \(I_D\).
  • AC (small signal): large coupling and bypass capacitors are short circuits, DC supplies become ground. Replace the transistor by its small-signal model with parameters computed from the DC current.

The DC answer feeds the AC answer: \(g_m = I_C/V_T\) (BJT) or \(g_m = 2I_D/V_{OV}\) (MOSFET). If the bias is wrong, every gain after it is wrong, so check the bias twice.

8. Phasors for oscillators and filters

Capacitor impedance \(1/(j\omega C)\), inductor \(j\omega L\). An oscillator runs at the frequency where the loop gain \(L(j\omega) = A\beta\) is purely real, and it starts when that real gain is at least 1 (Barkhausen). The recipe:

  1. Write the feedback network's transfer function \(\beta(j\omega)\) as one fraction.
  2. Set its imaginary part (or the imaginary part of the denominator) to zero: this gives \(\omega_0\).
  3. Put \(\omega_0\) back in to get \(\beta(\omega_0)\); the amplifier needs \(A \ge 1/\beta(\omega_0)\).

For a first-order RC section: \(\dfrac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}\) (low-pass) and \(\dfrac{j\omega RC}{1 + j\omega RC}\) (high-pass). The corner is \(f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)\), where the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt2\) (−3 dB) and the phase is ∓45°.

9. Integrals for op-amp waveforms

An inverting integrator gives \(v_O(t) = -\dfrac{1}{RC}\displaystyle\int v_I\,dt + v_O(0)\). A constant input makes a ramp of slope \(-V_I/(RC)\) V/s; a square wave makes a triangle whose peak-to-peak value is \(\dfrac{V_I}{RC}\cdot\dfrac{T}{2}\). A differentiator gives \(v_O = -RC\,dv_I/dt\): a triangle in, a square out.

10. Calculator moves that save minutes

  • SOLVE (fx-991: SHIFT CALC) for transcendental equations like \(5 = V_D/1 + 10^{-13}e^{V_D/0.025}\). Give a starting guess of 0.7.
  • EQN mode for quadratics and 2×2 / 3×3 linear systems (node equations).
  • TABLE mode to tabulate a transfer characteristic or a diode current for several inputs.
  • Store intermediate results in memories (A, B, C …) instead of retyping rounded values: rounding early is how a correct method ends with a wrong final number.
