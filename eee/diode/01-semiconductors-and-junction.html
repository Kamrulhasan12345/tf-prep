Semiconductors and the pn Junction

No recent term final has a stand-alone question on band theory: since 2022–23 theory gets 7 to 17 marks and the numericals take the rest. But this chapter is the vocabulary of every later one, class tests ask true/false and fill-in-the-gap items on it (batch 23, CT-1), and the old papers (2012–13 Q4, 46⅔ marks; 2013–14 Q1) built whole questions on reading a diode's I–V curve: where it starts conducting forward and where it breaks down in reverse. Learn the six definitions, the built-in potential formula, the three regions of the I–V curve, and how to turn a curve into a waveform.

1. Energy bands: conductor, semiconductor, insulator

In a solid the discrete energy levels of single atoms spread into bands. Two of them decide conduction.

Valence band (energy top \(E_V\))
The highest band that is full of electrons at 0 K. Electrons here are held in covalent bonds.
Conduction band (energy bottom \(E_C\))
The lowest band of empty states. An electron here is free to move and carry current.
Band gap \(E_g = E_C - E_V\)
The forbidden energy range between them. Conductor: no gap (the bands overlap). Semiconductor: small gap, about 1.1 eV for silicon (Sadat writes 1.1 eV, Khairul's slide 1 eV). Insulator: large gap, above about 5–6 eV, so heat cannot lift electrons across.

At 0 K a semiconductor's valence band is full and its conduction band empty: it does not conduct. Above 0 K thermal energy lifts a few electrons across the gap, leaving a hole (an empty bond) in the valence band. Both carry current.

2. Intrinsic and extrinsic silicon

Intrinsic
Pure silicon. Every broken bond gives one free electron and one hole, so \(n = p = n_i\) (\(n_i \approx 1.5\times10^{10}\) cm⁻³ at 300 K). Recombination: a free electron falls into a hole and both disappear.
n-type
Doped with a pentavalent donor (phosphorus). The fifth electron is free; the donor becomes an immobile positive ion. Electrons are the majority carriers: \(n_n \approx N_D\). Donor levels sit just below \(E_C\).
p-type
Doped with a trivalent acceptor (boron, aluminium). It takes an electron from a neighbour bond, creating a hole; the acceptor becomes an immobile negative ion. Holes are the majority: \(p_p \approx N_A\). Acceptor levels sit just above \(E_V\).
Mass action law
In equilibrium \(np = n_i^2\) whatever the doping. So the minority concentration is \(p_n = n_i^2/N_D\) (n-type) or \(n_p = n_i^2/N_A\) (p-type).

Method · carrier concentrations

  1. Majority = doping: \(n_n = N_D\) or \(p_p = N_A\).
  2. Minority = \(n_i^2\) / majority.
  3. Doping by one part in \(10^6\) (about \(5\times10^{16}\) cm⁻³ in Si) raises the majority concentration by a factor of millions over \(n_i\): that is why doping, not temperature, sets the conductivity.

3. Drift and diffusion

Drift current
Carriers moved by an electric field: holes along the field, electrons against it. Both give current in the field direction.
Diffusion current
Carriers moving from a region of high concentration to low concentration, with no field needed. A concentration gradient is enough.

4. The pn junction with no bias

Write the formation as a numbered sequence; this is how an exam answer should read (Sadat L2, Khairul s.11–12):

  1. Holes are plentiful on the p side and electrons on the n side, so holes diffuse into n and electrons into p.
  2. They recombine near the junction. Left behind are the immobile ions: negative on the p side, positive on the n side. This carrier-free zone is the depletion region.
  3. The ion charge sets up an electric field pointing from n to p, and a potential step \(V_0\) (the barrier or built-in voltage).
  4. The field pushes minority carriers across (drift, \(I_S\), n→p) and opposes further diffusion (\(I_D\), p→n). Equilibrium: \(I_D = I_S\), zero net current.
\[ V_0 = V_T\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2},\qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 25.9\text{ mV at 300 K} \]

\(V_0\) is typically 0.6 to 0.8 V for silicon. It cannot be measured with a voltmeter across an open diode: the contact potentials at the metal ends cancel it. The depletion width (Sedra §3.4) is \(W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)V_0}\); it lies mostly in the lightly doped side, because both sides must uncover equal charge: \(x_pN_A = x_nN_D\).

Method · built-in voltage

  1. \(V_T = kT/q\) with \(k = 1.38\times10^{-23}\) J/K, \(q = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C, T in kelvin (Sedra uses 25.9 mV at 300 K here).
  2. \(V_0 = V_T\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)\). Keep the units of \(N\) and \(n_i\) the same (cm⁻³).
  3. Reverse bias \(V_R\) raises the barrier to \(V_0 + V_R\); forward bias \(V_F\) lowers it to \(V_0 - V_F\).

Worked example: a silicon junction (Sedra §3.4 numbers)

5. Forward and reverse bias

Forward bias (+ on p)
Carriers are pushed toward the junction: the depletion region narrows, the barrier falls to \(V_0 - V_F\), diffusion current grows exponentially. Above the cut-in voltage (about 0.5 V) current is noticeable; at 0.6–0.8 V the diode conducts fully.
Reverse bias (+ on n)
Carriers are pulled away: the depletion region widens and the field grows, the barrier rises to \(V_0 + V_R\). Diffusion stops; only the tiny minority drift current \(I_S\) (plus leakage) flows.
Breakdown
At a large reverse voltage \(V_{ZK}\) the current rises sharply at almost constant voltage. Zener effect (heavily doped, thin depletion region, below about 5 V): the field rips electrons out of bonds (tunnelling); negative temperature coefficient. Avalanche (above about 7 V): minority carriers gain enough kinetic energy to knock out more carriers by collision, which multiplies; positive temperature coefficient. Breakdown is not destructive if the power is limited (Zener diodes use it, Chapter 7).

6. The I–V characteristic

\[ i = I_S\left(e^{v/(nV_T)} - 1\right) \]
  • \(I_S\) (saturation or scale current) is tiny, about \(10^{-15}\) A for a small diode, proportional to the junction area, and doubles for every 5 °C rise (Chapter 6).
  • \(n\) is 1 to 2 (take 1 unless told); \(V_T\) = 25 mV in Sedra's examples, 25.9 mV in Sadat sir's notes.
  • Forward (\(v \gg V_T\)): drop the −1, \(i \approx I_Se^{v/nV_T}\). Reverse (\(v \ll -V_T\)): \(i \approx -I_S\).
  • Every chapter after this replaces the curve by something simpler: a switch, a 0.7 V battery, a battery plus resistor (Chapter 2), the exponential itself (Chapter 4), or a small resistance \(r_d\) (Chapter 5).

7. From an I–V curve to a waveform (type D-12)

When a paper gives the diode's I–V as a graph made of straight lines, read off three numbers and you have a model: the forward turn-on voltage \(V_{on}\), the forward slope (its inverse is \(r_D\)), and the reverse breakdown voltage \(V_{BR}\). With a resistor \(R\) in series and input \(v_{in}\):

Method · series diode with a piecewise-linear curve

  1. \(v_{in} > V_{on}\): forward conduction, \(v_R = (v_{in} - V_{on})\dfrac{R}{R + r_D}\).
  2. \(v_{in} < -V_{BR}\): breakdown, \(v_R = (v_{in} + V_{BR})\dfrac{R}{R + r_{BR}}\) (usually \(r_{BR} = 0\)).
  3. Otherwise the diode is open: \(v_R = 0\).
  4. Diode voltage \(v_D = v_{in} - v_R\): it follows the input while the diode is off and is held at \(V_{on}\) (or \(-V_{BR}\)) while it conducts with \(r_D = 0\).
  5. Breakpoints in time: for a ramp of slope \(s\), \(t = V/s\); for a sine, \(\omega t = \sin^{-1}(V/V_m)\).
  6. Reverse task (2012–13): read \(V_{on}\) and \(V_{BR}\) as the values of \(v_D\) where \(v_R\) leaves zero.

Theory answers to have ready

Short, numbered answers score best. Typical asks and the points to write:

  • Why is a semiconductor different from a conductor? Small band gap; conductivity set by doping; two carrier types (electrons and holes); conductivity rises with temperature (a metal's falls).
  • How is the depletion region formed? The four steps in §4, with the figure.
  • Forward vs reverse bias. Barrier \(V_0 \mp V\), width narrower/wider, which current dominates (diffusion vs drift \(I_S\)).
  • Zener vs avalanche breakdown. Doping, voltage range, mechanism (tunnelling vs impact ionisation), temperature coefficient (negative vs positive).
  • Why is reverse current nearly independent of reverse voltage? It is carried by thermally generated minority carriers, whose number is fixed by temperature, not by the voltage.

Traps (where marks go)

  • Saying the depletion field points p → n. It points from the positive ions (n side) to the negative ions (p side).
  • Reverse current "exactly zero": it is \(-I_S\) plus leakage (CT true/false item).
  • Using \(\log_{10}\) in \(V_0 = V_T\ln(\cdot)\).
  • Reading a waveform graph literally when it is mis-drawn: in 2012–13 the printed \(V_R\) peaks (2.2 V, −1.2 V) contradict the 1 V/s slopes; a series diode cannot make \(v_R\) rise faster than \(v_{in}\). State the physical reading.
  • Diode in parallel with R across an ideal source: the source fixes the voltage, so \(v_{out} = v_{in}\) whatever the diode does.

Exam questions

2012–13 · Sec A · Q4(a)(b)(c) · 20 + 16 + 10⅔ marks

An AC voltage source is applied to a series combination of a diode and a resistance. In the figure the dashed line is the input voltage \(V_{ac}\) and the solid line is the voltage across the resistance \(V_R\); \(dV_{ac}/dt\) = +1 V/s when rising and −1 V/s when falling. (a) Copy \(V_R\) exactly to scale and draw the voltage across the diode on the same graph. (b) Draw the I–V characteristic of the diode exactly to scale, marking the voltage(s) at which it begins to conduct. (c) Draw the voltage across the diode if it had been connected in parallel with the resistance.

Figure as printed (read with the 1 V/s slopes): \(V_{ac}\) is a triangle 0 → 3 V (3 s) → 0 (6 s) → −3 V (9 s) → 0 (12 s). \(V_R\) is zero until 1 s, rises to its peak at 3 s, is back to zero at 5 s, zero until 8 s, dips to its negative peak at 9 s and returns to zero at 10 s.

Show worked answer

(a) KVL round the loop: \(v_D = V_{ac} - V_R\). While \(V_R = 0\) the diode is open and \(v_D = V_{ac}\). \(V_R\) leaves zero at 1 s, when \(V_{ac}\) = 1 V: the diode starts conducting forward at 1 V. It rises with the same 1 V/s slope as \(V_{ac}\) (so the diode's forward resistance is negligible), reaching \(3 - 1 = 2\) V at 3 s and returning to zero at 5 s (\(V_{ac}\) = 1 V again). On the negative side \(V_R\) leaves zero at 8 s, when \(V_{ac}\) = −2 V: reverse breakdown at −2 V. It reaches \(-3 + 2 = -1\) V at 9 s and is zero again at 10 s.

Diode voltage: rises 0 → 1 V (0–1 s); held at +1 V (1–5 s); follows \(V_{ac}\) from 1 V down to −2 V (5–8 s); held at −2 V (8–10 s); follows \(V_{ac}\) from −2 V back to 0 (10–12 s).

(The printed figure shows peaks of about 2.2 V and −1.2 V. Read literally these would make \(v_D\) fall while the diode conducts, i.e. a negative resistance, which a passive series circuit cannot do. The intended values are 2 V and −1 V; say so in one line.)

(b) I–V: zero current for −2 V < \(v_D\) < +1 V; a vertical line at \(v_D\) = +1 V (forward conduction begins at 1 V, zero forward resistance); a vertical line downward at \(v_D\) = −2 V (breakdown begins at −2 V).

(c) In parallel with R, the diode is directly across the ideal source, so \(v_D = V_{ac}\): the same triangle, ±3 V. (A real source would be loaded heavily outside −2 V … +1 V, but with an ideal source the voltage is forced.)

Class-test items

CT-1, batch 23 (Jan 2025) · Q1 true/false · 5 marks

True or false: (a) a clipper cuts the signal at the diode forward voltage; (b) a real diode's reverse current is exactly zero; (c) half-wave and full-wave rectifiers give the same output frequency; (d) a full-wave rectifier smooths better; (e) an ideal diode has \(r_f = 0\) and \(r_r = \infty\).

Show worked answer

(a) False: the clip level is the diode drop plus any series reference voltage. (b) False: \(-I_S\) plus leakage flows. (c) False: the full-wave output repeats at 2f. (d) True: ripple \(V_p/(2fCR)\) instead of \(V_p/(fCR)\). (e) True.

CT-1, batch 23 (Jan 2025) · Q2 fill in the gaps · 5 marks (recalled)

(a) A silicon diode starts conducting at about ___ V. (b) A Zener diode holds its voltage in ___ bias, between ___ and ___. (c) A Schottky diode has a ___ forward drop. (d) A capacitor in a rectifier is used to ___ the output and reduce the ___.

Show worked answer

(a) about 0.5 V (fully on at 0.6–0.7 V). (b) reverse bias, in breakdown, between \(I_{ZK}\) and \(I_{Z,max}\). (c) lower, 0.3–0.5 V. (d) smooth (filter); ripple.

Practice

Key terms

Quick check

Summary: band gap separates conductors (none), semiconductors (about 1.1 eV) and insulators (large). Doping sets the majority concentration (\(N_D\) or \(N_A\)); minority \(= n_i^2/\)majority. At the junction, diffusion uncovers immobile ions, giving a depletion region, a field from n to p, and a barrier \(V_0 = V_T\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)\). Forward bias lowers the barrier and the current grows as \(I_Se^{v/nV_T}\); reverse bias raises it and only \(-I_S\) flows, until Zener or avalanche breakdown. A straight-line I–V graph is a model: \(V_{on}\), \(r_D\), \(V_{BR}\); then \(v_D = v_{in} - v_R\).
